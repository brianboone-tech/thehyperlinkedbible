## <span style="color: #1f618d; font-weight: bold;">Conclusion: Reading with the Grain</span>

Biblical authors like Moses, the psalmists, and the prophets built chiastic structures into their texts as <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">macro-level indicators of authorial intent</span>. These structures:

- Signal what matters most (by centering it)
- Create meaningful comparisons (by placing elements in parallel)
- Aid memory (by providing mental architecture)
- Generate synergy (by making parts work together)
- Display artistic beauty (by creating balance and harmony)

To ignore these structures is to read **against the grain** of how Scripture was written. To recognize them is to read **with the grain**, allowing the biblical authors to guide you to the truths they intended to communicate.

<span style="color: #8e44ad; font-weight: bold;">As Hamilton concludes:</span>

<span style="color: #1e90ff;">"The typological development through the Bible's literary structures draws our attention to the patterns and frames their beauty."</span>

These patterns don't merely point to abstract theological truths—they point to <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">Christ</span>, <span style="color: #1e90ff;">"the one to whom the Scriptures point, whose coming we await... the true and better Adam, bridegroom and beloved."</span>

<span style="color: #922b21; font-weight: bold;">Every chiasm in Scripture ultimately reveals the Author's intent: to display the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ.</span>
