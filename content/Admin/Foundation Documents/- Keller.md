## Overview

This document summarizes Timothy Keller's methodology for Christ-centered, gospel-driven preaching that engages both religious and secular audiences. Keller synthesizes insights from Edmund Clowney's redemptive-historical hermeneutics, Jonathan Edwards's theology of the heart, and missiological principles for reaching postmodern culture. The result is a comprehensive framework for preaching that:

1. Finds Christ in every text through redemptive-historical interpretation
2. Applies the gospel to heart idols rather than merely addressing behavior
3. Critiques both religion and irreligion with the "third way" of the gospel
4. Contextualizes the message for post-Christendom audiences

## Part I: Christ-Centered Exposition

### The Redemptive-Historical Approach

Every text must be understood in its place within the unfolding story of redemption. The Bible is not primarily a collection of moral examples or theological propositions but the story of God's salvation through Christ.

**Key Principle**: There are only two ways to read the Bible—is it basically about me or basically about Jesus? Is it about what I must do, or what he has done?

### Seven Ways Christ is Found in Scripture

Keller, drawing on Edmund Clowney, identifies multiple legitimate ways to move from any text to Christ:

| Method | Description | Example |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **1. Direct Prophecy** | Explicit messianic predictions | Isaiah 53, Psalm 22 |
| **2. Typology** | Persons, events, institutions that prefigure Christ | David as king, Passover lamb |
| **3. Analogy** | Parallel patterns between OT figures and Christ | Joseph's suffering leading to exaltation |
| **4. Themes** | Theological motifs that culminate in Christ | Covenant, Kingdom, Temple |
| **5. Law Completion** | Moral demands that drive us to Christ | The law as tutor (Galatians 3:24) |
| **6. Contrast** | OT failures that highlight Christ's perfection | Failed kings vs. true King |
| **7. Story Arc** | The text's place in the overall narrative | Creation-Fall-Redemption-Consummation |

### Example: David and Goliath

**Moralistic Reading**: "The bigger they come, the harder they fall, if you just go into your battles with faith in the Lord."

**Gospel Reading**: The Israelites cannot go up against Goliath—they need a substitute. David goes as a vulnerable figure, virtually as a sacrificial lamb. God uses his apparent weakness to destroy the giant. David becomes Israel's champion-redeemer, so that his victory is imputed to them. They receive all the fruit of having fought the battle themselves.

**Application**: Until I see that Jesus fought the real giants (sin, law, death) for me, I will never have the courage to fight ordinary giants in life.

## Part II: The Gospel vs. The Two Thieves

### The Third Way

Tertullian observed that "just as Christ was crucified between two thieves, so this doctrine of justification is ever crucified between two opposite errors." These two thieves are:

| **Moralism/Religion** | **Relativism/Irreligion** |
|----------------------|--------------------------|
| Stresses truth over grace | Stresses grace over truth |
| "Obey to be saved" | "Everyone is accepted" |
| Self-salvation through performance | Self-salvation through autonomy |
| Pharisee | Sadducee |
| Under-realized eschatology | Over-realized eschatology |

**Critical Insight**: Legalism and license are not opposites—they are the same thing. Both are strategies of self-salvation. Both make something other than Christ the functional savior.

### The Gospel Alternative

The gospel is not a halfway compromise between these poles but something entirely different from both:

- Gives us a God far more holy than a moralist can bear (your morality is filthy rags)
- Gives us a God far more loving than a relativist can imagine (his love cost him dearly)

**Practical Implication**: Non-Christians will automatically hear gospel presentations as appeals to become moral and religious—unless you explicitly use the good news of grace against legalism.

### Application to Various Life Issues

| Issue | Moralist Approach | Relativist Approach | Gospel Approach |
|-------|------------------|---------------------|-----------------|
| **Discouragement** | "You're breaking rules—repent harder" | "Just love yourself" | "Something has become more important than Christ—a pseudo-savior" |
| **Suffering** | God owes me (if good) or punishes me (if bad) | God is unjust or impotent | Christ's cross removes both self-pity and fear of punishment |
| **Relationships** | Co-dependency (earning salvation through being needed) | Negotiated partnership for mutual benefit | Sacrificial love without inappropriate need |
| **Guilt** | Flagellate yourself until worthy | Dismiss guilt as unhealthy | The only God who forgives; all other "gods" condemn |
| **Self-image** | Confident but not humble (if succeeding) | Humble but not confident (if failing) | Both bold and humble—perfect yet sinner |

## Part III: Heart-Level Application

### The Four-Step Application Framework

| Step | Description | Key Question |
|------|-------------|--------------|
| **1. What You Must Do** | The moral principle from the text | "Here is what the text tells us we must do or be" |
| **2. Why You Can't Do It** | The crisis—showing insurmountable problems | "But you can't do it! Here's why trying harder will never work" |
| **3. How He Did It** | Christ as the fulfillment | "But there's One who did—perfectly, wholly" |
| **4. How Through Him You Can** | Gospel-powered transformation | "Your failure is a functional rejection of what he did. Remembering him frees your heart" |

### The Doctrine of Idolatry

**Luther's Key Insight**: Self-justification (rather than Christ-justification) is the root of all sin. There is a particular heart-motive barrier under every sin—some form of unbelief in the gospel.

**Definition**: An idol is anything besides Christ that functions as an alternative "righteousness" or source of confidence—a pseudo-savior creating inordinate desires.

### Near and Far Idols

| **Far Idols** | **Near Idols** |
|---------------|----------------|
| More subtle and basic | More concrete and specific |
| Motivational drives at the roots | Objects/subjects on the surface |
| Power, Approval, Comfort, Control | Career, spouse, ministry, money |
| Dealt with through repenting and rejoicing | May remain if "put in their place" |

### The Four Root Idols

| Idol | What We Seek | Price Willing to Pay | Greatest Nightmare | Problem Emotion |
|------|--------------|---------------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Comfort** | Privacy, freedom from stress | Reduced productivity | Stress, demands | Boredom |
| **Approval** | Affirmation, love, relationship | Less independence | Rejection | Cowardice |
| **Control** | Self-discipline, certainty | Loneliness, spontaneity | Uncertainty | Worry |
| **Power** | Success, winning, influence | Burden, responsibility | Humiliation | Anger |

### Diagnostic Questions for Identifying Idols

1. What is my greatest nightmare? What do I worry about most?
2. What, if I failed or lost it, would cause me to feel I did not want to live?
3. What do I rely on or comfort myself with when things go bad?
4. What does my mind go to when I am free?
5. What prayer, unanswered, would make me think about turning from God?
6. What makes me feel the most self-worth?

### Dismantling Idols: Repent and Rejoice

**Step 1 - Repent (Taking Down the Idols)**:
1. **Name** the idols specifically in prayer
2. **Unmask** the idols—recognize how weak they are, how dangerous they are, how grievous they are to Christ

**Step 2 - Rejoice (Replacing the Idols)**:
- Rejoice in the particular thing Jesus brings that replaces the particular idol
- The thing that most comforts us (free grace) should most convict us

**Critical Principle**: Repentance without rejoicing leads to despair. Rejoicing without repentance is shallow. Both must go together.

## Part IV: The Missional Context

### The End of Christendom

For nearly 1,000 years in the West, social institutions "Christianized" people—instilling Christian cultural stories, ethics, worldview categories, and imagination. The church's job was simply to challenge these Christianized people into vital faith. That era is over.

**Consequences**:
- Biblical terms are no longer part of common discourse
- Non-Christian values permeate public life
- Traditional evangelism assumes worldview categories that no longer exist

### What Makes a Church "Missional"?

A missional church does not confine evangelism to a program. Instead, every aspect adapts to engage non-Christian culture:

| Element | Christendom Model | Missional Model |
|---------|------------------|-----------------|
| **Language** | Insider jargon assumed | Vernacular explanation of terms |
| **Preaching** | Exhortation to the convinced | Engagement with skeptics present |
| **Discipleship** | Private world skills (prayer, Bible study) | Public life, vocation, worldview |
| **Community** | Supportive relationships | Counter-cultural demonstration |
| **Evangelism** | Separate program | Permeates everything |

### Five Marks of Missional Discourse

1. **Vernacular**: Explain biblical terms; avoid tribal jargon
2. **Inclusive Address**: Always speak as if non-believers are present
3. **Respectful**: No "we-them" language or dismissive comments
4. **Non-Manipulative**: Avoid sentimental, pompous "inspirational" talk
5. **Gospel-Ironic**: Gentle, self-deprecating but joyful tone

## Part V: Contextualization

### The Preacher's Quadrant

|  | **Sender Oriented** | **Receptor Oriented** |
|---|---|---|
| **Message Centered** | **A. TRADITIONALIST**: Concerned for truth but only with own needs in view | **C. PREACHER**: Concerned with truth but enters receptor's frame to change it |
| **Context Centered** | **B. MANIPULATOR**: Uses any message to achieve own ends | **D. ACCOMMODATOR**: Tells audience what they want to hear |

**Goal**: Position C—Message-centered AND receptor-oriented. Enter the framework intellectually to challenge it, not accommodate it.

### Entering a Worldview

1. **Speak the receptor's language** (Logos)
2. **Use appropriate non-verbal communication** (Pathos)
3. **Be personally credible** (Ethos)
4. **Move from the RIGHT known to the RIGHT unknown**—find truths they hold in common with Scripture and affirm them before challenging

### Challenging a Worldview

1. **Identify** real, basic existing commitments
2. **De-stabilize** by showing inadequacy or incompatibility with their own principles
3. **Re-stabilize** with Christ—provide a new view of God that is true to their "right known" things

### Example: The Reality of God

**Entry Point**: Belief that oppression of the weak by the strong is wrong

**Challenge**: If nature is all there is, why would it be "wrong" for strong humans to trample weak ones? How could we know nature is abnormal unless there's a supernatural standard? The Bible makes sense of things:
- If there's no God, there'd be no way to know nature is abnormal
- If there was no Fall, there'd be no way to explain why God made a world like this
- Creation + Fall = the world is created yet fallen

### Example: The Uniqueness of Christ

**Entry Point**: Desire for inclusivity

**Challenge**: This is the only way to believe in grace without universalism. If you don't have to believe in Jesus, good works is enough. If good works is enough, God accepts people by performance. Somewhere there's a cutoff for moral goodness—that's exclusive. The Jesus-gospel is the only religion that claims justification by grace/faith alone. Not "the good are in and the bad are out" but "the humble are in and the proud are out."

## Part VI: Practical Application Strategies

### Three Perspectives on Application

Drawing on John Frame, every text can be applied through three lenses:

| Perspective | Focus | Danger if Exclusive |
|-------------|-------|---------------------|
| **Normative/Doctrinalist** | Sound doctrine, truth claims | Knowledge without transformation |
| **Existential/Pietist** | Personal relationship, heart change | Subjectivism, therapeutic focus |
| **Situational/Cultural-Transformationist** | Kingdom, justice, community | Social activism without gospel |

**Principle**: We need all three perspectives. Going deep into any one leads to the other two.

### Three Perspectives on the Gospel Itself

| Perspective | Emphasis | Entry Point |
|-------------|----------|-------------|
| **Gospel of Christ** (Normative) | Objective, historic work of Christ | Traditional cultures (guilt → forgiveness) |
| **Gospel of Sonship** (Existential) | New identity, liberation from law | Legalistic backgrounds (sadness → joy) |
| **Gospel of the Kingdom** (Situational) | Reversal of values, new creation | Post-moderns (oppression → freedom) |

### Weaving Application Throughout

1. **Running Application**: State every principle in its practical bearings immediately
2. **Collected Application**: Recap and drive home with deeper specificity at the end
3. **Direct Questions**: Pose inquiries calling for heart response
4. **Anticipate Objections**: Identify questions and express them before answering
5. **Self-Examination Tests**: Provide concrete tests to avoid rationalization
6. **Pliable Moments**: Recognize when attention is riveted and drive home truth
7. **Balance**: Warning, encouraging, comforting, urging—don't specialize in one tone

## Application for This Vault

### For Trajectory Tables

Keller's framework reinforces the importance of showing:
1. The insufficiency of OT types (they need fulfillment)
2. Christ as the true fulfillment (not just moral example)
3. Gospel transformation (how knowing Christ changes everything)

### For Intertextuality Pairs

When documenting NT use of OT:
1. Show how NT authors found Christ in the text
2. Distinguish gospel reading from moralistic reading
3. Note how the NT challenges both religious and irreligious assumptions

### For Foundation Texts

Include in theological development:
1. How the passage exposes human inability (driving to Christ)
2. How Christ fulfills what the type could only shadow
3. Heart-level application addressing idolatry, not just behavior
