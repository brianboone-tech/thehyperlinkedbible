# <span style="color: #1f618d;">How to Study the Bible's Use of the Bible</span>
### *A Comprehensive Summary of Schnittjer & Harmon*

---

> *"The authors of the New Testament did not invent a new hermeneutic. They followed the well-worn interpretive path used by prophets, psalmists, narrators, visionaries, and sages of Israel's scriptures."*

---

## <span style="color: #16a085;">Overview: The Central Thesis</span>

Schnittjer and Harmon argue that **the Bible interprets itself**—and does so contextually, not arbitrarily. The New Testament authors' hermeneutical methods are not innovations borrowed from Second Temple Judaism, but rather continuations of interpretive patterns already established within the Old Testament itself. Understanding how later biblical authors interpreted earlier Scripture is essential for grasping the unity of the Bible and the progressive unfolding of God's redemptive plan.

The book presents **seven hermeneutical choices** that interpreters must navigate when studying Scripture's use of Scripture.

---

## <span style="color: #c0392b;">The Seven Hermeneutical Choices</span>

---

### <span style="color: #16a085;">Choice 1: Sequestered versus Connected</span>

**The Question**: Should the Old and New Testaments be interpreted in isolation from each other, or should they be read as a unified canonical whole?

**The Authors' Position**: Scripture should be read as **connected**—the two testaments form an organic, unified whole.

#### Key Points:

1. **Dominant Scholarship's Error**: Many scholars treat the Old Testament as having meanings unrelated to New Testament fulfillment. This "sequestered" approach disconnects the testaments and often leads to claims that NT authors used the OT "noncontextually."

2. **The Hermeneutical Bridge**: The life, death, resurrection, and teachings of Jesus Christ provide the interpretive bridge between the testaments. Jesus himself taught that all Scripture points to him (Luke 24:27, 44-47).

3. **Organic Unity**: The Bible exhibits "canonical consciousness"—later authors demonstrate awareness of earlier Scripture as authoritative and interpret it accordingly.

4. **Two Principles**:
   - **Jesus as Hermeneutical Key**: Christ's own interpretive methods serve as the model
   - **OT Precedents**: The NT authors follow patterns already established within the OT itself

> **Practical Implication**: When studying how a NT author uses an OT text, first examine how that OT text was already being developed within the OT canon before looking to extrabiblical sources.

---

### <span style="color: #16a085;">Choice 2: Adjusting Meaning/Context versus Advancement of Revelation</span>

**The Question**: Did NT authors distort or adjust the original meaning of OT texts, or did they legitimately advance revelation within the text's original meaning?

**The Authors' Position**: NT authors practiced **contextual exegesis** that advances revelation—they did not arbitrarily adjust or distort meaning.

#### Key Points:

1. **The "Noncontextual" Charge**: Many scholars claim NT authors used midrashic, pesher, or other "creative" Jewish methods that ignored context. This is based on a misunderstanding.

2. **Progressive Revelation**: Scripture reveals God's redemptive plan incrementally. Later revelation builds on, clarifies, and fulfills earlier revelation without contradicting it.

3. **OT Precedent**: The OT itself demonstrates exegetical advancement:
   - Deuteronomy interprets and advances Exodus laws
   - Chronicles interprets and advances Samuel-Kings
   - Prophets interpret and advance Torah

4. **Organic Revelation vs. Mechanical Dictation**: Biblical inspiration is organic—God works through the human authors' understanding and methods, not bypassing them.

5. **Plain Sense Exegesis**: Both OT and NT authors practiced interpretation according to the context and established patterns within Scripture itself.

#### The Interpretive Framework:

| Problematic View | Biblical View |
|-----------------|---------------|
| NT authors adjust OT meaning | NT authors advance OT revelation |
| NT borrows Jewish midrash methods | NT follows OT interpretive precedents |
| Meaning changes between testaments | Meaning deepens and is fulfilled |
| Spirit adds new meaning | Spirit illuminates intended meaning |

> **Practical Implication**: Before concluding that a NT use of the OT is "noncontextual," examine whether the OT text itself contains elements pointing forward, and whether other OT texts already developed that passage.

---

### <span style="color: #16a085;">Choice 3: Detecting Allusions as Art versus Science</span>

**The Question**: Is identifying biblical allusions a precise science with clear criteria, or an interpretive art requiring judgment?

**The Authors' Position**: Detecting allusions is **both art and science**—it requires methodological rigor but also interpretive discernment.

#### Key Points:

1. **Terminology Spectrum**:
   - **Quotation**: Explicit verbal repetition, often marked ("as it is written")
   - **Paraphrase**: Intentional rewording of a donor text
   - **Allusion**: Intentional evocation without explicit citation
   - **Echo**: Subtle resonance (intentionality uncertain)
   - **Trace**: Unexpressed possible connection (speculative)

2. **Criteria for Identifying Allusions**:
   - **Verbal parallels**: Shared vocabulary, especially rare words
   - **Thematic parallels**: Shared concepts or ideas
   - **Syntactical parallels**: Shared grammatical structures
   - **Structural parallels**: Shared narrative patterns (extended echo effect)
   - **Inversion (Seidel's Theory)**: Deliberate reversal of word order

3. **Cautions**:
   - Avoid "parallelomania"—finding connections everywhere
   - Stock phrases and lyrical diffusion can create false positives
   - Consider whether shared vocabulary indicates dependence or common idiom

4. **Textual Considerations**:
   - NT authors may use Hebrew, Greek (LXX), or Aramaic texts
   - Apparent differences may reflect different textual traditions (Vorlage)
   - The LXX itself sometimes interprets the Hebrew

#### Practical Method:

1. Identify potential verbal/thematic parallels
2. Assess direction of dependence (which text is earlier?)
3. Consider the context of both donor and receptor texts
4. Evaluate whether the connection is intentional or coincidental
5. Determine the exegetical function of the allusion

> **Practical Implication**: Use methodological rigor but hold conclusions with appropriate humility. The clearest allusions have multiple indicators; uncertain cases should be treated cautiously.

---

### <span style="color: #16a085;">Choice 4: Horizontal Context versus Vertical Context</span>

**The Question**: Should interpretation focus only on the immediate literary context (horizontal), or also on the network of intertextual connections (vertical)?

**The Authors' Position**: Both **horizontal and vertical contexts** are essential for proper interpretation.

#### Key Points:

1. **Horizontal Context**: The surrounding verses, paragraphs, chapters, and book in which a text appears.

2. **Vertical Context**: The temporal relationship established by allusion—the connection between a receptor text and its donor text(s), creating interpretive "tethers" across Scripture.

3. **Deep Context**: When a receptor text alludes to a donor text that itself contains allusions to still earlier texts, creating layers of intertextual meaning.

4. **Interpretive Networks**: Some passages serve as "anchor texts" around which extensive networks of allusions form (e.g., Exodus 34:6-7, the "attribute formula").

#### The Attribute Formula Network (Exod 34:6-7):

This "anchor text" creates one of the most extensive networks in Scripture:
- Quoted/alluded to over 30 times in the OT
- Shapes understanding of God's character throughout both testaments
- Each use adds to the cumulative theological understanding

#### Practical Applications:

1. When studying a text, trace its allusions backward (to earlier texts) and forward (to later texts)
2. Consider how the cumulative network of allusions shapes meaning
3. Recognize that NT authors often expect readers to know the broader context of cited OT passages
4. Don't flatten meaning to only horizontal context—vertical connections add theological depth

> **Practical Implication**: When a NT author cites an OT verse, study not just that verse but its entire context, and trace how that passage was developed through the OT before reaching the NT.

---

### <span style="color: #16a085;">Choice 5: Biblical versus Extrabiblical Relationships</span>

**The Question**: Should biblical parallels or extrabiblical parallels (Second Temple Jewish literature, ANE texts) be prioritized when interpreting Scripture's use of Scripture?

**The Authors' Position**: **Biblical parallels must be prioritized**, though extrabiblical literature provides valuable comparative context.

#### Key Points:

1. **The Priority of Scripture**: Start with how other biblical texts interpret the passage in question. Scripture's use of Scripture is the primary foundation.

2. **Value of Extrabiblical Literature**:
   - Ancient Near Eastern texts illuminate OT context
   - Second Temple Jewish texts show the interpretive environment
   - Provides comparison and contrast with biblical interpretation

3. **Cautions with Extrabiblical Sources**:
   - Dating difficulties (especially rabbinic literature)
   - Availability questions (did the biblical author know this text?)
   - Context matters (understand the extrabiblical text in its own setting)
   - Avoid "parallelomania"

4. **Methodology**:
   ```
   START → Biblical parallels (OT→OT, NT→OT)
         → Extrabiblical parallels (for comparison)
         → RETURN to biblical interpretation
   ```

#### Key Resources:

| Category | Resources |
|----------|-----------|
| ANE Texts | ANET, COS, ANEP |
| LXX | Göttingen, Rahlfs, NETS |
| Targums | ArBib, TBA |
| Dead Sea Scrolls | DSSSE, DSSB, BQS |
| Apocrypha/Pseudepigrapha | OTP, NETS |
| Philo/Josephus | LCL editions |
| Rabbinic | Mishnah (Danby), Str-B (use cautiously) |

> **Practical Implication**: When studying Paul's use of Genesis 15:6, first trace how the OT itself develops this text (Psalm 106:30-31, Nehemiah 9:8) before turning to Jewish interpretive traditions.

---

### <span style="color: #16a085;">Choice 6: Backward-Looking versus Forward-Looking Typological Patterns</span>

**The Question**: Are types in Scripture only recognized retrospectively (backward-looking), or do some types contain inherent forward-pointing expectation?

**The Authors' Position**: Scripture contains **both forward-looking and backward-looking types**—both are legitimate.

#### Key Points:

1. **Definition of Typology**: Intentional scriptural shaping to draw selective expectational analogies between persons, events, institutions, or procedures within the historical framework of progressive revelation.

2. **Key Elements of Typology**:
   - **Intentional shaping**: Author-intended, not reader-imposed
   - **Points of analogy**: Clear correspondence between type and antitype
   - **Rooted in history**: Real persons, events, institutions
   - **Escalation**: Antitype surpasses type in significance
   - **Selectivity**: Only certain details are typologically significant

3. **Forward-Looking Types (Expectational)**:
   - Contain indicators pointing to future fulfillment
   - Often marked by language like "like" (כְּ) or "according to"
   - Example: Deuteronomy 18:15-19 (prophet like Moses)
   - Example: Psalm 110:4 (priest after the order of Melchizedek)

4. **Backward-Looking Types (Prefigurative)**:
   - Not inherently expectational in original context
   - Recognized as typological in light of later fulfillment
   - Example: Psalm 22 applied to Christ's crucifixion
   - Example: Jonah's three days as a pattern of Christ's death/resurrection

5. **Temporal vs. Spatial Typology** (Geerhardus Vos):
   - **Temporal**: Before/after (shadow → reality)
   - **Spatial**: Lower/higher (earthly tabernacle → heavenly reality)

#### Distinguishing Typology from Other Analogies:

| Feature | Typology | Extended Echo Effect | Allegory |
|---------|----------|---------------------|----------|
| Selectivity | Selective | Selective | Non-selective |
| Context | Respects context | Respects context | Ignores context |
| Historical basis | Real events | Real events | Philosophical grid |
| Function | Expectational | Literary | Ideological |

#### Controls for Identifying Types:

1. Consider the historical context of the donor text
2. Look for larger redemptive patterns in Scripture
3. Prioritize major patterns over minor details
4. Let typological connections serve the plain meaning, not replace it

> **Practical Implication**: Don't limit typology to only those examples explicitly identified in Scripture, but exercise caution and humility with proposed types. Look for divine design in redemptive history, not arbitrary connections.

### <span style="color: #16a085;">Choice 7: Historical Exegesis versus Historical and Prosopological Exegesis</span>

**The Question**: Should interpretation be confined to the historical context alone, or does Scripture also employ prosopological exegesis (reading speeches in light of new characters)?

**The Authors' Position**: Scripture employs **both historical and prosopological exegesis**—they are not mutually exclusive.

#### Key Points:

1. **Definition**: Prosopological exegesis refers to a biblical author reading an earlier biblical speech in light of a new character—assigning a new speaker, referent, or auditor to the speech.

2. **Components**:
   - **Speaker**: Who is speaking?
   - **Referent**: Who/what is being spoken about?
   - **Auditor**: Who is being addressed?

3. **Example** (Psalm 45:6-7 → Hebrews 1:8-9):
   - **Original**: Royal bride speaks to human king
   - **Hebrews**: God speaks to the Son
   - Prosopological shift: speaker (bride → God) and referent (human king → Christ)

4. **OT Precedents**:
   - Psalm superscriptions assign speakers/contexts
   - 1 Chronicles 16:7-36 reframes Psalm medley in David's narrative
   - Psalm 2:7 revoices 2 Samuel 7:14 as direct divine speech
   - 2 Chronicles 6:41-42 blends Psalm 132 and Isaiah 55:3

5. **NT Development**:
   - Jesus uses prosopological exegesis (Mark 12:35-37; 14:62)
   - Voice from heaven at baptism/transfiguration employs it
   - Hebrews extensively uses prosopological readings
   - Paul applies it in Romans 15, 2 Corinthians 4, Acts 13

6. **Not Ahistorical**: Prosopological exegesis does not replace historical meaning—Hebrews 3:7 and 4:7 show the author affirming both David's authorship AND the Holy Spirit's speech.

#### Major Examples in Hebrews:

| OT Text | Original Speaker | Hebrews Attribution |
|---------|-----------------|---------------------|
| Psalm 2:7 | Yahweh to Davidic king | God to the Son |
| Psalm 45:6-7 | Royal bride to king | God about the Son |
| Psalm 102:25-27 | Psalmist to Yahweh | God to the Son |
| Psalm 22:22 | Psalmist to Yahweh | Jesus to God |
| Psalm 95:7-11 | Psalmist/Yahweh | Holy Spirit |

> **Practical Implication**: Recognize that Scripture can legitimately apply speeches to new persons within the framework of typological fulfillment, while still honoring the original historical context.

## <span style="color: #8e44ad;">Key Terminology Reference</span>

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Donor Text** | The earlier biblical text being cited/alluded to |
| **Receptor Text** | The later text making the citation/allusion |
| **Allusion** | Intentional evocation of a donor text |
| **Echo** | Subtle resonance where intentionality is uncertain |
| **Extended Echo Effect** | Recurring similar elements (a-b-c, a-b-c) for literary effect |
| **Vertical Context** | Intertextual connections across Scripture |
| **Horizontal Context** | Immediate literary surroundings |
| **Interpretive Blend** | Combining multiple donor texts in interpretation |
| **Plain Sense Exegesis** | Interpretation according to context and Scripture's own patterns |
| **Progressive Revelation** | Incremental disclosure of God's redemptive will |
| **Canonical Consciousness** | Awareness of earlier Scripture as authoritative |
| **Seidel's Theory** | Inversion of word order to signal allusion |
| **Type/Antitype** | Pattern and fulfillment in redemptive history |
| **Ectype** | Intermediate installment between type and antitype |
| **Prosopological Exegesis** | Reading speeches with new speaker/referent/auditor |

## <span style="color: #16a085;">Practical Methodology Summary</span>

### When Studying Scripture's Use of Scripture:

1. **Identify the Allusion**
   - Note verbal, thematic, structural parallels
   - Assess strength of connection
   - Determine direction of dependence

2. **Study the Donor Text**
   - Read in full horizontal context
   - Trace vertical context (earlier allusions)
   - Note how the text develops within the OT

3. **Study the Receptor Text**
   - Examine horizontal context
   - Note how the allusion functions in the argument
   - Identify any interpretive blend with other texts

4. **Analyze the Exegetical Outcome**
   - What does the receptor text do with the donor text?
   - Is there advancement of revelation?
   - Is typology or prosopological exegesis involved?

5. **Consult Extrabiblical Sources (Secondary)**
   - Compare with Jewish interpretive traditions
   - Note similarities and differences
   - Return to biblical interpretation as primary

6. **Synthesize**
   - How does this use fit within the canonical whole?
   - What does it reveal about progressive revelation?
   - How does it point to Christ?

---

## <span style="color: #16a085;">Conclusion</span>

The Bible is a unified book with a coherent hermeneutic. The NT authors did not abandon contextual interpretation when they read the OT—they followed the interpretive methods already established within the OT itself. Understanding these methods is essential for:

- Recognizing the unity of Scripture
- Tracing the development of revelation
- Seeing Christ as the center of all Scripture
- Interpreting difficult NT uses of the OT
- Avoiding both flat literalism and uncontrolled allegory

The goal is not to impose external frameworks on Scripture, but to read Scripture according to its own self-interpreting patterns—the Bible's use of the Bible.
