#### Context

Psalm 141:2 expresses David's longing that his prayer be as acceptable to God as the fragrant incense burned at the golden altar: "Let my prayer be counted as incense before you, and the lifting up of my hands as the evening sacrifice!" David appeals to temple worship imagery—specifically the twice-daily incense offering (Exodus 30:7-8) and evening sacrifice—to express his desire for acceptable prayer. The verb "be counted" (תִּכּוֹן, from כּוּן) means "be established, reckoned as acceptable," showing David wants his prayer to have the same favor before God as prescribed offerings. The phrase "before you" (לְפָנֶיךָ) echoes Exodus 30:8's "perpetual incense before the LORD," indicating David seeks direct access to God's presence through prayer. The "lifting up of hands" represents the prayer posture, and comparing it to "the evening sacrifice" (מִנְחַת־עָרֶב) connects personal petition with corporate worship. This psalm demonstrates OT saints understood incense represented prayer, though the explicit connection awaited NT revelation (Revelation 5:8; 8:3-4). David's longing finds fulfillment in Christ, through whom believers' prayers ascend as acceptable incense, made fragrant by His mediating work.

#### Ninefold Analysis

**OT Context**

Psalm 141 is a prayer for deliverance from evildoers and protection from temptation. David composed it during personal crisis, possibly during Absalom's rebellion or Saul's persecution. The opening verses (vv. 1-2) establish urgency: "O LORD, I call upon you; hasten to me! Give ear to my voice when I call to you! Let my prayer be counted as incense..." The psalm's setting suggests David prayed at the hour of evening sacrifice (around 3 PM), when priests would burn incense at the golden altar while offering the evening burnt offering (Exodus 29:38-42; 30:7-8). David draws explicit parallel between his personal prayer and temple worship—he wants his petition to have the same acceptance as prescribed offerings. The "lifting up of hands" (מַשְׂאַת כַּפַּי) was standard prayer posture (1 Kings 8:22, 54; Lamentations 3:41). Comparing prayer to incense and sacrifice shows David understood worship symbolism pointed beyond ritual to heart reality. The golden altar stood "before the veil...before the mercy seat" (Exodus 30:6), representing mediated access—David longs for that same access through prayer, anticipating the direct access Christ would provide.

**Jewish Backgrounds**

Second Temple Judaism preserved this psalm in worship, especially evening prayers. The Talmud (Berakhot 26b) established three daily prayer times—morning (shacharit), afternoon (mincha), and evening (maariv)—corresponding to temple sacrifice times. Psalm 141:2's evening imagery influenced this pattern. Philo (On the Migration of Abraham 91) allegorized hand-lifting as soul ascending to God. The Mishnah (Tamid 5:1) described the evening sacrifice service when "they began to ascend the steps of the sanctuary" to burn incense, creating the backdrop for Psalm 141:2. Rabbinic literature (Midrash Tehillim 141:2) connected this psalm to Moses' intercession (Exodus 32:11-14), seeing hand-lifting as powerful petition. The Targum emphasized prayer's value: "Let my prayer be as sweet as incense." Jewish prayer liturgy preserved this imagery—the Amidah's opening includes praise for God who "remembers the pious deeds of the patriarchs," linking prayer to ancient righteousness. First-century Jews like Zechariah's contemporaries (Luke 1:10) prayed outside while priests burned incense, embodying Psalm 141:2's longing—believers praying while incense ascended, desiring their prayers to be equally acceptable.

**Text Form**

Psalm 141:2 employs jussive mood expressing desire: "Let my prayer be counted" (תִּכּוֹן תְּפִלָּתִי). The verb תִּכּוֹן (tikkon, from כּוּן) in Niphal stem means "be established, be firmly set, be acceptable." The noun תְּפִלָּה (prayer) is emphatic by position—"My prayer—let it be counted as incense!" The prepositional phrase לְפָנֶיךָ ("before you") echoes temple language—incense burned "before the LORD" (לִפְנֵי יְהוָה, Exodus 30:8). The comparison structure uses כְּ (as/like) twice: כִּקְטֹרֶת ("as incense") and כְּמִנְחַת־עָרֶב ("as evening sacrifice"). The parallelism creates equation: prayer = incense // hand-lifting = evening sacrifice. Both lines emphasize acceptable worship ascending to God. The construct phrase מִנְחַת־עָרֶב ("offering of evening") specifically refers to the twilight sacrifice (Numbers 28:4), when incense was also burned (Exodus 30:8). The syntax creates ascending movement: prayer rises like incense, hands lift like offering—both symbolizing petition reaching God's presence. The possessive pronouns ("my prayer," "my hands") emphasize personal, intimate petition, not merely corporate ritual.

**Hermeneutical Use**

The New Testament interprets Psalm 141:2 as anticipating prayer's nature revealed in Christ. Revelation 5:8 and 8:3-4 provide explicit identification: incense is the prayers of the saints. What David desired—prayer counted as incense—becomes reality for believers whose prayers ascend through Christ's mediation. First Timothy 2:8's instruction to "lift holy hands" echoes the psalm's imagery, showing NT continuity with OT prayer posture. Hebrews 13:15 reinterprets sacrifice as "fruit of lips that acknowledge his name," spiritualizing what the psalm anticipated. First Peter 2:5's "spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ" fulfills David's longing—prayers are indeed acceptable, but only through Christ's mediating work. The pattern is desire (Psalm 141:2, OT saints wanting prayers to be like incense) → incarnation (Christ becoming the mediator) → fulfillment (Revelation 5:8; 8:3-4, prayers are incense through Christ). David's longing for acceptable prayer finds answer in Christ who makes prayers fragrant by mingling His intercession with believers' petitions.

**Theological Use**

Psalm 141:2 grounds theology of prayer and worship. In prayer theology, the psalm establishes that prayer should be: (1) Regular—like twice-daily incense; (2) Acceptable—meeting God's standards; (3) Fragrant—pleasing to God; (4) Ascending—reaching God's presence. In the theology of worship, comparing prayer to prescribed offerings shows personal devotion must align with revealed worship patterns. In mediation theology, David's need for prayers to be "counted" as acceptable points to Christ—our prayers require mediator to make them acceptable. In the doctrine of grace, the phrase "be counted" (תִּכּוֹן) anticipates imputation theology—just as righteousness is "counted" to believers (Genesis 15:6; Romans 4:3), prayers are "counted" acceptable through Christ. In ecclesiology, the psalm foreshadows believers as "royal priesthood" (1 Peter 2:9) offering spiritual sacrifices. In Old Testament theology, the psalm demonstrates saints' understanding that ritual pointed to reality—incense symbolized prayer, sacrifice represented heart devotion. Fulfillment comes in Christ who makes the symbol reality.

**Rhetorical Use**

The psalm functions rhetorically as prayer for deliverance from enemies (vv. 3-10), with verse 2 establishing acceptable approach. The incense-sacrifice imagery creates rhetorical effect of urgency and propriety—David seeks immediate help ("hasten to me!" v. 1) but wants his prayer to be proper and acceptable. The twice-daily incense service provides rhetorical model: as regularly as priests burn incense, David seeks God's ear. The evening sacrifice timing creates specific rhetorical moment—David prays at the appointed hour when God promised to meet His people (Exodus 29:42-43). The hand-lifting image creates visual rhetoric: supplication reaching toward heaven like smoke ascending. For ancient readers familiar with temple service, this rhetoric was powerful—David seeks the same access priests enjoyed at the golden altar. The rhetorical strategy is: God prescribed acceptable worship at the altar; I want my prayer to have that same acceptance; therefore, hear me. For NT believers, this rhetoric encourages: pray persistently like regular incense, confidently like prescribed offering, expectantly like David who knew God heard acceptable prayer.

**Canonical Context**

Within the Psalter, 141:2 provides key prayer-theology alongside Psalms like 51:17 (God desires broken spirit over sacrifice) and 66:18-19 (God hears when no iniquity harbored). Within the OT, the psalm connects temple worship (Exodus 30; 29:38-42) with personal devotion, showing ritual's intended spiritual meaning. Within the whole canon, Psalm 141:2 traces from Exodus 30 (altar prescribed) → David's longing (prayer as incense desired) → Zechariah's service (Luke 1, people praying while incense burns) → explicit fulfillment (Revelation 5:8; 8:3-4, prayers are incense). The canonical progression shows: physical altar with symbolic incense → spiritual reality those symbols represented → Christ making that reality accessible. What began as David's poetic comparison becomes NT doctrine: prayers are incense, Christ is the priest, heaven is the sanctuary, God's throne is the destination. The trajectory moves from desire (Psalm 141) → shadow (Exodus 30) → substance (Christ's intercession) → consummation (eternal worship, Revelation 21-22).

#### Christological Connection

Psalm 141:2 expresses David's longing for prayer to be as acceptable to God as the fragrant incense burned at the golden altar, a longing completely fulfilled in Jesus Christ through whose mediation believers' prayers ascend as acceptable incense before the Father's throne. Where David prayed, "Let my prayer be counted as incense before you," Christ has made this reality for all who approach God through Him. Revelation 5:8 and 8:3-4 reveal the fulfillment: the golden bowls full of incense "are the prayers of the saints," and the angel at the golden altar offers "much incense...with the prayers of all the saints," showing Christ presenting believers' prayers with His own perfect intercession, making them fragrant and acceptable. What David desired—prayer counted as the incense burned twice daily at the altar (Exodus 30:7-8)—Christ provides: He "always lives to make intercession" (Hebrews 7:25), perpetually presenting prayers before the Father, never ceasing like the "perpetual incense" (תָּמִיד, Exodus 30:8). Where David could only long for his prayers to have the same acceptance as prescribed offerings, believers' prayers are made acceptable through Christ's merit mingled with our petitions. First Peter 2:5 declares we "offer spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ"—the acceptability flows from Christ's mediation, not our worthiness. Where David lifted hands hoping they would be counted as the evening sacrifice (מִנְחַת־עָרֶב), Christ has become our evening sacrifice, our morning sacrifice, our perpetual offering—the Lamb slain whose blood speaks perpetually, whose intercession never ends. Romans 8:34 confirms: "Christ Jesus...who is at the right hand of God, who indeed is interceding for us." First John 2:1 adds: "if anyone does sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous." Where the golden altar stood "before the veil...before the mercy seat" (Exodus 30:6) representing mediated access with incense penetrating where priests could not go, Christ has entered "heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf" (Hebrews 9:24), serving at the true altar before the true throne. The incense David longed to have his prayers counted as has become reality—our prayers are incense, made fragrant not by our eloquence but by Christ's intercession. The hands David lifted hoping they would be counted as acceptable offering are now lifted "in every place...holy hands" (1 Timothy 2:8), made holy through Christ's sanctifying work. What was David's poetic longing—"Let my prayer be counted"—becomes apostolic declaration: prayers are counted, reckoned, established as acceptable through Christ. The counting (תִּכּוֹן) David desired anticipates the imputation believers experience: Christ's righteousness counted to us (Romans 4:3-5), our prayers counted acceptable through Him. The trajectory is longing for acceptable prayer (Psalm 141) → incense service at earthly altar (Exodus 30) → Christ's intercession at heavenly throne (Hebrews 7:25) → prayers as incense through Christ (Revelation 5:8; 8:3-4) → eternal worship without need for symbol (Revelation 21-22), demonstrating that David's desire to have prayer counted as fragrant incense finds perfect fulfillment in Christ who takes our stammering petitions, mingles them with His perfect intercession, and presents them as acceptable incense before the Father, ensuring that what David could only long for—prayer reaching God's presence like the twice-daily incense ascending from the golden altar—every believer now experiences through union with Christ, the mediator whose intercession makes our prayers fragrant, acceptable, and effectual before God's throne forever.
