#### Context

Hebrews 2:5-9 presents the author's christological interpretation of Psalm 8, establishing Jesus as the true "son of man" who fulfills humanity's God-intended destiny through suffering, death, and resurrection glory. The passage begins by noting that God did not subject "the world to come" to angels but to humanity (v. 5), then quotes Psalm 8:4-6 as scriptural testimony (vv. 6-8a). The author acknowledges present reality: "we do not yet see everything in subjection to him" (v. 8b)—humanity's lost dominion remains unfulfilled. However, "we see Jesus" (v. 9), the crucial turning point. Jesus was "made lower than the angels for a little while" (incarnation and humiliation), experienced death ("taste death"), and now is "crowned with glory and honor" (resurrection and exaltation). The purpose clause "so that by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone" establishes substitutionary atonement as the means by which Jesus secures humanity's destiny. The passage answers the problem posed by Psalm 8: though humanity was crowned to rule creation, Adam's fall lost that dominion; Christ recapitulates Adam's story but succeeds through obedient suffering, recovering and perfecting the glory Adam forfeited. Believers will share this restored dominion through union with Christ.

#### Ninefold Analysis

**OT Context**

The author quotes Psalm 8:4-6 from the Septuagint, David's meditation on humanity's exalted status despite apparent insignificance. Psalm 8 reflects on Genesis 1:26-28's creation mandate—humanity made "a little lower than God/angels," crowned with glory and honor, given dominion over creation. The psalm was written after the fall, creating inherent tension: David celebrates humanity's intended glory while living in post-fall reality where dominion is compromised. The rhetorical question "What is man that you are mindful of him?" expresses wonder that God cares for finite, fallen humanity. The psalm functions both as remembrance (what humanity was created to be) and hope (what will be restored). The Hebrew אֱלֹהִים in Psalm 8:5 is ambiguous ("God" or "heavenly beings"), and the LXX chose ἀγγέλους ("angels"), which Hebrews quotes. Either reading emphasizes humanity's exalted position in creation's hierarchy. Hebrews recognizes Psalm 8 as both creation theology and messianic prophecy—the "son of man" who fulfills humanity's destiny is Christ. The already/not-yet tension is key: Psalm 8 describes what should be (dominion), Hebrews acknowledges what is (dominion lost), and points to what will be (dominion restored in Christ).

**Jewish Backgrounds**

Second Temple Judaism interpreted Psalm 8 with varied emphases. The Septuagint's translation "angels" (ἀγγέλους) for Hebrew אֱלֹהִים highlighted humanity's cosmic position between God and angels. Targum Jonathan expanded the psalm to emphasize Israel's special status and future glory. Rabbinic literature (Midrash Tehillim 8:2) debated humanity's rank relative to angels, with some arguing humanity's potential for righteousness exceeds angels when obedient to Torah. 4 Ezra 8:4-6 expressed wonder at God's care for small humanity amid vast creation, echoing Psalm 8's amazement. Philo (On the Creation 82-88) connected Psalm 8 to Genesis 1:26-28, emphasizing humanity as microcosm bearing divine image. Some Jewish interpreters applied "son of man" messianically, connecting it to Daniel 7:13-14's vision of one like a son of man receiving kingdom. The Dead Sea Scrolls do not directly quote Hebrews 2's passage, but messianic expectation permeates Qumran literature. Hebrews' innovation is the explicit application to Jesus, identifying Him as the suffering and glorified son of man who fulfills both Psalm 8 and Daniel 7.

**Text Form**

The passage employs sophisticated rhetorical structure. Verse 5 introduces the topic negatively: "not to angels did He subject the world to come" (implied contrast: but to humanity). Verses 6-8a present scriptural proof through extended quotation of Psalm 8:4-6, with the formula "someone somewhere testified" creating rhetorical distance appropriate for well-known psalms. The quotation itself uses synonymous parallelism ("mindful"/"care for," "man"/"son of man," "glory"/"honor") typical of Hebrew poetry. Verse 8b provides interpretive comment acknowledging present reality: "we do not yet see everything in subjection." The crucial δὲ ("but") in verse 9 creates dramatic contrast: we don't see dominion fulfilled generally "but we see Jesus." The participle phrases create causal sequence: "made lower" → "crowned with glory" → "so that...he might taste death." The purpose clause (ὅπως) explains the divine plan: lowering and suffering were means to accomplish substitutionary death. The final phrase "for everyone" (ὑπὲρ παντός) establishes universal scope of Christ's death.

**Hermeneutical Use**

Hebrews 2:5-9 demonstrates christological-typological interpretation of the Psalms. The author reads Psalm 8 at multiple levels: (1) Original meaning—David's meditation on humanity's creation glory and mandate; (2) Present reality—humanity's lost dominion due to fall; (3) Prophetic fulfillment—Christ as true "son of man" who realizes what Psalm 8 envisions. The hermeneutical move recognizes that Psalm 8's vision has never been fully realized in fallen humanity but finds complete fulfillment in Christ. The phrase "we do not yet see everything in subjection" acknowledges the already/not-yet eschatological tension: Christ has been crowned, securing victory, but full subjection awaits consummation. The author's use of "son of man" connects Psalm 8 with Jesus' self-designation throughout the Gospels and with Daniel 7:13-14's apocalyptic vision. The hermeneutical framework assumes typological correspondence: Adam as type (lost dominion), Christ as antitype (restored dominion); Adam's story recapitulated but with obedient outcome. The "made lower than angels for a little while" interprets Psalm 8 as prophesying incarnation—temporary humiliation preceding exaltation. This reading is neither allegory nor mere proof-texting but recognizing divinely-intended messianic sense within the text's canonical function.

**Theological Use**

Hebrews 2:5-9 grounds multiple doctrines. In Christology, the passage establishes both Jesus' genuine humanity ("son of man") and His exalted status ("crowned with glory"). The incarnation is interpreted as deliberate humiliation: "made lower than angels" to accomplish redemption. In soteriology, verse 9's purpose clause provides the rationale for incarnation and suffering: "so that by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone"—substitutionary atonement explicitly stated. The "taste death" idiom emphasizes Christ's full experience of death's reality, not merely symbolic gesture. In anthropology, the passage reaffirms humanity's intended destiny: crowned with glory, given dominion—but acknowledges present fallen condition where this remains unrealized except in Christ. In eschatology, the already/not-yet framework is explicit: "we do not yet see everything in subjection" (not yet) "but we see Jesus...crowned" (already). Christ's exaltation guarantees believers' future share in dominion. In federal theology, Christ succeeds where Adam failed, pioneering humanity's path to glory. The "world to come" (v. 5) will be subjected to glorified humanity, not angels, fulfilling God's original purpose for image-bearers to rule creation.

**Rhetorical Use**

The passage functions rhetorically within Hebrews' larger argument about Christ's supremacy. After establishing Christ's superiority to angels (chapter 1), the author addresses a potential objection: if Christ is superior, why was He incarnate and subject to death, seemingly lower than angels? Hebrews 2:5-9 answers: Christ's temporary lowering was purposeful, accomplishing what angels could never achieve—redemption through substitutionary death. The rhetorical strategy moves from Scripture (Psalm 8) to present reality (dominion not yet fully realized) to christological resolution (Jesus crowned through suffering). The contrast "we do not yet see...but we see Jesus" provides rhetorical focus—our hope rests not on empirical observation of universal subjection (which we don't see) but on Jesus' accomplished victory (which we do see by faith). The "crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering of death" establishes that exaltation comes through suffering—Jesus didn't bypass the cross to reach the throne but was enthroned because He bore the cross. This rhetorically encourages readers facing persecution: suffering precedes glory, humiliation precedes exaltation, cross precedes crown. The "for everyone" (ὑπὲρ παντός) emphasizes grace's universal offer, preparing for the subsequent call to "not neglect so great a salvation" (v. 3).

**Canonical Context**

Within Hebrews, 2:5-9 bridges Christ's superiority to angels (1:4-14) with His identification with humanity (2:10-18). The passage introduces the theme of Christ's suffering, which dominates chapters 2-10. Within the Epistle, this establishes the pattern: glory through suffering, perfection through testing, priesthood through shared humanity. Within the NT, Hebrews 2:5-9 complements Paul's Adam-Christ typology (Romans 5; 1 Corinthians 15) by adding Psalm 8's dominion language and emphasizing suffering's role. Philippians 2:6-11's Christ-hymn parallels the humiliation-exaltation pattern. Within the whole canon, Hebrews 2:5-9 connects Genesis 1:26-28 (creation dominion) → Psalm 8 (dominion remembered and anticipated) → Christ (dominion incarnate and secured) → new creation (dominion shared with glorified saints, Revelation 5:10; 22:5). The passage answers the canonical question: How will God's purpose for humanity (dominion over creation) be fulfilled when Adam failed? Answer: through the second Adam who succeeds via suffering obedience. The canonical progression is creation → fall → promise → incarnation → cross → resurrection → exaltation → consummation—with Hebrews 2:5-9 interpreting the central act (incarnation through exaltation) as fulfillment of Psalm 8's prophetic vision.

#### Christological Connection

Hebrews 2:5-9 presents Jesus Christ as the true "son of man" who fulfills Psalm 8's vision of humanity crowned with glory and given dominion. Where Adam was created "a little lower than God" yet fell and lost dominion, Jesus was "made a little lower than the angels" through incarnation, endured suffering and death, and was "crowned with glory and honor" in resurrection and ascension. The passage emphasizes that Jesus' lowering was temporary and purposeful: "for a little while" (βραχύ τι) He took on human flesh, experienced human frailty, and "tasted death." This was not unfortunate necessity but divine strategy: "so that by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone" (v. 9). The substitutionary nature is explicit—His death was vicarious, "for" (ὑπέρ) others, accomplishing what humanity could not accomplish for itself. His resurrection and exaltation vindicate this sacrifice: He is "crowned with glory and honor" precisely "because of the suffering of death" (διὰ τὸ πάθημα τοῦ θανάτου). Where Psalm 8 asks "What is man?" marveling at God's care, Hebrews answers: Jesus is the man, the true son of man, the representative human who succeeds where Adam failed. His success guarantees ours: "For it was fitting that he, for whom and by whom all things exist, in bringing many sons to glory, should make the founder of their salvation perfect through suffering" (v. 10). Christ pioneers the path to glory, making Him both pattern and provision for believers. The "we do not yet see everything in subjection to him" acknowledges that full dominion awaits consummation, but "we see Jesus...crowned" assures that victory is secured. Believers participate in Christ's fulfilled Psalm 8: already crowned positionally in Him (Ephesians 2:6), being conformed to His image progressively (2 Corinthians 3:18), and awaiting full conformity ultimately (1 John 3:2). What Adam lost in Eden—glory, honor, dominion—Christ recovers on Calvary and in resurrection, ensuring that God's original purpose for humanity reaches fulfillment in the last Adam and all who are united to Him by faith. The trajectory is incarnation → suffering → death → resurrection → exaltation → bringing many sons to glory, making Jesus not merely restorer of paradise but inaugurator of new creation where humanity finally exercises the dominion Psalm 8 envisions, reigning with Christ forever (Revelation 22:5).
