#### Context

Genesis 1:26-28 presents the climactic sixth day of creation where God creates humanity (אָדָם, *adam*) as the pinnacle of His creative work. The deliberative plural "Let us make" indicates the Trinity's counsel in creating mankind uniquely in God's own image and likeness—a dignity no other creature receives. This divine image entails both vertical relationship (fellowship with God) and horizontal responsibility (dominion over creation as God's vice-regents). The mandate to "be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth and subdue it" establishes humanity's calling to extend God's ordered rule throughout creation, exercising benevolent stewardship under God's authority. Adam stands as the federal head of humanity, representing all his posterity in this covenant of works.

#### Ninefold Analysis

**OT Context**

Genesis 1:26-28 stands at the climax of the creation week, presenting humanity as God's unique creation bearing His image. The chapter's structure builds through six days to this pinnacle moment. The deliberative "Let us make" distinguishes humanity's creation from all prior creative acts (where God simply commands "Let there be"). The dual terminology of "image" (צֶלֶם) and "likeness" (דְּמוּת) emphasizes that humanity uniquely represents God in creation—possessing rationality, moral responsibility, relationality, and creative capacity. The dominion mandate flows directly from the imago Dei: humanity rules creation as God's authorized representatives, mirroring divine sovereignty. The blessing of fruitfulness connects to God's commission that humanity fill the earth—not remaining in Eden but spreading God's ordered rule throughout creation. This passage establishes the covenant of works: Adam's obedience would secure blessing for his posterity; his disobedience would bring curse.

**Jewish Backgrounds**

Second Temple Judaism extensively developed the concept of Adam as the archetypal human and federal head. The Wisdom of Solomon (2:23) states "God created man for incorruption and made him in the image of his own eternity," emphasizing the immortality originally intended for humanity. 4 Ezra (3:4-7; 7:11) reflects on Adam's creation in glory and his catastrophic fall which brought death to all his descendants, developing the corporate understanding of Adam's representative role. Rabbinic literature (Genesis Rabbah 8:1) debated the meaning of "image of God," with some suggesting it referred to human form, others to rational capacity, and still others to humanity's royal status. The Targums consistently rendered "image" and "likeness" to avoid anthropomorphism while maintaining humanity's unique status. Philo (On the Creation 69-71) distinguished between the heavenly "image" (the Logos) and earthly humanity created according to that image, influencing later Christian theology.

**Text Form**

The passage employs several significant literary features. The jussive "Let us make" (נַעֲשֶׂה) represents deliberative language, suggesting divine counsel—which Christian theology understands as Trinitarian. The dual terms "image" and "likeness" form a hendiadys, with "likeness" qualifying "image" to prevent misunderstanding (humanity is like God but not identical to God). The verse structure moves from divine intention (v. 26) to divine action (v. 27) to divine blessing and commission (v. 28), creating a threefold pattern. The chiastic structure of verse 27 emphasizes "in the image of God" at the center: "So God created man / in his own image / in the image of God he created him / male and female he created them." The repetition of "created" (בָּרָא, *bara*) three times in verse 27 underscores the divine initiative and special significance of humanity's creation. The dominion verbs (רָדָה, כָּבַשׁ) are strong terms suggesting active royal rule, not passive stewardship.

**Hermeneutical Use**

The New Testament interprets Genesis 1:26-28 christologically and ecclesiologically. Paul identifies Christ as "the image of God" (2 Corinthians 4:4; Colossians 1:15), presenting Him as the perfect fulfillment of what Adam was meant to be. Hebrews 2:5-9 applies Psalm 8's meditation on Genesis 1:26-28 to Christ, arguing that while "we do not yet see everything in subjection to him" (humanity's lost dominion), "we see Jesus... crowned with glory and honor." This typological reading recognizes Jesus as the true image-bearer who succeeds where Adam failed. The NT also applies this passage ecclesiologically: believers are being "renewed in knowledge after the image of their creator" (Colossians 3:10) and "predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son" (Romans 8:29). The dominion mandate finds eschatological fulfillment in Revelation 5:10 where the redeemed "shall reign on the earth." Thus the NT reads Genesis 1:26-28 as both promise (what humanity was meant to be) and prophecy (what Christ accomplishes and shares with His people).

**Theological Use**

Genesis 1:26-28 provides foundational doctrines for systematic theology. In the doctrine of God, the plural "Let us make" has been understood by Christian theology as an early indication of Trinitarian plurality within divine unity. In anthropology, the passage establishes that humanity alone bears the imago Dei, grounding human dignity, rationality, and moral responsibility. The image theology informs ethics: murder is heinous because it destroys God's image (Genesis 9:6); racism denies that all humans equally bear God's image. In soteriology, the passage sets up the protoevangelium—understanding that redemption means restoring the marred image and recovering lost dominion through Christ the last Adam. Federal theology grounds covenant representation in Adam's headship established here: one man acts for many. Eschatologically, the passage anticipates new creation where the image is fully restored and dominion perfectly exercised under Christ's reign.

**Rhetorical Use**

The passage functions rhetorically to establish humanity's exalted status and solemn responsibility. The climactic placement at the end of the creation week emphasizes humanity as the crown of God's creative work. The deliberative "Let us make" creates anticipation and signals special divine attention. The dual terminology ("image" and "likeness") drives home humanity's unique status—we are not merely part of creation but God's special representatives. The dominion mandate functions as both privilege and obligation: humanity is exalted above creation yet accountable to exercise authority rightly as God's stewards. The blessing of fruitfulness creates expectation of growth and expansion—humanity is designed to flourish and fill creation with image-bearers. The rhetorical effect is to establish human dignity while simultaneously highlighting human accountability. This dual emphasis serves Paul's argument in Romans 5: Adam's exalted position as federal head explains why his fall has such catastrophic consequences—and why Christ's obedience as the last Adam has such glorious redemptive power.

**Canonical Context**

Within Genesis, this passage initiates themes that structure the entire book: the special status of the chosen seed, blessing and fruitfulness, the land promise (filling and subduing the earth), and covenant representation. Genesis 2 will expand on the creation of Adam, while Genesis 3 narrates the tragic fall. The genealogies trace the seed line through which God will fulfill His purposes. Within the Pentateuch, humanity's creation in God's image provides the rationale for the law: Israel is to be holy because God is holy, reflecting divine character. The prophets look forward to restored creation and renewed humanity. Within the whole canon, Genesis 1:26-28 establishes the bookends: humanity created to rule creation in God's image → humanity fallen and dominion lost → Christ the true image-bearer restoring dominion → new creation where the redeemed reign with Christ forever. The passage sets up the entire biblical narrative arc.

#### Christological Connection

Genesis 1:26-28 finds its ultimate fulfillment in Jesus Christ, the last Adam and perfect image of God. While the first Adam was created in God's image, Christ is "the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15) and "the exact imprint of his nature" (Hebrews 1:3)—not merely bearing God's image but being the very radiance of God's glory. Where Adam failed to exercise righteous dominion and fell into sin, Christ succeeded, living in perfect obedience and securing dominion over all creation through His resurrection. Paul's Adam-Christ typology in Romans 5 and 1 Corinthians 15 demonstrates that Christ recapitulates Adam's story: He enters the world as the representative head of a new humanity, faces temptation but overcomes (whereas Adam fell), and through His obedience brings justification and life (whereas Adam brought condemnation and death). The dominion mandate lost through Adam's fall is restored through Christ's victory: "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me" (Matthew 28:18), and Ephesians 1:20-23 declares God "put all things under his feet and gave him as head over all things to the church." Believers participate in this restored image and dominion through union with Christ: we are being "conformed to the image of his Son" (Romans 8:29), transformed "into the same image from one degree of glory to another" (2 Corinthians 3:18), and will reign with Him in the new creation (Revelation 5:10; 22:5). What Adam lost, Christ recovers and perfects, bringing many sons to glory (Hebrews 2:10) and ensuring that God's original purpose for image-bearing, dominion-exercising humanity reaches its glorious consummation in Christ and His redeemed people.
