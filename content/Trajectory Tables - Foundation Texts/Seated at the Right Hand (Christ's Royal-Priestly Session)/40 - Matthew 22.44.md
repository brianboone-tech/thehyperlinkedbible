### [[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 22#Matthew 22 . 44|Matthew 22:44]]

**Context**: Jesus quotes Psalm 110:1 to the Pharisees, asking how David's son can be David's Lord if David calls him "my Lord." This confrontation demonstrates Jesus's claim to divine identity—He is both David's descendant (human Messiah) and David's Lord (divine Son). The Pharisees cannot answer, revealing their incomplete understanding of the Messiah's nature. Jesus's use of Psalm 110:1 establishes His authority to sit at God's right hand, a claim He will make explicitly before the Sanhedrin (Matthew 26:64).

**Christological Connection**: [[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 22#Matthew 22 . 44|Matthew 22:44]]'s quotation of [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 110#Psalm 110 . 1|Psalm 110:1]] represents Jesus's public claim to divine identity and authority to sit at God's right hand. By asking how the Messiah can be both David's son and David's Lord, Jesus exposes the inadequacy of merely human messianic expectations. The answer—which the Pharisees cannot give—is that the Messiah is the divine Son incarnate, truly David's descendant through human lineage yet truly David's Lord through eternal deity.

The seated position "at my right hand" establishes the Messiah's royal authority and exaltation. This is no mere honor but sharing of divine throne, participation in divine sovereignty. The phrase "until I make your enemies your footstool" indicates the session's duration—the Messiah sits in authority while enemies are being subdued, awaiting final consummation when all opposition is crushed. This session at God's right hand becomes central to NT proclamation: Peter quotes it at Pentecost ([[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 2#Acts 2 . 34|Acts 2:34-35]]), Stephen sees it in vision ([[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 7#Acts 7 . 55|Acts 7:55-56]]), Paul references it repeatedly ([[Readable Bible/45 - Romans/Romans 8#Romans 8 . 34|Romans 8:34]]; [[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 1#Ephesians 1 . 20|Ephesians 1:20]]), and Hebrews builds its entire argument on Christ's seated priesthood ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 1#Hebrews 1 . 3|Hebrews 1:3]], 13; 8:1; 10:12; 12:2).

Jesus's use of [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 110#Psalm 110 . 1|Psalm 110:1]] establishes the theological foundation for His later explicit claim before the Sanhedrin: "From now on you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 26#Matthew 26 . 64|Matthew 26:64]]). Where [[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 22#Matthew 22 . 44|Matthew 22:44]] proves from Scripture that Messiah must be divine to sit at God's right hand, [[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 26#Matthew 26 . 64|Matthew 26:64]] declares Jesus is that divine Messiah who will occupy that position. The trajectory moves from scriptural proof (ch. 22) to personal claim (ch. 26) to resurrection vindication (ch. 28) to apostolic proclamation (Acts, epistles).

The standing-sitting contrast, though not explicit in this passage, is implicit in the seated claim. Where Levitical priests stood daily offering repeated sacrifices ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 10#Hebrews 10 . 11|Hebrews 10:11]]), the Messiah sits at God's right hand, His seated position proving finished work and established authority. Jesus's question to the Pharisees—"how is he his son?"—finds answer in incarnation: the divine Son who sits eternally at the Father's right hand took on human nature to accomplish redemption, then returned to His rightful position having completed the work. The One who is David's Lord humbled Himself to become David's son, finished the work of atonement, and sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 1#Hebrews 1 . 3|Hebrews 1:3]]), where He remains until all enemies are made His footstool.
