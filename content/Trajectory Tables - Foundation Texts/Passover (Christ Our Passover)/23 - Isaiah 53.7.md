**Context**: Isaiah 53, the fourth and climactic Servant Song, portrays the Suffering Servant who bears the sins of many. Verse 7 describes the Servant's silent submission to unjust suffering using dual lamb imagery: "like a lamb that is led to the slaughter" and "like a sheep that before its shearers is silent." This marks the first explicit connection between the Passover lamb typology and the Messiah's redemptive suffering. The Servant's voluntary silence contrasts with typical human self-defense, revealing divine purpose in His suffering.

**Christological Connection**: [[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 53#Isaiah 53 . 7|Isaiah 53:7]]'s portrayal of the Servant as "a lamb that is led to the slaughter" provides the prophetic foundation for the entire NT Passover lamb typology. Seven centuries before Christ, Isaiah prophetically described the Messiah's silent suffering using explicit lamb imagery, connecting Israel's sacrificial system to the coming Redeemer. John the Baptist's declaration "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 1#John 1 . 29|John 1:29]]) directly echoes [[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 53#Isaiah 53 . 7|Isaiah 53:7]], identifying Jesus as the prophesied lamb. The fusion of Isaiah 53 (Suffering Servant) with Exodus 12 (Passover lamb) reveals that the Messiah would accomplish redemption through sacrificial death, not military conquest. Jesus perfectly fulfilled the silent lamb prophecy throughout His trials—before the Sanhedrin, "he remained silent" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 26#Matthew 26 . 63|Matthew 26:63]]); before Pilate, "he gave him no answer" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 19#John 19 . 9|John 19:9]]); before Herod, "he made no answer" ([[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 23#Luke 23 . 9|Luke 23:9]]). Peter explicitly connects Jesus' silence to Isaiah 53: "When he was reviled, he did not revile in return; when he suffered, he did not threaten, but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" ([[Readable Bible/60 - 1 Peter/1 Peter 2#1 Peter 2 . 23|1 Peter 2:23]]). The Servant's voluntary silence demonstrates not weakness but divine purpose—He could call legions of angels ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 26#Matthew 26 . 53|Matthew 26:53]]) but chose silence to accomplish redemption. Philip's explanation to the Ethiopian eunuch in [[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 8#Acts 8 . 32|Acts 8:32-35]] provides apostolic hermeneutic: this passage "was about Jesus," the good news that the Suffering Servant's lamb-like death brings salvation. Revelation's vision consummates the lamb imagery—"a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain" ([[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 5#Revelation 5 . 6|Revelation 5:6]])—the slaughtered lamb now reigning, worshiped by all creation. Isaiah's prophecy reveals what Exodus's ritual foreshadowed: God Himself would provide the lamb ([[Readable Bible/01 - Genesis/Genesis 22#Genesis 22 . 8|Genesis 22:8]]), His own Son, the perfect Passover sacrifice "without blemish or spot" ([[Readable Bible/60 - 1 Peter/1 Peter 1#1 Peter 1 . 19|1 Peter 1:19]]).
