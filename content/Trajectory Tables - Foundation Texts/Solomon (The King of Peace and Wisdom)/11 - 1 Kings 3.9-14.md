**Context:**
At Gibeon's high place, the LORD appears to Solomon in a dream, offering: "Ask what I shall give you" (v. 5). Rather than requesting wealth, long life, or victory over enemies, Solomon humbly acknowledges his inadequacy—"I am but a little child; I do not know how to go out or come in" (v. 7)—and requests "an understanding heart to govern your people, that I may discern between good and evil" (v. 9). God is so pleased with Solomon's unselfish request that He grants not only unprecedented wisdom—"a wise and discerning mind, so that none like you has been before you and none like you shall arise after you" (v. 12)—but also the riches and honor Solomon didn't seek (v. 13). This divine bestowal of wisdom establishes Solomon as the wisest king in history and prefigures Christ, "in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Colossians 2:3).

**Ninefold Analysis:**

**OT Context:**
First Kings 3 narrates Solomon's pivotal encounter with God at Gibeon, where the tabernacle and bronze altar resided (2 Chronicles 1:3). The chapter opens with Solomon's marriage alliance with Pharaoh's daughter—a political arrangement typical of ancient Near Eastern diplomacy but foreshadowing later compromises. Despite this, Solomon demonstrates genuine piety: "Solomon loved the LORD, walking in the statutes of David his father" (v. 3). At Gibeon, Solomon offers massive sacrifices—"a thousand burnt offerings"—indicating both his devotion and the kingdom's prosperity. God's nocturnal appearance grants Solomon unprecedented access to divine favor: "Ask what I shall give you." Solomon's response reveals remarkable humility and wisdom-before-wisdom. He acknowledges three realities: (1) God's covenant faithfulness to David—"You have shown great and steadfast love (*ḥeseḏ*) to your servant David" (v. 6); (2) His own inadequacy—"I am but a little child" (v. 7), not literally indicating age but expressing insufficiency for the task; (3) The weight of responsibility—"Your people... are too many to be numbered" (v. 8). His request combines three Hebrew concepts: (1) *lēḇ šōmēa'* ("hearing heart")—not merely intellectual capacity but moral sensitivity; (2) *šāp̄aṭ* ("to judge/govern")—royal responsibility for justice; (3) *bîn bên-ṭôḇ lərā'* ("discern between good and evil")—ethical wisdom recalling Eden's tree of knowledge, now sought in proper dependence on God rather than autonomous rebellion. God's pleasure at Solomon's request ("It pleased the Lord that Solomon had asked this," v. 10) leads to threefold blessing: (1) Unparalleled wisdom—"none like you" before or after (v. 12); (2) Unrequested wealth and honor (v. 13); (3) Conditional long life tied to covenant obedience (v. 14). The immediate demonstration of this wisdom follows in the famous judgment of the two women claiming one baby (vv. 16-28), where Solomon's insight into human nature reveals the true mother.

**Jewish Backgrounds:**
Jewish tradition celebrated Solomon's wisdom as second only to God's. Targum Jonathan expands verse 12: "I have given you a wise and understanding heart, and there has not arisen before you like you, and after you there shall not arise like you among the kings." The Talmud (Berakhot 58a) teaches that upon seeing crowds of Israel, one should bless God who is "wise in secrets" (*ḥaḵam raz*), noting Solomon's request for such wisdom to govern the multitude. Rabbinic interpretation (Midrash Mishle 1:1) attributed Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and Song of Solomon to Solomon at different life stages—passion (Song), wisdom (Proverbs), and reflection (Ecclesiastes)—demonstrating wisdom's development. The Wisdom of Solomon (Apocrypha 7:7-14) expands Solomon's prayer: "I prayed, and understanding was given me; I called on God, and the spirit of wisdom came to me. I preferred her to scepters and thrones, and I accounted wealth as nothing in comparison with her." This personification of wisdom influenced later Jewish thought, seeing Wisdom as mediator between God and creation. Josephus (*Antiquities* 8.23-49) emphasizes Solomon's universal wisdom—botanical, zoological, philosophical—making him famous throughout the known world. Medieval commentators like Rashi noted that Solomon's request for "a hearing heart" (*lēḇ šōmēa'*) indicated desire to hear God's voice, not merely human wisdom. Maimonides (*Guide for the Perplexed* 3:54) argued Solomon's wisdom was prophetic gift enabling direct apprehension of divine truth. Kabbalistic tradition saw Solomon's wisdom as accessing the *sefirot* (divine emanations), particularly *Binah* (understanding) and *Ḥokhmah* (wisdom).

**Text Form:**
The passage employs dialogue structure highlighting divine-human interaction. God's question—"Ask what I shall give you" (*šə'al māh 'ettēn-lāḵ*)—uses rhetorical openness, granting unlimited request. Solomon's response structures in three movements: (1) Acknowledgment of past grace (v. 6); (2) Confession of present need (vv. 7-8); (3) Specific petition (v. 9). The phrase "I am but a little child" (*na'ar qāṭōn 'ānōḵî*) uses *na'ar*—"youth, servant"—not indicating literal age (Solomon was likely 20-25) but expressing humility, echoing Moses's "I am slow of speech" (Exodus 4:10) and Jeremiah's "I am only a youth" (Jeremiah 1:6). The idiom "go out and come in" (*lāṣē't wəlāḇô'*) means "to carry out responsibilities," derived from military terminology. Solomon's request for "understanding heart" (*lēḇ šōmēa'*) literally means "hearing heart"—organ of perception that listens to God. The infinitive "to judge" (*lišp̄ōṭ*) indicates purpose: wisdom for governance, not personal enrichment. The phrase "discern between good and evil" (*ləhāḇîn bên-ṭôḇ lərā'*) recalls Genesis 2:9, 17; 3:5, 22, where the knowledge of good and evil was grasped autonomously. Now Solomon seeks this knowledge dependently, through divine gift. God's response uses emphatic structure: "Because (*ya'an 'ăšer*) you have asked this and not asked (*wəlō'-šā'altā*)... behold (*hinnēh*), I do according to your word (*'āśîṯî ḵiḏḇāreḵā*)." The repetition "none like you (*kāmôḵā*) before you, none like you shall arise after you" emphasizes Solomon's unique status. The conditional promise (v. 14) uses *'im* ("if"): long life depends on walking in God's ways.

**Hermeneutical Use:**
First Kings 3:9-14 functions typologically as Solomon's wisdom establishes the category that Christ fulfills completely. The Queen of Sheba's testimony—"the half was not told me" (1 Kings 10:7)—applies infinitely more to Christ, whose wisdom surpasses Solomon's as divinity surpasses humanity. Where Solomon received wisdom as a gift, Christ is wisdom itself—the eternal Logos "through whom are all things" (1 Corinthians 8:6). Paul's declaration that in Christ "are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Colossians 2:3) directly contrasts with Solomon's limited, though great, wisdom. The pattern of asking and receiving finds universal application in James 1:5: "If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God, who gives generously to all without reproach, and it will be given him"—democratizing what was concentrated in Solomon. Jesus' teaching on asking (Matthew 7:7-11) promises that the Father gives "good things to those who ask him," with the Holy Spirit being the ultimate gift (Luke 11:13). The "greater than Solomon" saying (Matthew 12:42; Luke 11:31) establishes Christ's superiority in all wisdom-related categories. Solomon's wisdom led to literary production (Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Solomon); Christ's teaching produces the gospel and apostolic doctrine. Solomon's wisdom governed earthly Israel; Christ's wisdom governs the church and will govern the new creation. Solomon's wisdom came through dream-revelation; Christ possesses wisdom eternally as the second person of the Trinity. Solomon's wisdom couldn't save him from idolatry in old age (1 Kings 11); Christ's wisdom never fails, never compromises. The typology moves from gifted wisdom to incarnate Wisdom, from temporary application to eternal reign, from Israelite king to cosmic Christ.

**Theological Use:**
This passage establishes foundational truths about wisdom and divine gifting: **(1) True wisdom comes from God alone**—Solomon doesn't rely on education or experience but asks God directly. This aligns with Proverbs 2:6: "The LORD gives wisdom; from his mouth come knowledge and understanding." Human wisdom, apart from God, is "earthly, unspiritual, demonic" (James 3:15), but wisdom from above is "pure, then peaceable, gentle, open to reason" (James 3:17). **(2) Wisdom requires humility**—Solomon's "I am but a little child" models the prerequisite for receiving divine wisdom. Jesus declares: "Unless you turn and become like children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 18:3). **(3) Wisdom is for service, not self**—Solomon seeks wisdom to govern God's people, not for personal glory. Christ's wisdom serves redemption: "Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24) accomplishes salvation. **(4) Wisdom includes ethical discernment**—"Discern between good and evil" shows wisdom isn't merely intellectual but moral. Hebrews 5:14 describes maturity: "Those who have their powers of discernment trained by constant practice to distinguish good from evil." **(5) God delights in unselfish requests**—"It pleased the Lord that Solomon had asked this" (v. 10). God rewards those who seek His priorities: "Seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you" (Matthew 6:33). **(6) Divine wisdom surpasses human categories**—"None like you before you and none like you shall arise after you" (v. 12) points to Christ's absolute uniqueness. **(7) Wisdom is gift, not achievement**—Solomon doesn't earn wisdom through study but receives it through prayer, anticipating the Spirit's distribution of gifts (1 Corinthians 12:8).

**Rhetorical Use:**
The passage's rhetorical power lies in the striking contrast between what Solomon could have asked and what he actually requested. God's open offer—"Ask what I shall give you"—creates unlimited possibility, testing Solomon's heart. The narrative invites readers to ask themselves: What would I request if given one wish? Wealth? Power? Long life? Victory over enemies? Solomon's choice of wisdom—specifically, wisdom to serve others—reveals his character and God's priorities. The threefold structure of God's response (wisdom granted, riches added, long life conditional) teaches that seeking God's kingdom first brings additional blessings (Matthew 6:33). The immediate demonstration of Solomon's wisdom in the next narrative episode (3:16-28) provides rhetorical proof: God kept His promise. The Queen of Sheba's later testimony (10:1-13) extends the proof internationally. Rhetorically, the passage functions as wisdom literature's foundational charter: all subsequent biblical wisdom flows from this divine bestowal. Proverbs begins "The proverbs of Solomon, son of David" (1:1), grounding its authority in this God-given wisdom. For Christian readers, the passage models prayer: specific, humble, other-centered, aligned with God's purposes. It also establishes the typological key for understanding Christ: if Solomon's wisdom was unprecedented and unsurpassed among humans, how much more must we seek the wisdom found in Christ?

**Christological Connection:**
First Kings 3:9-14 establishes Solomon as wisdom-type of Christ, who fulfills and infinitely exceeds the pattern. **Solomon asked for wisdom; Christ is wisdom incarnate.** Paul declares Christ "the wisdom of God" ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 1#1 Corinthians 1 . 24|1 Corinthians 1:24]]) and states that "in him are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" ([[Readable Bible/51 - Colossians/Colossians 2#Colossians 2 . 3|Colossians 2:3]]). Where Solomon received wisdom as external gift, Christ eternally possesses wisdom as the divine Logos "through whom all things were made" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 1#John 1 . 3|John 1:3]]). **Solomon's wisdom was unprecedented among humans; Christ's wisdom is absolute.** Jesus declares: "Something greater than Solomon is here" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 12#Matthew 12 . 42|Matthew 12:42]]). The Queen of Sheba traveled to hear Solomon; the wise men traveled to worship infant Christ. Solomon's wisdom attracted visitors; Christ's wisdom draws all peoples to Himself ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 12#John 12 . 32|John 12:32]]). **Solomon sought wisdom to discern good and evil; Christ judges all things.** [[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 5#John 5 . 22|John 5:22]] reveals: "The Father judges no one, but has given all judgment to the Son." Where Solomon needed wisdom-gift to judge Israel, Christ judges the living and dead with perfect righteousness. **Solomon's wisdom came through dream; Christ's wisdom is eternal.** Before Abraham existed, Christ declares "I am" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 8#John 8 . 58|John 8:58]]). His wisdom doesn't develop or increase but eternally exists: "In the beginning was the Word" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 1#John 1 . 1|John 1:1]]). **Solomon's wisdom built a physical temple; Christ's wisdom accomplishes cosmic redemption.** Paul marvels at "the manifold wisdom of God" displayed through the church, "so that through the church the manifold wisdom of God might now be made known to the rulers and authorities in the heavenly places" ([[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 3#Ephesians 3 . 10|Ephesians 3:10]]). What Solomon's wisdom organized in Israel, Christ's wisdom orders in new creation. **Solomon's wisdom couldn't save him from folly; Christ's wisdom never fails.** First Kings 11 records Solomon's tragic fall into idolatry through foreign wives. Christ, though "tempted as we are," remained "without sin" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 4#Hebrews 4 . 15|Hebrews 4:15]]). His wisdom never compromises, never corrupts. **Solomon's wisdom was for earthly governance; Christ's wisdom governs eternally.** [[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 5#Revelation 5 . 12|Revelation 5:12]] proclaims the slaughtered Lamb "worthy to receive power and wealth and wisdom and might and honor and glory and blessing!" Where Solomon's wisdom administered temporal kingdom, Christ's wisdom redeems and rules forever. Most profoundly, Christ transforms what seemed foolish into ultimate wisdom: "The foolishness of God is wiser than men, and the weakness of God is stronger than men" ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 1#1 Corinthians 1 . 25|1 Corinthians 1:25]]). The cross—apparent folly to the world—proves to be "the wisdom of God" accomplishing what human wisdom never could: reconciliation between holy God and sinful humanity. Solomon's wisdom answered difficult questions ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 10#1 Kings 10 . 3|1 Kings 10:3]]); Christ's wisdom solves the ultimate problem: how can God be "just and the justifier" ([[Readable Bible/45 - Romans/Romans 3#Romans 3 . 26|Romans 3:26]])? The answer: the cross, where divine wisdom and power unite to save. Therefore believers receive wisdom not through their own striving but through union with Christ: "You are in Christ Jesus, who became to us wisdom from God, righteousness and sanctification and redemption" ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 1#1 Corinthians 1 . 30|1 Corinthians 1:30]]).
