**Context:**
Following Adonijah's attempted coup, David decisively acts to secure Solomon's succession through immediate anointing and public enthronement. Zadok the priest and Nathan the prophet lead the procession to Gihon spring, where Solomon rides David's own mule—a sign of royal authority transferred from father to son. The anointing with sacred oil from the tabernacle consecrates Solomon as God's chosen king. The blowing of the trumpet announces the new reign, and the people respond with such joyous celebration that "the earth split with their noise." This peaceful succession, orchestrated by divine providence and David's wisdom, contrasts sharply with typical ancient Near Eastern power transitions marked by violence and bloodshed. Solomon's very name (*šəlōmōh*, from *šālôm*—peace) prophetically identifies his reign's character and prefigures the Prince of Peace who would bring eternal peace between God and humanity.

**Ninefold Analysis:**

**OT Context:**
First Kings 1 narrates the succession crisis precipitated by David's advanced age and Adonijah's premature attempt to seize the throne. Adonijah, David's fourth son and oldest surviving heir after Amnon, Chileab, and Absalom, assumed the throne was his by primogeniture. He "exalted himself, saying, 'I will be king'" (v. 5), gathering support from Joab the military commander and Abiathar the priest. However, Adonijah fatally miscalculated by excluding three key figures from his coronation feast: Zadok the priest, Benaiah the warrior, and Nathan the prophet—the very men loyal to David's choice of Solomon. Nathan's strategic intervention through Bathsheba (vv. 11-27) prompts David's immediate action. The aged king swears by the LORD that Solomon will reign after him (vv. 28-30), then issues decisive commands for immediate enthronement (vv. 32-35). The anointing at Gihon, Jerusalem's eastern water source, deliberately parallels the tabernacle's location and priestly anointing. Solomon rides David's personal mule—in ancient Near Eastern culture, riding the king's mount signified legitimate succession and transfer of royal authority. The use of sacred oil from the tent of meeting consecrates Solomon as both divinely chosen and ceremonially set apart. The trumpet blast (*šôp̄ār*) announces regime change, summoning the people to recognize the new king. Their response—"Long live King Solomon!" (*yəḥî hammelleḵ šəlōmōh*)—acknowledges his legitimate authority. The people's rejoicing is so intense that "the earth split with their noise" (v. 40), suggesting cosmic affirmation of God's will. This celebration reaches Adonijah's feast, causing his supporters to scatter in terror (vv. 41-49). The peaceful transition, achieved without civil war, demonstrates God's sovereignty in establishing Solomon and fulfills the Davidic covenant's promise that David's son would build the temple and establish the kingdom.

**Jewish Backgrounds:**
Jewish tradition emphasized Solomon's succession as paradigmatic of messianic enthronement. Targum Jonathan on 1 Kings 1:39 expands: "Zadok the priest took the horn of oil from the tabernacle of the LORD and anointed Solomon; and they blew with trumpets, and all the people said, 'Long live King Solomon!'" The Midrash (Ecclesiastes Rabbah 1:1) notes that Solomon's anointing marked the third time in Scripture where oil consecrated a king specifically chosen by divine decree, not merely human selection—following Saul and David. Rabbinic interpretation saw significance in the trumpet blast: as the *šôp̄ār* announced Solomon's coronation, so it would herald Messiah's coming (Isaiah 27:13). The Talmud (Horayot 11b-12a) discusses Solomon's double anointing—here at Gihon and later at Gibeon (1 Kings 1:38-40; 1 Chronicles 29:22)—as establishing uncontested kingship. Medieval Jewish commentators like Rashi noted that Solomon's coronation during David's lifetime ensured smooth transition and prevented civil war, contrasting with Adonijah's failed coup. Ibn Ezra observed that riding David's mule symbolized continuity of covenant faithfulness—the kingdom transferred peacefully from God's chosen servant to his heir. The Zohar (mystical tradition) interpreted Solomon's name from *šālôm* (peace) as reflecting his role in bringing cosmic harmony—prefiguring the messianic age when universal peace would reign. Josephus (*Antiquities* 7.346-359) emphasizes the divine legitimacy of Solomon's anointing: "David commanded Zadok the high priest and Nathan the prophet to anoint Solomon at Gihon, that being the place where the tabernacle was, and where judgments were rendered, signifying that God Himself approved of this king."

**Text Form:**
The narrative employs rapid wayyiqtol sequences to convey urgency and decisive action: "So Zadok... and Nathan... went down and caused Solomon to ride... and anointed him... and they blew... and all the people said..." The staccato rhythm mirrors the swift, purposeful execution of David's commands. The text strategically positions Solomon between sacred and military authority: "Zadok the priest and Nathan the prophet" frame his legitimacy, while "Benaiah son of Jehoiada and the Cherethites and Pelethites" provide security. The location "Gihon" carries symbolic weight—as water source sustaining Jerusalem, it represents life and divine blessing. The verb "anointed" (*māšaḥ*) intentionally recalls earlier uses for Saul (1 Samuel 10:1) and David (1 Samuel 16:13), placing Solomon in the succession of divinely appointed kings. The people's acclamation "Long live King Solomon!" (*yəḥî hammelleḵ šəlōmōh*) uses the jussive form expressing wish or prayer: "May the king live!" This formula acknowledges both human authority and dependence on divine preservation. The description of celebration—"the people went up after him, playing on pipes, and rejoicing with great joy, so that the earth split with their noise"—uses hyperbolic language (*tib̄bāqa' hā'āreṣ*, "the earth split") suggesting eschatological significance. The musical instruments (*pipes*, *ḥălîlîm*) typically accompanied worship and festive occasions, indicating this is not merely political but liturgical event—the Lord's anointed receives His people's worship. The structural climax comes in verse 46: "Solomon sits on the royal throne" (*wayyēšeḇ šəlōmōh 'al kissē' hammləḵûṯ*)—the definitive statement establishing his reign.

**Hermeneutical Use:**
This passage functions typologically as divine blueprint for messianic enthronement, establishing patterns that recur throughout salvation history and reach culmination in Christ. The peaceful succession without violence prefigures Christ's kingdom not established by sword but through submission to God's will. Solomon's anointing by priest and prophet points to Christ who uniquely combines prophetic (speaking God's word), priestly (mediating between God and people), and kingly (ruling God's people) offices. The trumpet blast announcing Solomon's reign anticipates eschatological trumpets heralding Christ's return and kingdom consummation (Matthew 24:31; 1 Corinthians 15:52; 1 Thessalonians 4:16; Revelation 11:15). The people's spontaneous joy—"rejoicing with great joy"—foreshadows the gladness accompanying Messiah's advent: Zechariah 9:9 commands "Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion!" at the coming king, fulfilled when crowds welcome Jesus with "Hosanna!" (Matthew 21:9). Solomon riding the king's mule parallels Christ's triumphal entry on a donkey (Matthew 21:1-11), both signifying peaceful rather than military conquest. The anointing with sacred oil typifies Christ's anointing with the Holy Spirit: as Solomon received external oil, Christ receives the Spirit without measure (John 3:34), making Him the ultimate "Anointed One" (*Māšîaḥ*/*Christos*). The earthquake-like response to Solomon's coronation ("the earth split") anticipates the cosmic response to Christ's death, resurrection, and return (Matthew 27:51; 28:2; Revelation 11:13, 19). Hebrews 1:8-9 explicitly applies Psalm 45's royal anointing to Christ: "God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of gladness beyond your companions," identifying Jesus as the ultimate fulfillment of Davidic kingship. The peaceful establishment of Solomon's throne previews Christ's kingdom "not of this world" (John 18:36), established not through political revolution but through sacrificial death and resurrection.

**Theological Use:**
First Kings 1:38-40 establishes foundational truths about divine kingship that inform Christology and ecclesiology: **(1) Divine election, not human merit**—Solomon becomes king not because he was the eldest or most qualified, but because God chose him and David confirmed that choice. Similarly, Christ's kingship derives from divine decree: "I have set my King on Zion" (Psalm 2:6). Human credentials don't qualify; God's sovereign choice does. **(2) Anointing consecrates and empowers**—The sacred oil set Solomon apart for royal office. Christ's name means "Anointed One" (Christos = Messiah), anointed with the Holy Spirit at His baptism (Matthew 3:16-17). Believers share this anointing: "You have been anointed by the Holy One" (1 John 2:20), consecrated for priestly service in God's kingdom. **(3) Peaceful succession reflects divine blessing**—The absence of civil war demonstrates God's favor. Christ's kingdom advances not through violence but through the gospel's peaceful proclamation (Isaiah 52:7). **(4) Public acclamation confirms legitimacy**—The people's joyful recognition of Solomon parallels the church's confession of Christ as Lord. True kingship requires both divine appointment and people's acknowledgment. **(5) Continuity with covenant promises**—Solomon's enthronement fulfills 2 Samuel 7:12-13's promise that David's son would establish the kingdom. Christ's enthronement fulfills this ultimately: "The Lord God will give to him the throne of his father David, and he will reign over the house of Jacob forever" (Luke 1:32-33). **(6) Joy as appropriate response to God's king**—The people's rejoicing models the glad submission believers offer Christ: "Your people will offer themselves freely on the day of your power" (Psalm 110:3). **(7) The trumpet announces kingdom inauguration**—As the *šôp̄ār* heralded Solomon's reign, trumpets will announce Christ's return: "The trumpet will sound, and the dead will be raised" (1 Corinthians 15:52).

**Rhetorical Use:**
The passage's rhetorical power lies in its dramatic reversal and decisive resolution. The narrative builds tension through Adonijah's presumptuous feast (1:5-10), Nathan's urgent intervention (1:11-27), and David's oath (1:28-31), then releases that tension through rapid-fire action as Solomon is anointed, acclaimed, and enthroned—all while Adonijah feasts in ignorance. The swift succession of verbs creates unstoppable momentum: "went down... caused to ride... took the horn of oil... anointed... blew the trumpet... said... went up after him... playing... rejoicing." This literary velocity mirrors the theological point: when God acts to establish His king, no human opposition can prevail. The contrast between competing feasts—Adonijah's self-congratulatory banquet versus the people's spontaneous celebration—highlights the difference between self-appointed and divinely appointed authority. The strategic placement of key figures—Zadok, Nathan, Benaiah—demonstrates that legitimate kingship requires priestly, prophetic, and military affirmation, not merely popular support. The earthquake-like rejoicing ("the earth split") elevates the scene from political event to cosmic significance, suggesting that Solomon's enthronement has implications beyond Israel. Rhetorically, the passage invites readers to align themselves with the people's joyful acclamation rather than Adonijah's fearful scattering. The repeated "Long live King Solomon!" becomes a liturgical formula, training God's people to acclaim His chosen king. The peaceful succession proves that God's purposes advance not through human scheming (Adonijah) but through divine selection confirmed by faithful servants (David, Nathan, Zadok). For Christian readers, the passage models appropriate response to Christ's kingship: joyful, wholehearted, public acclamation accompanied by worship and submission.

**Christological Connection:**
First Kings 1:38-40 provides an OT paradigm for understanding Christ's enthronement and kingdom. Every element of Solomon's coronation finds greater fulfillment in Jesus: **The peaceful succession** anticipates Christ who, though "despised and rejected" ([[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 53#Isaiah 53 . 3|Isaiah 53:3]]), receives the kingdom not through military conquest but through sacrificial death and victorious resurrection. Solomon's smooth transition prevented civil war; Christ's cross-and-resurrection victory defeats the usurper Satan without earthly bloodshed. **The anointing with sacred oil** prefigures Christ's anointing with the Holy Spirit. At His baptism, "the Spirit of God descended on him like a dove" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 3#Matthew 3 . 16|Matthew 3:16]]), consecrating Him for His messianic mission. Peter declares: "God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power" ([[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 10#Acts 10 . 38|Acts 10:38]]). While Solomon received external oil, Christ receives the Spirit without measure ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 3#John 3 . 34|John 3:34]]), making Him the ultimate *Māšîaḥ*—the Anointed One. **Riding the king's mount** prefigures Christ's triumphal entry into Jerusalem. Solomon rode David's mule; Jesus rode a donkey's colt ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 21#Matthew 21 . 1|Matthew 21:1-7]]), deliberately fulfilling [[Readable Bible/38 - Zechariah/Zechariah 9#Zechariah 9 . 9|Zechariah 9:9]]: "Your king is coming to you, humble, and mounted on a donkey." Both entries signify peaceful kingship, not military conquest—the king comes to bless, not to conquer through force. **The trumpet blast** announcing Solomon's reign anticipates eschatological trumpets heralding Christ's return. Paul writes: "The Lord himself will descend from heaven with a cry of command, with the voice of an archangel, and with the sound of the trumpet of God" ([[Readable Bible/52 - 1 Thessalonians/1 Thessalonians 4#1 Thessalonians 4 . 16|1 Thessalonians 4:16]]). As the *šôp̄ār* summoned Israel to acknowledge Solomon, the final trumpet will summon all creation to acknowledge Christ: "At the name of Jesus every knee should bow" ([[Readable Bible/50 - Philippians/Philippians 2#Philippians 2 . 10|Philippians 2:10]]). **The people's joyful acclamation**—"Long live King Solomon!"—finds echo in the crowds welcoming Jesus: "Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord, even the King of Israel!" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 12#John 12 . 13|John 12:13]]). The verb "rejoicing with great joy" (*śāmēaḥ śimḥāh gədôlāh*) parallels the "great joy" (*charan megalen*) accompanying Christ's birth ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 2#Matthew 2 . 10|Matthew 2:10]]) and resurrection ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 28#Matthew 28 . 8|Matthew 28:8]]). **The earth splitting with noise** anticipates cosmic responses to Christ's saving work: earth quakes at His crucifixion ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 27#Matthew 27 . 51|Matthew 27:51]]), resurrection ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 28#Matthew 28 . 2|Matthew 28:2]]), and will quake at His return ([[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 11#Revelation 11 . 13|Revelation 11:13]], 19). **Solomon sitting on the throne** prefigures Christ's session at God's right hand: "He sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 1#Hebrews 1 . 3|Hebrews 1:3]]). Solomon's throne was temporary; Christ's is eternal: "Your throne, O God, is forever and ever" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 1#Hebrews 1 . 8|Hebrews 1:8]]). Most profoundly, Solomon's name—*šəlōmōh* from *šālôm* (peace)—anticipates Christ the "Prince of Peace" ([[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 9#Isaiah 9 . 6|Isaiah 9:6]]) who makes "peace by the blood of his cross" ([[Readable Bible/51 - Colossians/Colossians 1#Colossians 1 . 20|Colossians 1:20]]). Solomon brought political peace to Israel; Christ brings spiritual peace between God and sinners, and cosmic peace reconciling all things. The trajectory is clear: Solomon's peaceful succession, anointing, acclamation, and enthronement establish the pattern; Christ's incarnation, Spirit-anointing, triumphal entry, crucifixion, resurrection, and ascension fulfill it completely. Where Solomon's kingdom was established "in the hand of Solomon" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 2#1 Kings 2 . 46|1 Kings 2:46]]), Christ's kingdom is established in His own death-conquering power—"authority over all flesh" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 17#John 17 . 2|John 17:2]]), reigning until "he has put all his enemies under his feet" ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 15#1 Corinthians 15 . 25|1 Corinthians 15:25]]).
