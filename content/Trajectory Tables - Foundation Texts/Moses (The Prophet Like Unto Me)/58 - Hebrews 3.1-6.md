**Context:**
Hebrews 3:1-6 presents Jesus as superior to Moses—faithful Son over God's house versus faithful servant in God's house. The passage bridges from Christ's superiority to angels (chapters 1-2) to warning against unbelief using Israel's wilderness failure (3:7-4:13). The author addresses "holy brothers, who share in a heavenly calling" (v. 1), urging them to "consider Jesus, the apostle and high priest of our confession" (v. 1). The comparison: Moses was faithful in God's house as servant testifying to future things (v. 5), Christ is faithful over God's house as Son (v. 6). The conclusion: "we are his house if indeed we hold fast our confidence and our boasting in our hope" (v. 6). This establishes Jesus' credentials for warning against apostasy—having greater authority than Moses, His words demand greater heed. The passage's theology: Christ doesn't diminish Moses but fulfills what Moses prefigured; Moses' faithfulness was real but anticipatory; Christ's faithfulness is complete and consummatory.

**Ninefold Analysis:**

**OT Context:**
Hebrews 3:1-6 draws from Numbers 12:7-8, God's vindication of Moses when Miriam and Aaron challenged his unique authority. They questioned "Has the LORD indeed spoken only through Moses? Has he not spoken through us also?" (Numbers 12:2). God responded by defending Moses' exceptional status: "If there is a prophet among you, I the LORD make myself known to him in a vision; I speak with him in a dream. Not so with my servant Moses. He is faithful in all my house. With him I speak mouth to mouth, clearly, and not in riddles, and he beholds the form of the LORD" (12:6-8). The phrase "faithful in all my house" (*ne'eman bekol-beyti*) establishes Moses' comprehensive trustworthiness—entrusted with God's entire household (Israel), mediating covenant, law, and worship. Moses' faithfulness manifested multiply: obeying God's call despite reluctance (Exodus 3-4), confronting Pharaoh repeatedly (Exodus 5-12), leading complaining people patiently (Exodus 15-17; Numbers 11-14), interceding for sinful nation (Exodus 32:30-34; Numbers 14:13-19), constructing tabernacle exactly as commanded (Exodus 40:16: "Moses did this; according to all that the LORD commanded him, so he did"). Deuteronomy 34:10-12 eulogizes Moses: "And there has not arisen a prophet since in Israel like Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face, none like him for all the signs and the wonders that the LORD sent him to do in the land of Egypt." Yet Moses' faithfulness was instrumental and preparatory—he testified to God's future works without accomplishing them himself. Hebrews identifies Christ as greater: Moses served within house as faithful witness; Christ rules over house as faithful Son.

**Jewish Backgrounds:**
Jewish tradition venerated Moses supremely. Philo titled him "king, lawgiver, high priest, and prophet" (*Life of Moses* 2.2-7), portraying him as humanity's apex—philosopher-ruler embodying perfect wisdom. Josephus emphasized Moses' incomparability: "by reason of his virtue, of his reputation with God, and of the divine assistance he enjoyed in all his practices, he could not but be highly virtuous and successful in all matters he undertook" (*Antiquities* 3.320). Rabbinic literature developed extensive Moses theology. The Mishnah (Avot 1:1) traces oral Torah's transmission: "Moses received Torah from Sinai and transmitted it to Joshua, and Joshua to the elders"—establishing Moses as mediatorial fountainhead of all tradition. The Talmud declared "Moses is equivalent to all of Israel" (Berakhot 32a)—corporate representative embodying nation's identity. Midrash portrayed Moses as intercessor par excellence: when God threatened Israel's destruction after golden calf, Moses argued until God relented (Exodus Rabbah 42-44)—willingness to be blotted from God's book (Exodus 32:32) demonstrated ultimate self-sacrifice. Yet Jewish thought also recognized Moses' limitations. Deuteronomy 34:5-6 records his death and burial—he was mortal, not divine. Numbers 20:12 recounts God's punishment preventing Moses from entering Canaan—even faithful Moses sinned. Jewish messianic expectation anticipated greater-than-Moses figure. Deuteronomy 18:15's "prophet like Moses" generated debate whether Messiah would equal or surpass Moses. Some texts suggested Messiah would exceed Moses: Exodus Rabbah 1:26 taught Moses brought manna, but Messiah will bring bread from heaven permanently; Moses brought water from rock, but Messiah will cause rivers to flow from Jerusalem. The author of Hebrews enters this discussion, asserting Jesus surpasses Moses infinitely—not merely superior prophet but divine Son.

**Text Form:**
Hebrews 3:1-6 employs comparison structure with vocative address and inferential logic. Verse 1 opens with vocative: "Therefore, holy brothers, who share in a heavenly calling" (*hothen, adelphoi hagioi, klēseōs epouraniou metochoi*). The "therefore" (*hothen*) connects to preceding argument (Christ's incarnation qualifying Him as merciful high priest, 2:10-18). The phrase "holy brothers" (*adelphoi hagioi*) addresses Christian community using familial and sacred terminology. The participle "who share in" (*metochoi*, "partakers") with genitive "heavenly calling" (*klēseōs epouraniou*) emphasizes believers' participation in divine summons. The imperative "consider" (*katanoēsate*) uses aorist tense—decisive action required. The object "Jesus" receives double designation: "apostle and high priest of our confession" (*ton apostolon kai archierea tēs homologias hēmōn*). "Apostle" (*apostolon*, "sent one") identifies Jesus as God's sent representative; "high priest" (*archierea*) identifies Him as humanity's representative before God. The phrase "of our confession" (*tēs homologias hēmōn*) indicates these are confessed realities, not abstract doctrines. Verse 2 introduces comparison: "who was faithful to him who appointed him" (*piston onta tō poiēsanti auton*). The participial phrase parallels Moses' description coming in verse 5. The comparative clause "just as Moses also was faithful in all God's house" (*hōs kai Mōusēs en holō tō oikō autou*) quotes Numbers 12:7 establishing baseline. Verse 3 employs *qal wahomer* reasoning: "For Jesus has been counted worthy of more glory than Moses" (*pleionos... doxēs para Mōusēn*). The comparative *pleionos* ("more") with genitive *doxēs* ("glory") indicates degree of superiority. The explanatory clause uses household metaphor: "as much more glory as the builder of a house has more honor than the house itself" (*kath' hoson pleiona timēn echei tou oikou ho kataskeuasas auton*). Christ is builder (*kataskeuasas*), Moses is part of house (*oikou*). Verse 4 establishes theological premise: "For every house is built by someone, but the builder of all things is God" (*pas gar oikos kataskeuazetai hypo tinos, ho de panta kataskeuasas theos*). This prevents misunderstanding—acknowledging God as ultimate builder while maintaining Christ's superior role. Verse 5 resumes comparison: "Now Moses was faithful in all God's house as a servant" (*Mōusēs men pistos en holō tō oikō autou hōs therapōn*). The particle *men* signals first part of *men-de* ("on one hand... on the other hand") construction creating contrast. The designation "servant" (*therapōn*) uses term emphasizing service rather than slavery (*doulos*)—honorable service. The purpose clause "to testify to the things that were to be spoken later" (*eis martyrion tōn lalēthēsomenōn*) defines Moses' role—prophetic witness pointing forward. Verse 6 provides contrast: "but Christ is faithful over God's house as a son" (*Christos de hōs huios epi ton oikon autou*). The *de* completes *men-de* construction. The preposition *epi* ("over") with accusative indicates authority—not merely within house but ruling over it. The designation "son" (*huios*) establishes superior relationship. The concluding conditional: "whose house we are, if indeed we hold fast" (*hou oikos esmen hēmeis, ean... katachōmen*) identifies believers as Christ's house, conditioned on perseverance.

**Hermeneutical Use:**
The author's hermeneutic interprets Numbers 12:7 ("Moses faithful in all God's house") typologically—Moses' historical faithfulness prefigures Christ's superior faithfulness. The interpretive principle: OT saints received genuine callings and served faithfully, but their service pointed beyond itself to Christ who fulfills all OT types. This hermeneutic pervades Hebrews: tabernacle/priesthood/sacrifices are "copy and shadow of the heavenly things" (8:5), Moses was "servant... to testify to the things that were to be spoken later" (3:5). The household imagery interprets Israel corporately: Moses served within God's household (Israel as covenant community), Christ rules over God's household (church as new covenant community). The builder metaphor establishes Christ's creative role: Moses arranged household but didn't build it, Christ built all things as divine Word (John 1:3: "All things were made through him"). The servant-son contrast employs family structure: servants work for household, sons inherit household; servants' status is positional, sons' status is relational; servants' tenure is temporary, sons' tenure is eternal. This hermeneutic appears in Galatians 4:1-7: under law believers were like children under guardians (servants ruling them), but in Christ they're mature sons inheriting promises. The "testifying to things spoken later" (Hebrews 3:5) interprets all Moses' ministry as prophetic—law anticipated grace, old covenant anticipated new, external regulations anticipated internal transformation, earthly Canaan anticipated heavenly rest. The conditional "if we hold fast" (3:6) introduces perseverance theology developed through chapter 4—warning that failure to persevere evidences non-genuine faith (like wilderness generation who fell away).

**Theological Use:**
Hebrews 3:1-6 establishes systematic doctrines: (1) **Christology**: Jesus is apostle (sent from God to humanity) and high priest (sent from humanity to God)—perfect mediator bridging divine-human divide. (2) **Theology proper**: God is ultimate builder of all things (v. 4)—creation's architect whose Son executes His designs. (3) **Covenant theology**: Moses mediated old covenant as servant testifying to future realities, Christ mediates new covenant as Son accomplishing those realities. (4) **Ecclesiology**: Church is Christ's house (v. 6)—spiritual temple where Christ dwells, believers as living stones (1 Peter 2:5). (5) **Soteriology**: Participation in heavenly calling (v. 1) defines believers, conditioned on perseverance (v. 6)—genuine faith endures. (6) **Eschatology**: Moses testified to "things to be spoken later" (v. 5)—OT prophetically oriented toward Christ's coming and kingdom's consummation. (7) **Anthropology**: Believers are "holy brothers" (v. 1)—sanctified through Christ, adopted into God's family, united as siblings. (8) **Revelation theology**: Progressive revelation moves from servant (Moses) to Son (Christ)—fragmentary to complete, preparatory to consummatory. Systematic integration: Christ's person (divine-human Son) qualifies His work (perfect mediation); His superiority to Moses validates new covenant's superiority to old; His faithfulness as Son surpasses even Moses' faithfulness as servant; believers' union with Christ incorporates them into His house with responsibility to persevere.

**Rhetorical Use:**
The author's rhetorical strategy honors Moses while exalting Christ, preventing Jewish-Christian readers from regressing to Judaism. The opening address "holy brothers who share in heavenly calling" (v. 1) affirms readers' identity—encouraging perseverance by reminding them of privileged status. The imperative "consider Jesus" (*katanoēsate*) demands thoughtful reflection, not superficial acknowledgment—recognizing who Jesus truly is prevents apostasy. The double designation "apostle and high priest" combines offices—Jesus sent by God (like prophets) and represents humanity (like priests), uniquely qualified for complete mediation. The Moses comparison creates common ground: readers revere Moses (Jewish background) or respect him (Gentile background aware of Jewish tradition)—author honors this while asserting Christ's supremacy. The builder-house metaphor illustrates inherent superiority: as builder exceeds building, Christ exceeds Moses—not demeaning Moses but contextualizing his role within larger divine plan. The servant-son contrast employs household imagery familiar to readers: everyone understands son's authority surpasses servant's, making theological point accessible. The conditional conclusion (v. 6: "if we hold fast") introduces urgency transitioning to warning passage (3:7-4:13)—rhetorical preparation for exhortation against unbelief. The rhetoric balances honor (Moses was genuinely faithful) with escalation (Christ is infinitely more faithful), continuity (same God, same house) with discontinuity (servant versus Son, testimony versus accomplishment), assurance (we are Christ's house) with warning (if we hold fast).

**Christological Connection:**
[[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 3#Hebrews 3 . 1|Hebrews 3:1-6]] establishes Christ's superiority to Moses through systematic comparison magnifying every dimension of Moses' ministry while demonstrating Christ's infinite surpassing. Moses was faithful in God's house ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 12#Numbers 12 . 7|Numbers 12:7]]), Christ is faithful over God's house (v. 6)—preposition shift (*en*, "in" versus *epi*, "over") indicates positional difference: Moses served within household as steward, Christ rules over household as heir. Moses was servant (*therapōn*, v. 5), Christ is Son (*huios*, v. 6)—relational difference: servants work for wages, sons inherit estate; servants have limited tenure, sons have permanent status; servants execute others' plans, sons embody father's will. Moses testified to future things (v. 5), Christ accomplishes those things—temporal difference: Moses prophetically pointed forward, Christ historically fulfills; Moses anticipated Christ's coming, Christ achieved redemption; Moses' ministry was preparatory, Christ's is consummatory. The builder-house metaphor (v. 3) establishes ontological difference: Moses was creature within creation, Christ is Creator of creation ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 1#John 1 . 3|John 1:3]]: "All things were made through him, and without him was not any thing made that was made"). Though verse 4 clarifies "the builder of all things is God"—preventing subordinationism—the author maintains Christ's divine identity as agent through whom Father creates. Moses built tabernacle according to pattern shown on mountain ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 25#Exodus 25 . 40|Exodus 25:40]]: "See that you make them after the pattern... shown you on the mountain"); Christ built spiritual temple (church) according to eternal counsel. Moses received blueprints, Christ designed reality. Moses mediated old covenant through animal blood ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 24#Exodus 24 . 8|Exodus 24:8]]), Christ mediates new covenant through His own blood ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 9#Hebrews 9 . 12|Hebrews 9:12-14]]). Moses' mediation was external and temporary, Christ's is internal and eternal. Moses as apostle (sent one) was sent to Pharaoh ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 3#Exodus 3 . 10|Exodus 3:10]]: "I will send you to Pharaoh that you may bring my people... out of Egypt"); Christ as Apostle was sent into world ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 3#John 3 . 17|John 3:17]]: "God did not send his Son into the world to condemn the world, but in order that the world might be saved through him"). Moses confronted earthly king demanding physical liberation; Christ confronts spiritual powers securing eternal salvation. Moses as quasi-priest interceded for Israel ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 32#Exodus 32 . 30|Exodus 32:30-32]]), offering himself as substitute—"But now, if you will forgive their sin—but if not, please blot me out of your book"; Christ as High Priest offered Himself as sacrifice ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 7#Hebrews 7 . 27|Hebrews 7:27]]: "he has no need, like those high priests, to offer sacrifices daily... he did this once for all when he offered up himself"). Moses' intercession obtained temporary reprieve; Christ's intercession secures eternal redemption. Moses' priesthood was informal (not Levitical lineage), Christ's priesthood is formal and superior—"after the order of Melchizedek" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 5#Hebrews 5 . 6|Hebrews 5:6]]; [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 7#Hebrews 7 . 17|7:17]]), eternal and unchangeable. Moses led Israel to Canaan's border but couldn't enter ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 34#Deuteronomy 34 . 4|Deuteronomy 34:4]]: "I have let you see it with your eyes, but you shall not go over there"); Christ leads believers into heavenly rest ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 4#Hebrews 4 . 9|Hebrews 4:9-10]]: "So then, there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God, for whoever has entered God's rest has also rested from his works as God did from his"). Moses' destination was earthly and temporal, Christ's is heavenly and eternal. Moses' faithfulness was genuine but limited—he failed at Meribah ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 20#Numbers 20 . 12|Numbers 20:12]]: "Because you did not believe in me, to uphold me as holy... you shall not bring this assembly into the land"); Christ's faithfulness is perfect and complete ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 4#Hebrews 4 . 15|Hebrews 4:15]]: "one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin"). Moses sinned, disqualifying himself from entering promised land; Christ never sinned, qualifying Himself to enter God's presence and bring believers with Him. The glory comparison (v. 3) uses builder-building analogy: Moses participated in God's glory reflected; Christ possesses God's glory inherently—"the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 1#Hebrews 1 . 3|Hebrews 1:3]]). Moses' face shone from encountering God ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 34#Exodus 34 . 29|Exodus 34:29]]), glory fading; Christ's face shines eternally, glory increasing in believers ([[Readable Bible/47 - 2 Corinthians/2 Corinthians 3#2 Corinthians 3 . 18|2 Corinthians 3:18]]). The house identification (v. 6: "whose house we are") incorporates believers into Christ's household—not merely Israel (ethnic community) but church (spiritual community composed of Jews and Gentiles united in Christ). Moses ruled within Israel; Christ rules over church—"he is the head of the body, the church" ([[Readable Bible/51 - Colossians/Colossians 1#Colossians 1 . 18|Colossians 1:18]]). The conditional perseverance clause ("if we hold fast our confidence and our boasting in our hope") introduces warning theology: Moses' generation failed to enter rest through unbelief ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 3#Hebrews 3 . 19|Hebrews 3:19]]: "So we see that they were unable to enter because of unbelief")—greater danger exists in rejecting Christ than rejecting Moses. If disobeying Moses brought death ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 10#Hebrews 10 . 28|Hebrews 10:28]]: "Anyone who has set aside the law of Moses dies without mercy"), how much more rejecting Christ? The comparison honors Moses as faithful servant while establishing Christ as faithful Son—not replacement but fulfillment, not diminishment but escalation, not competing authorities but unified revelation (servant testified, Son accomplished). [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 3#Hebrews 3 . 1|Hebrews 3:1-6]] thus presents Jesus as Prophet greater than Moses (apostle sent from God), Priest greater than Aaron (high priest representing humanity), King greater than David (Son ruling over house)—threefold office culminating in Christ all OT anticipated, worthy of wholehearted devotion preventing apostasy through persevering faith.
