**Context:**
First Corinthians 5:7 appears within Paul's confrontation of sexual immorality in the Corinthian church (5:1-13). A church member was in incestuous relationship with his stepmother—sin so egregious even pagans condemned it (5:1). Yet the church was "arrogant" rather than mourning (5:2), failing to discipline the offender. Paul commands excommunication (5:2-5) then employs Passover imagery to explain sin's corporate danger: "Do you not know that a little leaven leavens the whole lump?" (5:6). Verse 7 provides theological grounding: "Cleanse out the old leaven that you may be a new lump, as you really are unleavened. For Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed." The logic: just as Israelites removed all leaven before Passover (Exodus 12:15-20), Christians must remove sin because Christ (our Passover Lamb) has already been sacrificed. The indicative ("Christ... has been sacrificed") grounds the imperative ("cleanse out"). This verse crystallizes Pauline soteriology: Christ fulfills Passover typology—His sacrificial death delivers from sin's bondage as Passover lamb's blood delivered Israel from Egyptian bondage.

**Ninefold Analysis:**

**OT Context:**
First Corinthians 5:7 presupposes detailed knowledge of Passover regulations established in Exodus 12-13. God instituted Passover on Israel's final night in Egypt: each household selected unblemished male lamb on tenth day of month, kept it until fourteenth day, slaughtered it at twilight, applied blood to doorposts and lintel (Exodus 12:3-7). The blood served as sign—when God struck Egyptian firstborn, He "passed over" houses marked with blood (12:13, 23). The meal accompanied specific instructions: roasted lamb, unleavened bread, bitter herbs, eaten in haste with loins girded for departure (12:8-11). Critically, Israel must remove all leaven (*se'or*) from houses for seven days (12:15)—eating leavened bread during this period brought excommunication: "that person shall be cut off from Israel" (12:15). The prohibition was comprehensive: "no leaven shall be found in your houses" (12:19), "unleavened bread shall be eaten for seven days; no leavened bread shall be seen with you, and no leaven shall be seen with you in all your territory" (13:7). Leaven symbolized corruption—fermented dough representing moral contamination. Passover's salvific structure: (1) lamb substitutes (dies instead of firstborn), (2) blood protects (applied to doorposts), (3) judgment passes over (death angel bypasses marked houses), (4) people exit bondage (exodus from Egypt), (5) unleavened bread symbolizes purity (no corruption in new life). Paul applies this pattern christologically: Christ is substitute Lamb whose blood protects believers from judgment, delivering them from sin's bondage to walk in moral purity (unleavened life).

**Jewish Backgrounds:**
First-century Judaism meticulously observed Passover and Unleavened Bread (merged into eight-day festival, Passover on 14th Nisan followed by seven days eating unleavened bread, 15th-21st Nisan). The Mishnah tractate Pesachim details removal of leaven: before Passover eve, households searched every corner by lamplight to remove all *chametz* (leavened products). On morning of 14th Nisan, remaining leaven was burned. The Passover Haggadah liturgy recounted exodus story, applying it existentially: "In every generation a person is obligated to regard himself as if he personally left Egypt"—making exodus contemporary, not merely historical. Philo allegorized leaven as "pride and self-elation" (*Special Laws* 2.184), interpreting unleavened bread as humility before God. Josephus (Jewish War 2.10) notes Passover drew massive crowds to Jerusalem—reminiscing corporate participation in covenant memory. Rabbinic exegesis connected leaven to *yetzer hara* (evil inclination)—moral corruption infecting soul as yeast permeates dough. The Jerusalem Talmud (Pesachim 10:1) developed "four questions" (mah nishtanah) asked by children, pedagogically transmitting Passover meaning across generations. Qumran community observed Passover according to solar calendar (not lunar), creating sectarian division over proper dating. Jewish expectation anticipated messianic Passover—final deliverance surpassing original exodus. Jesus' Last Supper (synoptic accounts place it on Passover, John's Gospel suggests day before to align Christ's crucifixion with Passover lamb slaughter) reinterpreted Passover messianically: bread as His body, wine as His blood establishing new covenant (Luke 22:19-20). Paul's use of Passover imagery in 1 Corinthians 5:7 reflects widespread Jewish-Christian understanding that Christ fulfilled Passover typology—original lamb's blood prefigured Christ's blood, original exodus from Egypt prefigured exodus from sin.

**Text Form:**
First Corinthians 5:7 employs imperative followed by indicative establishing theological pattern. The opening command *ekkatharate* ("cleanse out") uses aorist imperative—urgent, decisive action required, not gradual process. The compound verb (*ek-* "out" + *kathairō* "cleanse") intensifies meaning—thorough removal, not superficial cleaning. The object "old leaven" (*tēn palaian zymēn*) uses definite article (*tēn*)—specific leaven (sin in community), not generic leaven. The purpose clause "that you may be new lump" (*hina ēte neon phyrama*) employs subjunctive expressing purpose/result—removing old leaven enables being new lump. The comparative clause "as you really are unleavened" (*kathōs este azymoi*) introduces indicative reality—positionally, believers already are unleavened through union with Christ. The tension between imperative ("cleanse out") and indicative ("you are unleavened") creates already/not-yet dynamic: positionally sanctified (set apart in Christ), progressively sanctifying (removing sin practically). The explanatory "For" (*gar*) introduces theological grounding. The subject "Christ, our Passover lamb" (*to pascha hēmōn Christos*) uses possessive pronoun (*hēmōn*, "our")—personal appropriation, not abstract doctrine. The predicate nominative construction identifies Christ with Passover lamb directly. The verb "has been sacrificed" (*etythē*) employs aorist passive—completed action in past with ongoing effects. The passive voice implies divine agency—Father offered Son as sacrifice. The aorist tense indicates decisive, unrepeatable event—Christ sacrificed once for all (Hebrews 10:10). Grammatically, Paul moves from imperative (cleanse out leaven) to indicative (you are unleavened) to explanatory indicative (Christ sacrificed)—theological reality (Christ's finished work) grounds positional identity (believers are unleavened) which demands ethical response (remove sin).

**Hermeneutical Use:**
Paul's use of Passover typology exemplifies NT hermeneutic reading OT through christological lens. The interpretive method: (1) Identify OT institution (Passover), (2) Discern its redemptive significance (deliverance from bondage through substitutionary sacrifice), (3) Locate fulfillment in Christ (His death as ultimate Passover), (4) Apply to Christian life (remove sin because Christ already sacrificed). This hermeneutic pervades NT: John the Baptist identifies Jesus as "Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world" (John 1:29)—recognizing Christ as antitype of all sacrificial lambs. John's Gospel times Jesus' crucifixion to coincide with Passover lamb slaughter (John 19:14, 31)—emphasizing typological fulfillment. John notes that Jesus' bones weren't broken (19:36), fulfilling Passover regulation "you shall not break any of its bones" (Exodus 12:46). Peter writes "you were ransomed... with the precious blood of Christ, like that of a lamb without blemish or spot" (1 Peter 1:18-19)—applying Passover lamb's unblemished requirement to Christ. Revelation portrays Christ as "Lamb standing, as though it had been slain" (5:6)—combining sacrificial imagery with resurrection victory. The Lamb receives worship (5:8-14), opens judgments (6:1ff), leads redeemed to living waters (7:17), and weds His bride (19:7-9). Hebrews contrasts old covenant sacrifices (repeated daily) with Christ's sacrifice (offered once, 10:11-14)—superior because it permanently cleanses conscience. The hermeneutical principle: OT sacrificial system is God-ordained pedagogy teaching redemption's necessity and cost; Christ fulfills what sacrifices symbolized; believers appropriate Christ's finished work by faith, living in its reality (removing sin because reconciled to God).

**Theological Use:**
First Corinthians 5:7 establishes foundational doctrines: (1) **Substitutionary atonement**: Christ died as substitute—Passover lamb died instead of firstborn, Christ died instead of sinners. His death satisfies divine justice, averting wrath from those covered by His blood. (2) **Definitive sanctification**: "You really are unleavened"—positionally, believers are holy through union with Christ. Justification (declared righteous) includes sanctification (made holy). (3) **Progressive sanctification**: "Cleanse out old leaven"—positional holiness demands practical holiness. Christians must "work out salvation" (Philippians 2:12) that God works in them. (4) **Corporate holiness**: "Little leaven leavens whole lump" (5:6)—individual sin affects entire community. Church discipline protects corporate purity. (5) **Inaugurated eschatology**: Christ's sacrifice (past) creates new reality (present) awaiting consummation (future)—already/not-yet tension. (6) **Typological fulfillment**: OT institutions anticipate Christ; He doesn't abolish but fulfills law and prophets (Matthew 5:17). (7) **Union with Christ**: Believers participate in Christ's Passover—His sacrifice becomes theirs through faith. (8) **Ethical imperative from indicative**: What Christ accomplished (indicative) demands how Christians live (imperative)—theology shapes ethics. Systematic theology develops: Christ's death is penal (bearing penalty), substitutionary (in our place), representational (as our head), victorious (defeating sin/death/Satan); believers united to Christ receive all benefits of His work (justification, adoption, sanctification, glorification); Spirit applies Christ's finished work progressively conforming believers to Christ's image.

**Rhetorical Use:**
Paul's rhetorical strategy employs vivid imagery familiar to both Jewish and Gentile Corinthian Christians. For Jewish believers, Passover evoked foundational salvation narrative—annual observance kept exodus memory alive. Paul's identification "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" reframes Passover christologically: what they've celebrated annually finds ultimate fulfillment in Christ's once-for-all sacrifice. For Gentile converts, though lacking personal Passover experience, the principle was clear: just as leaven symbolically corrupted dough, sin corrupts community; just as Passover required leaven removal, Christian community requires sin removal. The imperative "cleanse out" uses domestic imagery—housecleaning—accessible to all. The phrase "you really are unleavened" provides encouragement: you're not becoming holy, you are holy—live accordingly. This indicative-before-imperative pattern motivates obedience from identity, not works-righteousness. Rhetorically, Paul balances warning (sin's danger) with assurance (Christ's sufficiency). The context (church discipline, 5:1-13) uses Passover imagery to justify excommunication: as leaven must be removed to observe Passover properly, impenitent sinner must be removed to preserve church purity. The concluding exhortation "Let us therefore celebrate the festival, not with the old leaven... but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth" (5:8) extends metaphor—Christian life is perpetual Passover feast, requiring continuous purity. Rhetorically, Paul transforms annual observance into daily reality: every day is Passover for those united to Christ.

**Christological Connection:**
First Corinthians 5:7 identifies Christ as ultimate Passover Lamb fulfilling Moses-led deliverance. Every element of original Passover finds escalated realization in Christ. The Passover lamb was selected on tenth day, examined four days for blemishes, then slaughtered on fourteenth ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 12#Exodus 12 . 3|Exodus 12:3, 6]]); Christ was presented at Jerusalem (triumphal entry), examined by religious leaders (trials before Sanhedrin), found without fault by Pilate ("I find no guilt in him," [[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 18#John 18 . 38|John 18:38]]), and crucified on Passover. The lamb must be unblemished ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 12#Exodus 12 . 5|Exodus 12:5]]); Christ was "without blemish or spot" ([[Readable Bible/60 - 1 Peter/1 Peter 1#1 Peter 1 . 19|1 Peter 1:19]]), "knew no sin" ([[Readable Bible/47 - 2 Corinthians/2 Corinthians 5#2 Corinthians 5 . 21|2 Corinthians 5:21]]). The lamb's blood applied to doorposts protected from death angel ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 12#Exodus 12 . 13|Exodus 12:13]]); Christ's blood protects believers from divine wrath ([[Readable Bible/45 - Romans/Romans 5#Romans 5 . 9|Romans 5:9]]: "Since... we have now been justified by his blood, much more shall we be saved by him from the wrath of God"). No bone of Passover lamb could be broken ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 12#Exodus 12 . 46|Exodus 12:46]]); soldiers didn't break Jesus' legs ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 19#John 19 . 33|John 19:33, 36]]), fulfilling typology. Passover lamb was roasted with fire ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 12#Exodus 12 . 8|Exodus 12:8]])—some interpreters see this prefiguring divine judgment's fire Christ bore. The lamb was eaten completely, nothing left for morning ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 12#Exodus 12 . 10|Exodus 12:10]])—symbolizing full appropriation; believers must receive Christ entirely, not selectively. Passover delivered from Egyptian bondage ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 12#Exodus 12 . 41|Exodus 12:41]]); Christ delivers from sin's bondage ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 8#John 8 . 36|John 8:36]]: "if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed"). Moses led Israel through Red Sea ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 14#Exodus 14 . 22|Exodus 14:22]]); Christ leads believers through baptism (death-and-resurrection) into new life ([[Readable Bible/45 - Romans/Romans 6#Romans 6 . 3|Romans 6:3-4]]). Unleavened bread symbolized putting away Egypt's corruption ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 12#Exodus 12 . 39|Exodus 12:39]]; [[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 16#Deuteronomy 16 . 3|Deuteronomy 16:3]]: "bread of affliction"); Christians put away sin's corruption ([[Readable Bible/51 - Colossians/Colossians 3#Colossians 3 . 9|Colossians 3:9]]: "put off the old self with its practices"). The escalation is systematic: Moses' Passover delivered one nation temporarily, Christ's Passover delivers all nations eternally; Moses' lamb's blood averted physical death, Christ's blood averts eternal death; Moses led to earthly Canaan, Christ leads to heavenly inheritance; Moses' deliverance required annual repetition, Christ's deliverance accomplished once for all ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 10#Hebrews 10 . 10|Hebrews 10:10]]: "we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all"). The phrase "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (*etythē*, perfect passive) emphasizes completed action with ongoing effects—sacrifice finished at Calvary but benefits continue eternally. Believers don't re-sacrifice Christ (contra sacerdotal error) but appropriate His once-for-all sacrifice through faith. The participatory language "our Passover" indicates personal appropriation—not abstract theological concept but intimate possession. As Israelites ate Passover lamb, believers spiritually feed on Christ ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 6#John 6 . 53|John 6:53-56]]). The ethical implication "cleanse out old leaven" flows from Christ's finished work—because He died removing sin's penalty (justification), believers must remove sin's presence (sanctification). The indicative "you are unleavened" grounds imperative "cleanse out leaven"—Christians don't become holy by removing sin; they remove sin because they are holy in Christ. This reflects Pauline pattern throughout: "you died" ([[Readable Bible/51 - Colossians/Colossians 3#Colossians 3 . 3|Colossians 3:3]]), therefore "put to death what is earthly" (3:5); "you were washed... sanctified... justified" ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 6#1 Corinthians 6 . 11|1 Corinthians 6:11]]), therefore honor God with bodies (6:20). Christ as Passover Lamb establishes believers' identity and empowers their ethics—united to slain-and-risen Lamb, indwelt by His Spirit, they progressively conform to His image, removing sin ("old leaven") as they walk in holiness ("unleavened bread of sincerity and truth"). The typological fulfillment doesn't negate Moses' Passover but reveals its purpose: God instituted annual commemoration pointing forward to Christ, training Israel to recognize Lamb of God when He appeared, and providing interpretive framework for understanding His atoning death. Every Passover celebration anticipated Christ; every element symbolized aspect of His redemptive work; every generation's participation testified to need for substitutionary sacrifice only Christ could ultimately provide.
