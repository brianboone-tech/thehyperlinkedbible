**Context:**
Acts 3:22-26 records Peter's sermon following the healing of the lame man at the temple gate, applying Deuteronomy 18:15-19 (Moses' prophecy of coming prophet) directly to Jesus. This passage constitutes one of Scripture's clearest identifications of Christ as the Prophet like Moses whom God promised to raise up. Peter quotes Moses verbatim: "The Lord God will raise up for you a prophet like me from your brothers. You shall listen to him in whatever he tells you. And it shall be that every soul who does not listen to that prophet shall be destroyed from the people" (vv. 22-23). Peter then connects this to covenant promises to Abraham (v. 25) and explains that God sent Jesus first to Israel "to bless you by turning every one of you from your wickedness" (v. 26). The sermon's logic: Moses predicted a Prophet; all subsequent prophets confirmed this; Jesus is that Prophet; rejecting Him brings covenant curse; accepting Him brings covenant blessing. This apostolic preaching establishes Jesus' messianic credentials through Mosaic prophecy, urging Israel to recognize their Messiah and repent.

**Ninefold Analysis:**

**OT Context:**
Acts 3:22-26 quotes and interprets Deuteronomy 18:15-19, Moses' prophetic promise delivered in his final sermons before Israel entered Canaan. The Deuteronomy context establishes prophetic office as God's authorized means of revelation, contrasting true prophecy with pagan divination (18:9-14). Israel requested mediator at Horeb because they feared hearing God's voice directly (Deuteronomy 5:23-27; Exodus 20:18-21)—God approved this request and promised to raise up prophets from among their brothers to speak His words. The phrase "prophet like me" (*kāmônî*) creates comparison with Moses who received God's words directly ("I will put my words in his mouth," 18:18) and mediated covenant between God and people. The warning "whoever will not listen... I myself will require it of him" (18:19) attaches covenant curse to prophetic rejection—disobeying prophet equals disobeying God. Throughout OT history, prophets arose (Samuel, Elijah, Isaiah, Jeremiah, etc.) partially fulfilling this promise, yet Deuteronomy 34:10 concluded "there has not arisen a prophet since in Israel like Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face"—indicating awaited fulfillment in eschatological Prophet. Peter identifies Jesus as that Prophet, citing Moses' words as messianic prophecy. The connection to Abrahamic covenant (Acts 3:25, quoting Genesis 12:3; 22:18) links Moses to Abraham, showing covenant continuity: God's promise to Abraham ("in your offspring all families blessed") finds fulfillment in the Prophet whom Moses predicted. The unified message: Abraham's seed and Moses' prophet converge in Christ.

**Jewish Backgrounds:**
First-century Jewish interpretation debated whether Deuteronomy 18:15 predicted one eschatological Prophet or succession of prophets. The Qumran community (4QTestimonia) cited Deuteronomy 18:18-19 as messianic proof-text, expecting "the Prophet" alongside priestly and royal messiahs. Josephus (Antiquities 4.165) interpreted historically—Moses predicted prophetic succession guiding Israel. Rabbinic tradition emphasized Moses' uniqueness: Deuteronomy Rabbah 9:9 taught that though many prophets arose, none equaled Moses, thus Deuteronomy 34:10 remained true. Yet rabbis also expected eschatological prophet (Elijah) heralding Messiah's coming (Malachi 4:5-6). The Samaritans especially emphasized Deuteronomy 18:15-18, expecting "Taheb" (Restorer), prophet like Moses who would restore true worship and reveal hidden sacred vessels. Jewish sectarian groups asked John the Baptist "Are you the Prophet?" (John 1:21), distinct from asking if he was Elijah or the Christ—showing expectation of specific prophetic figure based on Deuteronomy 18. When Jesus multiplied bread, crowds declared "This is indeed the Prophet who is to come into the world" (John 6:14), connecting Moses (manna-provider) with prophesied Prophet (bread-multiplier). Samaritan woman's expectation—"I know that Messiah is coming... he will tell us all things" (John 4:25)—reflects Deuteronomy 18:18 ("I will put my words in his mouth, and he shall speak to them all that I command him"). Peter's application of Deuteronomy 18 to Jesus (Acts 3:22-23) entered Jewish-Christian debate: Christians affirmed Jesus fulfilled this prophecy; traditional Jews denied, awaiting future fulfillment. Peter's sermon strategy appeals to Moses' authority—if Moses is accepted as authoritative prophet (which all Jews affirm), then his prediction of greater Prophet must be heeded.

**Text Form:**
Peter's quotation follows LXX (Septuagint) closely with minor modifications. Verse 22 begins "Moses said" (*Mōusēs men eipen*), attributing prophecy directly to Moses, establishing biblical authority. The future tense "will raise up" (*anastēsei*) uses same verb root as "resurrection" (*anastasis*)—subtle wordplay hinting that promised Prophet comes through resurrection. The phrase "from your brothers" (*ek tōn adelphōn hymōn*) emphasizes Messiah's Jewish identity—not foreign deliverer but fellow Israelite, as Moses was. The comparison "like me" (*hōs eme*) creates Moses-Prophet parallel in function, authority, and covenant role. The emphatic command "You shall listen to him" (*autou akousete*) uses emphatic pronoun (*autou*, "to him")—exclusive obedience required. The phrase "in whatever he tells you" (*kata panta hosa an lalēsē pros hymas*) indicates comprehensive authority—not selective obedience but total submission to Prophet's words. Verse 23's warning employs strong language: "every soul" (*pasa psychē*) indicates individual accountability without exception; "who does not listen" (*hētis ean mē akousē*) uses indefinite relative clause covering all non-listeners; "shall be destroyed" (*exolethreuthēsetai*) employs future passive of compound verb (*ex-* "out" + *olethros* "destruction")—complete removal from covenant community. The phrase "from the people" (*ek tou laou*) indicates covenant excommunication—rejection of Prophet results in rejection from God's people. Verse 24 broadens perspective: "all the prophets from Samuel and those who came after" (*pantes de hoi prophētai apo Samouēl kai tōn kathexēs*) indicates prophetic consensus—every prophet pointed to this day. Verse 25's "sons of the prophets and of the covenant" (*hymeis este hoi huioi tōn prophētōn kai tēs diathēkēs*) identifies audience as covenant heirs responsible for accepting covenant fulfillment. The quotation "In your offspring shall all the families of the earth be blessed" (*en tō spermati sou eneulogēthēsontai pasai hai patriai tēs gēs*) links Moses to Abraham, showing covenant unity. Verse 26's conclusion "God, having raised up his Servant" (*hymin prōton anastēsas ho theos ton paida autou*) uses *anastēsas* (same root as v. 22's "raise up")—Jesus is the raised-up Prophet accomplishing blessing by turning people from wickedness.

**Hermeneutical Use:**
Peter's hermeneutic establishes apostolic method for reading OT christologically. The interpretive moves: (1) Identify OT prophecy (Deuteronomy 18:15-19), (2) Show its fulfillment in Jesus, (3) Apply warning/promise to current audience, (4) Connect to broader covenant framework (Abrahamic promises). This hermeneutic pervades NT: Stephen repeats Peter's argument (Acts 7:37), identifying Jesus as Prophet like Moses whom Israel rejected (just as they rejected Moses initially, 7:35). John's Gospel repeatedly presents Jesus as fulfillment: "Moses... wrote of me" (John 5:46), "We have found him of whom Moses in the Law and also the prophets wrote" (John 1:45). The Transfiguration visually confirms this identification: voice from heaven commands "Listen to him!" (Mark 9:7)—echoing Deuteronomy 18:15 ("to him you shall listen"), with Moses and Elijah present yielding authority to Jesus. Hebrews 1:1-2 establishes hermeneutical framework: "God spoke to our fathers by the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son"—climactic revelation superseding preparatory revelation. Hebrews 3:1-6 develops Moses-Christ comparison: both "faithful," but Moses as "servant" in God's house testifying to future things, Christ as "Son" over God's house accomplishing those things. The hermeneutical principle: OT prophets (including Moses) spoke of Christ; their words find ultimate fulfillment in Him; rejecting Christ is rejecting God's final word; accepting Christ is entering covenant blessings promised to Abraham. This hermeneutic counters supersessionism (church replacing Israel) and Marcionism (OT vs NT deity)—instead affirming covenant continuity: same God, same promises, progressive fulfillment climaxing in Christ.

**Theological Use:**
Acts 3:22-26 establishes essential doctrines: (1) **Progressive revelation**: God spoke through prophets progressively, climaxing in Christ who is final Prophet (Hebrews 1:1-2). Each prophet pointed forward; Christ fulfills completely. (2) **Christological messianism**: Jesus is Prophet predicted by Moses, Servant sent to Israel (v. 26), Seed of Abraham blessing nations (v. 25)—multiple OT trajectories converge in Him. (3) **Covenant theology**: Abrahamic and Mosaic covenants aren't separate but unified, both fulfilled in Christ who mediates new covenant. (4) **Soteriological urgency**: Rejecting Prophet brings covenant curse ("destroyed from the people"), accepting Him brings blessing ("turn from wickedness"). Salvation requires hearing and obeying Christ. (5) **Ecclesiological continuity**: Church is "sons of the prophets and of the covenant" (v. 25)—Gentile believers grafted into Israel's olive tree (Romans 11:17-24), inheriting Abrahamic promises through faith in Christ. (6) **Missional priority**: Jesus sent "first to you" (Israel, v. 26) then to nations—"to the Jew first and also to the Greek" (Romans 1:16) reflects God's covenant order. (7) **Ethical transformation**: Christ blesses "by turning every one of you from your wickedness" (v. 26)—salvation isn't merely forensic (declared righteous) but transformative (made righteous through Spirit's work). (8) **Eschatological judgment**: The warning "destroyed from the people" anticipates final judgment where Christ-rejecters face eternal separation from God (2 Thessalonians 1:8-9). Systematic theology integrates: Christ as Prophet reveals God perfectly; as Priest atones for sin; as King rules eternally; rejecting this threefold mediator forfeits salvation; accepting Him secures eternal blessing.

**Rhetorical Use:**
Peter's rhetorical strategy appeals to Jewish audience's biblical literacy and covenant identity. Opening with "Moses said" (v. 22) invokes Moses' unquestioned authority—all Jews revere Moses, so his testimony carries weight. Quoting Deuteronomy verbatim establishes biblical grounding, not mere human opinion. The comparison "like me" flatters Moses while elevating Jesus—not diminishing Moses but showing God's pattern of raising greater deliverer. The command "You shall listen to him" creates urgency—disobeying isn't optional. The warning "destroyed from the people" (v. 23) evokes covenant fear—being cut off from God's people is ultimate tragedy for Jews whose identity derives from covenant membership. Peter then broadens appeal: "all the prophets from Samuel" (v. 24) indicates unanimous prophetic witness—not isolated text but scriptural consensus. The phrase "you are sons of the prophets and of the covenant" (v. 25) affirms audience's identity while creating responsibility—as covenant heirs, they must accept covenant fulfillment. Quoting Abraham ("in your offspring all families blessed") connects Moses to patriarchal promises, showing covenant unity. The phrase "to you first" (v. 26) honors Israel's priority—God sent Jesus to His own people before extending blessing to Gentiles, maintaining covenant order. The final phrase "turning every one of you from your wickedness" offers hope—repentance brings blessing, restoration is possible. Rhetorically, Peter balances honor (you're covenant sons), warning (rejection brings destruction), and hope (turn and receive blessing). The sermon's placement after miracle (healing lame man, 3:1-10) provides visual proof validating verbal claim—as Moses performed signs authenticating his mission (Exodus 4:1-9), Jesus (through apostles) performs signs authenticating His messiahship.

**Christological Connection:**
[[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 3#Acts 3 . 22|Acts 3:22-26]] identifies Jesus as the Prophet like Moses whom God raised up to mediate final covenant. Every aspect of Moses' ministry prefigures Christ's superior ministry. Moses was raised up from among Israelites ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 2#Exodus 2 . 10|Exodus 2:10]]), sharing ethnic identity with those he delivered; Christ was "born of woman, born under the law" ([[Readable Bible/48 - Galatians/Galatians 4#Galatians 4 . 4|Galatians 4:4]]), true Israelite representing His people. Moses spoke God's words received at Sinai ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 19#Exodus 19 . 3|Exodus 19:3-6]]; [[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 5#Deuteronomy 5 . 4|Deuteronomy 5:4-5]]); Christ speaks Father's words perfectly ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 8#John 8 . 28|John 8:28]]: "I do nothing on my own authority, but speak just as the Father taught me"). Moses mediated old covenant at Sinai ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 24#Exodus 24 . 7|Exodus 24:7-8]]); Christ mediates new covenant through His blood ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 9#Hebrews 9 . 15|Hebrews 9:15]]: "Therefore he is the mediator of a new covenant"). Moses performed signs validating divine commission ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 4#Exodus 4 . 30|Exodus 4:30-31]]); Jesus performed greater signs proving messianic identity ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 10#John 10 . 37|John 10:37-38]]: "If I am not doing the works of my Father, then do not believe me; but if I do them, even though you do not believe me, believe the works"). Moses interceded for Israel's sins, offering himself as substitute ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 32#Exodus 32 . 32|Exodus 32:32]]); Christ actually became sin offering ([[Readable Bible/47 - 2 Corinthians/2 Corinthians 5#2 Corinthians 5 . 21|2 Corinthians 5:21]]: "For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin"). Moses led Israel from Egyptian bondage to promised land; Christ leads believers from sin's bondage to eternal inheritance. Yet Christ's superiority is explicit: [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 3#Hebrews 3 . 3|Hebrews 3:3]] declares "Jesus has been counted worthy of more glory than Moses—as much more glory as the builder of a house has more honor than the house itself." Moses was faithful servant within God's house; Christ is faithful Son over God's house ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 3#Hebrews 3 . 5|Hebrews 3:5-6]]). Moses' ministry brought death (law condemns sin, [[Readable Bible/47 - 2 Corinthians/2 Corinthians 3#2 Corinthians 3 . 7|2 Corinthians 3:7]]); Christ's ministry brings life (Spirit gives life, [[Readable Bible/47 - 2 Corinthians/2 Corinthians 3#2 Corinthians 3 . 6|2 Corinthians 3:6]]). Moses' glory faded ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 34#Exodus 34 . 29|Exodus 34:29-35]]); Christ's glory is eternal and increasing ([[Readable Bible/47 - 2 Corinthians/2 Corinthians 3#2 Corinthians 3 . 18|2 Corinthians 3:18]]: "we all... are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another"). The warning "destroyed from the people" ([[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 3#Acts 3 . 23|Acts 3:23]]) intensifies in Christ's context: if disobeying Moses brought physical death ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 10#Hebrews 10 . 28|Hebrews 10:28]]: "Anyone who has set aside the law of Moses dies without mercy"), how much more does rejecting Christ bring eternal destruction ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 10#Hebrews 10 . 29|Hebrews 10:29]]: "How much worse punishment, do you think, will be deserved by the one who has trampled underfoot the Son of God")? The Abrahamic connection ([[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 3#Acts 3 . 25|Acts 3:25]]) identifies Christ as "seed" through whom blessing comes: Paul explains "Now the promises were made to Abraham and to his offspring. It does not say, 'And to offsprings,' referring to many, but referring to one, 'And to your offspring,' who is Christ" ([[Readable Bible/48 - Galatians/Galatians 3#Galatians 3 . 16|Galatians 3:16]]). Christ is singular Seed fulfilling promise that "in your offspring all families blessed"—Gentiles blessed through faith in Christ inherit Abrahamic covenant ([[Readable Bible/48 - Galatians/Galatians 3#Galatians 3 . 29|Galatians 3:29]]: "if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's offspring, heirs according to promise"). The phrase "turning every one from wickedness" ([[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 3#Acts 3 . 26|Acts 3:26]]) describes Christ's saving work: He doesn't merely forgive past sins but transforms sinners into saints through Spirit's indwelling ([[Readable Bible/56 - Titus/Titus 2#Titus 2 . 14|Titus 2:14]]: Christ "gave himself for us to redeem us from all lawlessness and to purify for himself a people for his own possession who are zealous for good works"). The prophetic consensus "from Samuel and those who came after" ([[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 3#Acts 3 . 24|Acts 3:24]]) demonstrates OT's unified testimony to Christ: Samuel anointed David whose throne Christ inherits forever ([[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 1#Luke 1 . 32|Luke 1:32-33]]); Isaiah prophesied virgin birth and Suffering Servant; Jeremiah predicted new covenant; Ezekiel envisioned new temple (Christ's body, [[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 2#John 2 . 21|John 2:21]]); Daniel foresaw Son of Man's eternal kingdom—all converging on Christ. Peter's identification of Jesus as the Prophet establishes His messianic credentials: Moses himself predicted Him, Abraham's covenant promises find fulfillment in Him, all prophets testified of Him, His works validate His claims, and rejecting Him brings covenant curse while accepting Him brings covenant blessing—turning people from wickedness to righteousness through His atoning death, powerful resurrection, and Spirit's transforming work.
