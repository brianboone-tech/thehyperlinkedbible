**Context:**
Exodus 3:7-10 records God's self-revelation to Moses at the burning bush, commissioning him as deliverer of Israel from Egyptian bondage. These verses form the theological heart of the burning bush encounter, revealing God's covenant character through threefold repetition: "I have surely seen... I have heard... I know" (vv. 7-9). God's seeing, hearing, and knowing aren't passive observations but covenant commitments—He sees Israel's affliction, hears their cry, knows their sufferings, and therefore acts decisively. Verse 8 announces divine descent: "I have come down to deliver them out of the hand of the Egyptians and to bring them up out of that land to a good and broad land." The dual movement (down to deliver, up to promised land) establishes exodus pattern. Verse 10's commission—"Come, I will send you to Pharaoh that you may bring my people, the children of Israel, out of Egypt"—unites divine action with human agency: God delivers through Moses. This passage establishes Moses as God's mediatorial instrument, prefiguring Christ who supremely embodies God's seeing human misery, hearing human cry, knowing human suffering, descending to deliver, and ascending to bring believers into heavenly rest.

**Ninefold Analysis:**

**OT Context:**
Exodus 3:7-10 follows Moses' forty-year exile in Midian after killing the Egyptian (Exodus 2:11-15). The burning bush theophany (vv. 1-6) establishes God's holiness ("Do not come near; take your sandals off your feet, for the place on which you are standing is holy ground," v. 5) and covenant identity ("I am the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob," v. 6). Verses 7-10 shift from self-identification to mission announcement. The threefold divine perception—"I have surely seen" (*rā'ōh rā'îṯî*, intensive infinitive absolute emphasizing certainty), "I have heard their cry," "I know their sufferings"—demonstrates covenant attentiveness. God didn't merely observe but engaged compassionately. The phrase "cry because of their taskmasters" (*ṣə'āqāṯām mippənê nōḡəśāyw*, v. 7) connects to Exodus 2:23 where Israel's groaning ascended to God. The divine response (v. 8) employs verbs of movement: "I have come down" (*wā'ēreḏ*) and "to bring them up" (*ûləha'ălōṯô*). The "coming down" (*yāraḏ*) of God is technical term for theophanic intervention (Genesis 11:5, 7; 18:21), indicating direct divine action in history. The destination—"a good and broad land, a land flowing with milk and honey" (*'ereṣ ṭôḇâ ûrəḥāḇâ 'el-'ereṣ zāḇaṯ ḥālāḇ ûḏəḇāš*, v. 8)—fulfills Abrahamic promises (Genesis 15:18-21). The list of displaced nations (Canaanites, Hittites, etc.) anticipates conquest. Verse 10's commission—"Come now, and I will send you to Pharaoh" (*wə'aṯṯâ ləḵâ wə'ešlāḥăḵā*, literally "Come now, and I will send you")—creates divine-human partnership: God acts through Moses. Moses' objection ("Who am I that I should go to Pharaoh?" v. 11) and God's assurance ("But I will be with you," v. 12) establish pattern of inadequate human instruments empowered by divine presence—recurring throughout Scripture (Judges 6:12-16; Jeremiah 1:6-8).

**Jewish Backgrounds:**
Jewish tradition developed rich theology around Exodus 3:7-10. The Targums emphasize God's covenantal memory: Targum Onkelos renders "I have surely seen" as "the oppression of my people has been surely revealed before me," emphasizing God's omniscience and covenant loyalty. Targum Neofiti expands verse 8: "My Memra has revealed itself to deliver them," identifying God's *Memra* (Word) as active agent of deliverance—later Christian interpreters saw this as prefiguring Christ as divine Word (John 1:1, 14). Rabbinic midrash (Exodus Rabbah 3:1) teaches that God's descent wasn't literal spatial movement but condescension to human need—God "lowered Himself" to engage with suffering humanity. The Mekhilta (early rabbinic commentary on Exodus) interprets "I have come down to deliver" as God's commitment to personal involvement, not delegation to angels—establishing principle that redemption requires God Himself, not intermediaries. Philo (Life of Moses 1.59-62) allegorizes the passage: God's "seeing" represents divine providence, "hearing" represents divine responsiveness to prayer, "knowing" represents divine wisdom penetrating all things. The descent signifies divine immanence entering material realm. Josephus (Antiquities 2.264-276) recounts the burning bush encounter historically but emphasizes Moses' reluctance, requiring divine signs (staff to serpent, leprous hand) to convince him. The rabbis debated why God chose Moses—some emphasized his compassion (he turned aside to see the bush, Exodus 3:3, showing care for details), others his humility (keeping Jethro's sheep in wilderness, away from inhabited areas to avoid trespassing). The Jewish liturgical tradition connects Exodus 3:7-10 to God's ongoing redemptive pattern: as He heard Israel's cry in Egypt, so He hears prayers of Israel in exile, anticipating future redemption through Messiah.

**Text Form:**
The passage employs covenant formula language and parallel structures. The threefold perception creates emphatic parallelism: "I have seen (*rā'îṯî*)... I have heard (*šāma'tî*)... I know (*yāḏa'tî*)" (vv. 7, 9). The verbs escalate from external perception (seeing, hearing) to internal empathy (knowing experientially). The intensive infinitive absolute construction "I have surely seen" (*rā'ōh rā'îṯî*) doubles the verb root for emphasis—used elsewhere for divine certainty (Genesis 18:18; Jeremiah 32:4). Verse 8's structure balances deliverance from bondage with deliverance to blessing: "to deliver them (*ləhaṣṣîlô*) out of the hand of the Egyptians and to bring them up (*ûləha'ălōṯô*) out of that land to a good and broad land." The prepositions mark spatial and experiential movement: *min* ("from, out of") indicates source (Egypt, oppression), *'el* ("to, toward") indicates destination (promised land). The description "land flowing with milk and honey" (*'ereṣ zāḇaṯ ḥālāḇ ûḏəḇāš*) uses participial form (*zāḇaṯ*, "flowing") suggesting continuous abundance—not mere static possession but dynamic provision. The list of six nations to be displaced (v. 8) employs anaphoric "and" (*wə-*) creating rhythmic litany emphasizing completeness of conquest. Verse 9's resumptive repetition ("now behold, the cry of the people of Israel has come to me, and I have also seen the oppression") reprises verse 7, creating inclusio bracket around verse 8's divine intervention announcement. Verse 10's commission uses imperative (*ləḵâ*, "go, come") followed by cohortative (*wə'ešlāḥăḵā*, "and I will send you"), uniting divine command with divine enablement. The purpose clause "that you may bring my people, the children of Israel, out of Egypt" (*wəhôṣē' 'eṯ-'ammî ḇənê-yiśrā'ēl mimmiṣrāyim*) employs causative Hiphil stem (*hôṣē'*, "cause to go out"), indicating Moses as instrumental cause while God remains ultimate cause of exodus.

**Hermeneutical Use:**
The NT interprets Exodus 3:7-10 as establishing God's redemptive pattern fulfilled in Christ. Acts 7:30-34 (Stephen's sermon) quotes this passage directly, connecting Moses' commissioning to Jesus' mission. Stephen's point: as God sent Moses to deliver Israel from physical bondage, so God sent Jesus to deliver humanity from spiritual bondage. The parallel "I have come down to deliver them" (Exodus 3:8) and "I have come down from heaven" (John 6:38) establishes typology: Moses as sent deliverer prefigures Christ as sent Savior. The Incarnation theology draws from God's "descent" language—Philippians 2:6-8 echoes exodus pattern: Christ "emptied himself, taking the form of a servant... he humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death." The divine descent to deliver oppressed parallels Christ's descent to save sinners. Hebrews 2:14-18 develops the "knowing their sufferings" motif: "Since therefore the children share in flesh and blood, he himself likewise partook of the same things, that through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death... Therefore he had to be made like his brothers in every respect, so that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest... For because he himself has suffered when tempted, he is able to help those who are being tempted." Christ's incarnational solidarity with human suffering fulfills what Moses' identification with Israel's suffering typologically anticipated. The "I will send you" commissioning (Exodus 3:10) establishes mediatorial pattern: as God sent Moses to Pharaoh, God sent Christ to the world (John 3:17; 17:18). The hermeneutical principle: OT deliverance narratives are redemptive-historical types—physical exodus from Egypt prefigures spiritual exodus from sin; Moses as mediator prefigures Christ as ultimate mediator; promised land rest prefigures eternal Sabbath rest (Hebrews 4:1-11).

**Theological Use:**
This passage establishes fundamental doctrines: (1) **Divine compassion**: God's seeing, hearing, knowing aren't abstract attributes but covenant commitments—He engages empathetically with human suffering. This counters deism's distant deity; biblical God intervenes personally in history. (2) **Divine initiative**: Israel didn't summon God; God chose to act. "I have come down" demonstrates grace preceding human merit or effort—election and redemption originate in divine will, not human worthiness. (3) **Divine sovereignty in redemption**: God announces deliverance as accomplished certainty ("I have come down") before Moses acts, establishing that salvation's guarantee rests on divine promise, not human performance. (4) **Mediatorial principle**: Though God could deliver directly, He chooses to work through human agents (Moses)—establishing pattern of incarnational ministry where divine power works through human weakness. (5) **Covenant faithfulness**: God's action fulfills promises to Abraham (Genesis 15:13-14; 46:4), demonstrating that God remembers and keeps covenant across generations. (6) **Mission theology**: "I will send you" establishes God as sending God—Father sends Son (John 20:21), Father and Son send Spirit (John 14:26; 16:7), God sends church (Matthew 28:19-20). Mission flows from God's nature as sending God. (7) **Substitutionary representation**: Moses goes where Israel cannot—confronting Pharaoh, mediating with God—prefiguring Christ's substitutionary work doing for us what we cannot do for ourselves. Systematic theology develops: God's attributes (omniscience, compassion, sovereignty) ground His redemptive works (election, incarnation, atonement, resurrection).

**Rhetorical Use:**
The passage's rhetorical power lies in emphatic repetition and escalating urgency. The threefold "I have seen... I have heard... I know" creates crescendo of divine attention, assuring Israel (and readers) that God hasn't forgotten them. The intensive infinitive absolute "I have surely seen" (*rā'ōh rā'îṯî*) adds certainty—no doubt about God's awareness or commitment. The shift from third person description of Israel's suffering (v. 7) to second person address to Moses (v. 10) creates immediacy: "Come now, and I will send you." The rhetorical effect invites readers into the conversation—God's call to Moses becomes God's call to all servants. The description "good and broad land, flowing with milk and honey" employs sensory language evoking abundance and satisfaction—stark contrast to Egypt's oppression. The "good" (*ṭôḇâ*) echoes Eden ("it was very good," Genesis 1:31), suggesting exodus as re-creation, return to pre-Fall abundance. The "broad" (*rəḥāḇâ*) contrasts with Egypt's confinement, evoking freedom and space. The phrase "flowing with milk and honey" uses hyperbole for persuasive effect—milk (animal husbandry) and honey (wild abundance) together signify complete provision. The list of displaced nations (v. 8) creates sense of obstacles to be overcome, building narrative tension—how will God accomplish this? Moses' commission (v. 10) introduces protagonist who will enact divine will. The rhetorical structure invites trust: if God sees, hears, knows, and acts, His people can confidently rely on His deliverance. The passage's placement (before plagues, Red Sea crossing, wilderness wandering) functions as promissory note—whatever trials come, God's commitment ("I have come down to deliver") guarantees success. Rhetorically, it establishes theology of assured hope: God's promises cannot fail because they're grounded in His character and power, not human ability.

**Christological Connection:**
[[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 3#Exodus 3 . 7|Exodus 3:7-10]] prefigures Christ's redemptive mission in every detail. God's threefold perception—"I have seen... I have heard... I know"—finds ultimate fulfillment in the Incarnation where God doesn't merely observe human misery from distance but enters it personally. "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 1#John 1 . 14|John 1:14]]) embodies God's descent to know experientially what humanity suffers. Christ's empathy flows from incarnational solidarity: "For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 4#Hebrews 4 . 15|Hebrews 4:15]]). Where God "came down" to deliver Israel from Egyptian bondage, Christ "came down from heaven" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 6#John 6 . 38|John 6:38]]) to deliver humanity from sin's bondage—physical exodus typifies spiritual exodus. [[Readable Bible/50 - Philippians/Philippians 2#Philippians 2 . 6|Philippians 2:6-8]]'s descent language directly parallels [[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 3#Exodus 3 . 8|Exodus 3:8]]: "though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men. And being found in human form, he humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross." Christ's descent surpasses God's Exodus descent—not merely coming to deliver but becoming like those needing deliverance, experiencing their suffering unto death. The dual movement "down to deliver, up to bring them" in [[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 3#Exodus 3 . 8|Exodus 3:8]] prefigures Christ's death and resurrection: He descended into death ([[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 4#Ephesians 4 . 9|Ephesians 4:9]]), then ascended ([[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 4#Ephesians 4 . 10|Ephesians 4:10]]), bringing believers with Him into heavenly places ([[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 2#Ephesians 2 . 6|Ephesians 2:6]]). Where Moses received commission "I will send you to Pharaoh," Jesus received mission "As you sent me into the world, so I have sent them into the world" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 17#John 17 . 18|John 17:18]]). The "sending" motif pervades John's Gospel—"God sent his Son" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 3#John 3 . 17|John 3:17]]; [[Readable Bible/62 - 1 John/1 John 4#1 John 4 . 9|1 John 4:9-10]]) establishes Christ as ultimate sent deliverer whom Moses typologically anticipated. The destination "good and broad land, flowing with milk and honey" prefigures "new heavens and new earth in which righteousness dwells" ([[Readable Bible/61 - 2 Peter/2 Peter 3#2 Peter 3 . 13|2 Peter 3:13]])—eschatological rest and abundance surpassing Canaan's temporal blessings. [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 4#Hebrews 4 . 8|Hebrews 4:8-11]] explicitly contrasts Joshua's (Moses' successor) incomplete rest with Christ's perfect rest: "So then, there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God, for whoever has entered God's rest has also rested from his works as God did from his. Let us therefore strive to enter that rest." The exodus deliverance from Egypt to Canaan typifies final deliverance from sin to eternal life. Christ's superiority to Moses emerges at every point: Moses heard God's call and reluctantly obeyed; Christ eternally existed in divine counsel planning redemption ([[Readable Bible/60 - 1 Peter/1 Peter 1#1 Peter 1 . 20|1 Peter 1:20]]). Moses delivered one nation from physical bondage; Christ delivers "people from every tribe and language and people and nation" ([[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 5#Revelation 5 . 9|Revelation 5:9]]) from spiritual bondage. Moses brought Israel through Red Sea to temporary land; Christ brings believers through baptismal death-and-resurrection to eternal inheritance ([[Readable Bible/60 - 1 Peter/1 Peter 1#1 Peter 1 . 3|1 Peter 1:3-5]]). Moses' mediation was external and temporary; Christ's mediation is internal ("I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts," [[Readable Bible/24 - Jeremiah/Jeremiah 31#Jeremiah 31 . 33|Jeremiah 31:33]]) and eternal ("he holds his priesthood permanently," [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 7#Hebrews 7 . 24|Hebrews 7:24]]). The exodus pattern—God sees affliction, descends to deliver, sends mediator, brings people out to bring them in—finds complete, final, perfect fulfillment in Christ who saw our sin's misery, descended in incarnation, accomplished deliverance through cross and resurrection, and brings believers from death to life, from bondage to freedom, from exile to home eternal in the heavens.
