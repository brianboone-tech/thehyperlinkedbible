**Context:**
Luke 9:31 occurs within the Transfiguration narrative (Luke 9:28-36), where Moses and Elijah appear in glory with Jesus and discuss His coming "exodus" (*exodos*) to be accomplished at Jerusalem. This single verse crystallizes Luke's theological vision: Jesus' death and resurrection constitute the true Exodus, fulfilling what Moses' deliverance from Egypt typologically prefigured. The term *exodos* is linguistically and theologically loaded—referring literally to "way out, departure, death" but unmistakably evoking Israel's paradigmatic redemption from Egyptian bondage. Moses, who led the first exodus, appears to discuss the final exodus with Jesus, the greater Moses who will deliver not from Pharaoh but from sin and death. Luke's Gospel presents Jesus' journey to Jerusalem as new exodus journey (9:51: "he set his face to go to Jerusalem"), and this Transfiguration scene previews the glory awaiting beyond crucifixion's suffering.

**Ninefold Analysis:**

**OT Context:**
Luke 9:31's use of *exodos* deliberately evokes the Exodus narrative, Israel's foundational redemption event. The original exodus (Exodus 1-15) established God's pattern for deliverance: (1) oppressed people cry out, (2) God hears and remembers covenant, (3) God sends deliverer (Moses), (4) deliverer confronts oppressor through signs/plagues, (5) final plague (Passover) requires sacrificial blood for protection, (6) people exit bondage through water (Red Sea), (7) oppressor destroyed, (8) delivered people journey toward promised land, (9) covenant established at Sinai making them God's treasured possession. This pattern shapes all subsequent biblical redemption theology. The prophets reinterpreted Israel's Babylonian exile and return as "new exodus"—Isaiah 40-55 saturates text with exodus imagery: "In the wilderness prepare the way of the LORD" (40:3), "I am doing a new thing... I will make a way in the wilderness and rivers in the desert" (43:19), "For you shall not go out in haste, and you shall not go in flight, for the LORD will go before you, and the God of Israel will be your rear guard" (52:12). These passages reapply exodus language to post-exilic restoration, yet they await fuller fulfillment—the prophetic hope for ultimate exodus when God definitively defeats evil and gathers His people. Luke identifies Jesus' death-resurrection as that ultimate exodus: as Moses led Israel through Red Sea waters from slavery to freedom, Jesus leads believers through death-resurrection waters from sin's bondage to eternal life. The Passover connection is central: as lamb's blood protected Israel from death angel (Exodus 12), Christ's blood as "Lamb of God" (John 1:29) protects believers from divine judgment. Luke carefully times Jesus' death during Passover (Luke 22:1, 7-8), making typological fulfillment explicit.

**Jewish Backgrounds:**
First-century Judaism viewed the Exodus as paradigmatic redemption, expecting Messiah to lead "second exodus" surpassing the first. The Targums interpreted prophetic "new exodus" texts messianically. Targum Isaiah 40:9 adds "the kingdom of your God is revealed"—connecting exodus to kingdom inauguration. The Dead Sea Scrolls understood their desert community as reliving exodus: the Damascus Document (CD 1:3-11) portrays sectarians as new wilderness generation awaiting final deliverance. The Qumran War Scroll envisions eschatological battle where God defeats evil as He defeated Egypt. Jewish liturgy commemorated exodus daily (Mishnah Berachot 1:5 mandates reciting Exodus passage morning and evening), keeping redemptive memory alive and fostering hope for future deliverance. Philo allegorized exodus as soul's journey from material bondage (Egypt) to spiritual freedom (Canaan), with Moses representing reason guiding passion-enslaved humanity toward virtue. Rabbinic tradition developed concept of four redemptions: Egypt, Babylon, Greece, Rome—anticipating final messianic redemption surpassing all previous deliverances. The Passover Haggadah declared "In every generation a person is obligated to regard himself as if he personally left Egypt"—making exodus existentially present, not merely historical memory. This context illuminates Luke 9:31: Moses discussing Jesus' *exodos* signals that Moses' original deliverance was always pointing toward greater fulfillment in Messiah. Jewish hope for new exodus finds answer in Christ's death-resurrection accomplishing ultimate liberation.

**Text Form:**
Luke 9:31 employs carefully chosen vocabulary signaling theological depth. The opening *elegon* ("they were speaking") uses imperfect tense indicating continuous conversation—Moses and Elijah didn't merely mention but discussed extensively Jesus' coming passion. The phrase "who appeared in glory" (*ophthentes en doxē*) employs passive participle—God manifested them in glory, not self-generated appearance. The "glory" (*doxa*) recalls Sinai theophany where Moses couldn't see God's face but saw His glory (Exodus 33:18-23)—now Moses himself appears in glory previewing resurrection bodies. The central term *exodos* carries triple meaning: (1) literal departure/death, (2) linguistic connection to book of Exodus, (3) theological fulfillment of exodus redemption pattern. Luke's choice of *exodos* instead of common death terms (*thanatos*, "death"; *teleute*, "end"; *apokalypsis*, "revelation") is deliberate—interpreting Jesus' death within salvation-historical framework. The verb *emellen* ("was about to") indicates divine necessity—Jesus' exodus isn't accident but divinely ordained plan. The infinitive *pleroun* ("to fulfill, accomplish, complete") intensifies purpose: Jesus' exodus will fulfill God's redemptive purposes prefigured in Moses' exodus. The phrase "in Jerusalem" (*en Ierousalēm*) specifies geographic location but carries theological weight—Jerusalem is where sacrifices offered, where temple stands, where Passover celebrated, where prophets killed (Luke 13:33), and where Messiah must die to accomplish atonement. Luke's meticulous word choice creates layered meaning: surface narrative (Jesus will die in Jerusalem) overlays theological interpretation (Jesus' death fulfills exodus deliverance, requiring sacrificial blood in covenant city).

**Hermeneutical Use:**
The NT extensively develops exodus typology centered on Christ. First Corinthians 5:7 declares "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed"—identifying Jesus as fulfillment of Passover lamb whose blood protects from judgment. First Corinthians 10:1-4 interprets Israel's Red Sea crossing as baptismal type: "all were baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea... and the Rock was Christ"—seeing Christ present in wilderness providing spiritual sustenance. Hebrews 3:1-19 contrasts Moses' faithfulness with Christ's superiority: Moses faithful as servant, Christ as Son; Moses led to earthly rest, Christ to eternal rest; Israel's wilderness generation failed through unbelief, believers warned not to repeat their hardness of heart. Revelation 15:3 pictures redeemed singing "the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb"—combining exodus deliverance (Moses) with cross deliverance (Lamb), showing both are one unified redemption. John's Gospel saturates narrative with exodus motifs: Jesus as bread from heaven surpassing manna (John 6:31-35), Jesus as light surpassing pillar of fire (John 8:12), Jesus providing living water surpassing rock's water (John 7:37-39). The hermeneutical principle: OT exodus is type, Christ's death-resurrection is antitype; physical deliverance from Egypt prefigures spiritual deliverance from sin; temporal promised land anticipates eternal inheritance; Moses as mediator points to Christ as ultimate mediator. Luke 9:31's *exodos* becomes interpretive key unlocking Christ's passion as redemptive exodus accomplishing what Moses' exodus foreshadowed.

**Theological Use:**
Luke 9:31 establishes rich theology: (1) **Redemptive continuity**: Christ's work isn't separate from OT but fulfills it—Moses' exodus and Jesus' exodus are organically connected as type and antitype. (2) **Substitutionary atonement**: As Passover lamb's blood substituted for firstborn's life, Christ's blood substitutes for sinners deserving judgment. (3) **Resurrection hope**: Exodus led from death-like bondage to new life; Christ's exodus leads through actual death to resurrection life—believers die with Christ (baptism) and rise to new life (Romans 6:3-4). (4) **Cosmic scope**: Original exodus delivered one nation from one oppressor; Christ's exodus delivers all nations from universal oppressors—sin, death, Satan. (5) **Already/not-yet eschatology**: Christ accomplished exodus (already) but full inheritance awaits consummation (not yet)—like Israel between Red Sea crossing and Canaan entry. (6) **Glory through suffering**: Moses and Elijah appeared "in glory" discussing Jesus' exodus—glory comes through suffering, not bypassing it. Transfiguration previews resurrection glory awaiting beyond crucifixion. (7) **Divine necessity**: Jesus "was about to accomplish" (*emellen pleroun*)—His death wasn't tragedy but teleology, necessary for redemption. (8) **Trinitarian cooperation**: Father planned exodus, Son accomplished it, Spirit empowers believers to appropriate it—Godhead unified in redemptive mission. Systematic theology develops: Moses' exodus provides vocabulary and framework for understanding Christ's work; Passover lamb prefigures Christ's sacrifice; Red Sea crossing prefigures baptism; wilderness wandering prefigures sanctification; Canaan entry prefigures glorification.

**Rhetorical Use:**
Luke strategically places Transfiguration (9:28-36) between Jesus' first passion prediction (9:22) and disciples' failure to grasp its necessity (9:44-45). The Transfiguration functions rhetorically to validate Jesus' suffering path: if Moses and Elijah (representing Law and Prophets) appear to discuss Jesus' exodus, then His death fulfills rather than contradicts Scripture. The heavenly voice commanding "Listen to him!" (9:35) endorses Jesus' teaching about necessary suffering (9:22: "The Son of Man must suffer many things and be rejected"). Luke's audience, facing persecution, needs assurance that suffering precedes glory—Transfiguration provides this assurance. The term *exodos* rhetorically connects Jesus' story to Israel's founding narrative, making His death comprehensible within salvation history. For Luke's Gentile audience (Luke 1:3 addresses Theophilus, likely Gentile), exodus typology demonstrates continuity between Israel's story and church's story—Gentile believers inherit Abrahamic promises through Christ who accomplished ultimate exodus. The "glory" language (*doxa* appears three times in 9:28-32) contrasts with suffering language in surrounding context (9:22, "suffer many things"; 9:23, "take up his cross"), creating rhetorical tension resolved at resurrection when suffering yields glory. Peter's misguided suggestion to build three tents (9:33) reveals desire to preserve glory-moment avoiding exodus-suffering—cloud overshadowing and voice speaking correct this misconception. Rhetorically, Luke teaches that glory comes through exodus, not around it; resurrection follows crucifixion, not replaces it; crown comes after cross, not instead of cross. The Transfiguration episode rhetorically bridges Jesus' Galilean ministry (chapters 4-9) and Jerusalem journey (9:51-19:27)—showing divine approval for suffering path and previewing vindication awaiting beyond death.

**Christological Connection:**
[[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 9#Luke 9 . 31|Luke 9:31]] reveals Christ as greater Moses accomplishing ultimate exodus. Every element of Moses' deliverance finds escalated fulfillment in Christ's work. Moses confronted Pharaoh demanding "Let my people go" ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 5#Exodus 5 . 1|Exodus 5:1]]); Christ confronted Satan, sin, and death commanding release of captives ([[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 4#Luke 4 . 18|Luke 4:18]]: "to proclaim liberty to the captives"). Moses instituted Passover requiring lamb's blood on doorposts ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 12#Exodus 12 . 7|Exodus 12:7, 13]]); Christ became Passover Lamb whose blood marks believers for protection from wrath ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 5#1 Corinthians 5 . 7|1 Corinthians 5:7]]). Moses led Israel through Red Sea waters where Egyptian army drowned ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 14#Exodus 14 . 27|Exodus 14:27-28]]); Christ leads believers through baptismal waters where old self dies with Him ([[Readable Bible/45 - Romans/Romans 6#Romans 6 . 3|Romans 6:3-4]]: "We were buried therefore with him by baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life"). Moses brought Israel to Sinai receiving law for covenant ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 19#Exodus 19 . 20|Exodus 19:20]]; [[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 24#Exodus 24 . 12|24:12]]); Christ established new covenant writing law on hearts through Spirit ([[Readable Bible/24 - Jeremiah/Jeremiah 31#Jeremiah 31 . 33|Jeremiah 31:33]]; [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 8#Hebrews 8 . 10|Hebrews 8:10]]). Moses interceded for Israel's sin offering himself in their place ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 32#Exodus 32 . 32|Exodus 32:32]]: "But now, if you will forgive their sin—but if not, please blot me out of your book that you have written"); Christ became sin offering actually bearing punishment ([[Readable Bible/47 - 2 Corinthians/2 Corinthians 5#2 Corinthians 5 . 21|2 Corinthians 5:21]]: "For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God"). Moses led toward earthly Canaan, land flowing with milk and honey ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 3#Exodus 3 . 8|Exodus 3:8]]); Christ leads to heavenly inheritance, eternal kingdom ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 11#Hebrews 11 . 16|Hebrews 11:16]]: "But as it is, they desire a better country, that is, a heavenly one"). The superiority emerges systematically: Moses' exodus delivered from temporal slavery, Christ's from eternal bondage; Moses' exodus required animal blood, Christ's His own blood; Moses' exodus brought to physical land, Christ's to spiritual inheritance; Moses' exodus benefited one nation, Christ's all nations ([[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 5#Revelation 5 . 9|Revelation 5:9]]: "people from every tribe and language and people and nation"). Yet the continuity is equally vital: Christ didn't replace Moses' exodus but fulfilled it—Moses' deliverance was genuine but incomplete, pointing forward to complete deliverance in Christ. Luke's choice of *exodos* creates this dual emphasis: same term (continuity) but greater accomplishment (escalation). The Transfiguration scene visualizes this relationship: Moses and Elijah don't compete with Jesus but converse with Him, acknowledging His exodus consummates what theirs anticipated. Moses represents Law, Elijah represents Prophets—both yielding to Christ who fulfills both ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 5#Matthew 5 . 17|Matthew 5:17]]: "Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I have not come to abolish them but to fulfill them"). The command "Listen to him!" ([[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 9#Luke 9 . 35|Luke 9:35]]) echoes [[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 18#Deuteronomy 18 . 15|Deuteronomy 18:15]]'s promise of prophet like Moses "to him you shall listen"—identifying Jesus as that Prophet whose words carry final authority. Christ's *exodos* "which he was about to accomplish" (*emellen pleroun*) emphasizes intentionality and completion: Moses' exodus was incomplete (that generation died in wilderness, [[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 14#Numbers 14 . 29|Numbers 14:29-30]]), but Christ's exodus perfectly accomplishes salvation—"It is finished" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 19#John 19 . 30|John 19:30]]). The phrase "at Jerusalem" connects Christ's exodus to sacrificial system: as Passover lambs slain at Jerusalem temple, Christ slain at Jerusalem accomplishing what temple sacrifices symbolized. [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 13#Hebrews 13 . 11|Hebrews 13:11-13]] develops this: "For the bodies of those animals whose blood is brought into the holy places by the high priest as a sacrifice for sin are burned outside the camp. So Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood. Therefore let us go to him outside the camp and bear the reproach he endured." Christ's exodus creates new exodus community (church) called to "go outside the camp"—leaving old covenant structures for new covenant reality. The glory Moses and Elijah appeared in previews resurrection glory awaiting Christ and believers: as Christ's *exodos* leads through death to resurrection, so believers follow same pattern ([[Readable Bible/50 - Philippians/Philippians 3#Philippians 3 . 10|Philippians 3:10-11]]: "that I may know him and the power of his resurrection, and may share his sufferings, becoming like him in his death, that by any means possible I may attain the resurrection from the dead"). [[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 9#Luke 9 . 31|Luke 9:31]] thus establishes Jesus as new and greater Moses whose exodus accomplishes ultimate deliverance from ultimate bondage through ultimate sacrifice, bringing ultimate people into ultimate inheritance—escalating every aspect of original exodus while fulfilling its prophetic trajectory.
