**Context:**
Hebrews 2:13 quotes two Old Testament passages—Isaiah 8:17b and Isaiah 8:18—as words spoken by Christ, establishing His solidarity with redeemed humanity. The broader context (Hebrews 2:5-18) addresses Christ's incarnation and its necessity for accomplishing salvation. The passage opens (2:5) asserting the "coming world" won't be subjected to angels but to humanity—citing Psalm 8:4-6 (Heb. 2:6-8) which celebrates humanity's creation "a little lower than angels" yet crowned with glory, having everything subjected under feet. Yet presently "we do not yet see everything in subjection to him" (2:8b)—humanity's intended dominion unfulfilled due to fall. Verse 9 pivots to Christ: "But we see him who for a little while was made lower than the angels, namely Jesus, crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering of death, so that by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone." Christ temporarily descended below angels through incarnation, suffered death, then was exalted—accomplishing what Adam failed: securing humanity's destiny. Verse 10 explains divine rationale: "For it was fitting that he, for whom and by whom all things exist, in bringing many sons to glory, should make the founder of their salvation perfect through suffering." God's plan required Christ's suffering to pioneer salvation—not suggesting Christ lacked moral perfection, but that incarnation-suffering-death-resurrection completed Messianic work, qualifying Him as "founder" (*archēgon*, "pioneer, originator"). Verse 11 establishes unity: "For he who sanctifies and those who are sanctified all have one source. That is why he is not ashamed to call them brothers." Christ (sanctifier) and believers (sanctified) share common origin—both from God, both human—enabling familial relationship. Verses 12-13 support this with three OT quotations presented as Christ's words: (1) Psalm 22:22—"I will tell of your name to my brothers; in the midst of the congregation I will sing your praise"; (2) Isaiah 8:17b—"I will put my trust in him"; (3) Isaiah 8:18—"Behold, I and the children God has given me." All three texts originally spoken by David (Psalm 22) and Isaiah (Isaiah 8), yet Hebrews applies typologically to Christ—showing prophetic figures prefigured Messiah's relationship to redeemed community. The third quotation (Isaiah 8:18) identifies Christ with "children God has given"—believers as Father's gift to Son, forming unified family. The original Isaiah context: Isaiah and his sons bore symbolic names (Shear-jashub, "remnant returns"; Maher-shalal-hash-baz, "swift plunder"; Isaiah, "Yahweh saves") functioning as "signs and portents in Israel" (Isaiah 8:18)—living prophecies embodying divine message. Hebrews reinterprets: as Isaiah stood with children embodying God's word, Christ stands with spiritual children embodying gospel. The escalation: Isaiah's children bore symbolic names; believers bear Christ's name ("Christians"). Isaiah's family represented temporal judgment/salvation; Christ's family represents eternal redemption. Verses 14-18 explain incarnation's necessity: "Since therefore the children share in flesh and blood, he himself likewise partook of the same things, that through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil, and deliver all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong slavery. For surely it is not angels that he helps, but he helps the offspring of Abraham. Therefore he had to be made like his brothers in every respect, so that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest in the service of God, to make propitiation for the sins of the people. For because he himself has suffered when tempted, he is able to help those who are being tempted." Christ assumed full humanity—flesh and blood—to accomplish redemption through death, sympathize with tempted, intercede as high priest. The theological significance: Christ's solidarity with humanity isn't incidental but essential—He must share human nature to represent humans, experience temptation to sympathize, suffer death to atone. The quotation strategy: Hebrews presents Christ speaking OT texts—demonstrating continuity between OT faithful (David, Isaiah) and Christ, showing Christ identified with people of God throughout redemptive history.

**Ninefold Analysis:**

**OT Context:**
Isaiah 8:17-18 originates in context of Isaiah withdrawing from public ministry after widespread rejection of his message. The historical setting: Syro-Ephraimite crisis (735-732 BC) when Syria and Northern Israel formed anti-Assyrian coalition, pressuring Judah to join. King Ahaz refused Isaiah's counsel to trust God, instead appealing to Assyria for help (2 Kings 16:7-9)—tragically making Judah Assyrian vassal. Isaiah's prophecies warning against this course were rejected, prompting withdrawal: "Bind up the testimony; seal the teaching among my disciples" (Isaiah 8:16)—creating written record for future vindication. Verse 17 expresses Isaiah's patient faith during obscurity: "I will wait (*wəḥikkîṯî*) for the LORD, who is hiding his face from the house of Jacob, and I will hope (*wəqiwwîṯî*) in him." The verbs "wait" (*ḥāḵāh*) and "hope" (*qāwāh*) express confident expectation despite present hiddenness—God temporarily withdraws favor from disobedient nation, yet faithful remnant perseveres. The phrase "hiding his face" (*mastîr pānāyw*) indicates divine displeasure withdrawing covenant blessings (contrast Numbers 6:25-26, Aaronic benediction asking God's face to shine). Yet Isaiah doesn't abandon faith; he waits expectantly for vindication. Verse 18 follows: "Behold (*hinnēh*), I and the children whom the LORD has given me (*'ăšer nāṯan-lî YHWH*) are signs (*lə'ōṯôṯ*) and portents (*ûləmôp̄əṯîm*) in Israel from the LORD of hosts, who dwells on Mount Zion." The demonstrative *hinnēh* ("behold") draws attention—Isaiah presents himself and sons as living sermon. The relative clause "whom the LORD has given me" emphasizes divine gift and purpose—these children exist for theological message beyond natural generation. Isaiah's three sons bore symbolic names: (1) Shear-jashub (*šə'ār yāšûḇ*, "a remnant shall return," Isaiah 7:3)—promising preservation through judgment; (2) Maher-shalal-hash-baz (*mahēr šālāl ḥāš baz*, "swift is booty, speedy is prey," Isaiah 8:1-4)—warning imminent Assyrian conquest of Syria-Israel; (3) Isaiah himself (*yəša'yāhû*, "Yahweh is salvation")—proclaiming ultimate deliverance. These names embody God's dual message: judgment on faithless (swift conquest) and salvation for faithful (remnant returns; Yahweh saves). The designation "signs and portents" (*'ōṯôṯ ûmôp̄əṯîm*) employs vocabulary typically describing miraculous events (Exodus plagues, Deuteronomy 6:22)—yet here applied to persons. This incarnational principle—message embodied in messenger—anticipates Christ who IS God's Word made flesh (John 1:14). The phrase "from the LORD of hosts, who dwells on Mount Zion" authenticates Isaiah's prophetic authority—not human invention but divine revelation from covenant God dwelling in Jerusalem temple. The canonical context: Isaiah 8:18 establishes prophetic family typology pointing forward to Messiah and redeemed community. As Isaiah and symbolic-named children represented God's judgment-salvation pattern to 8th-century Judah, Christ and Spirit-named believers represent God's eternal redemption to all nations. The typological correspondences: Isaiah's children = Christ's spiritual children; symbolic names = bearing Christ's name; temporal message = eternal gospel; local witness = universal mission.

**Jewish Backgrounds:**
Jewish interpretation of Isaiah 8:17-18 emphasized prophetic faith amid rejection and prophetic family as covenant witnesses. The Targum Jonathan (Aramaic paraphrase) renders verse 18: "Behold, I and the children, the house of Israel, whom the Memra [Word] of the LORD has given to me for a memorial, are signs and wonders from before the LORD of hosts, whose Shekinah dwells in the sanctuary which is on Mount Zion." The Targum corporatizes "children" as "house of Israel," interpreting personally symbolic family as representing entire nation. The term "Memra" (Word) serves as divine intermediary—Jewish theology's development avoiding direct attribution of actions to God Himself, paralleling Philo's Logos concept and anticipating Christian Logos Christology (John 1:1). Rabbinic tradition (Babylonian Talmud, Berachot 10a) interpreted Isaiah's sons' names as summarizing redemptive history: Maher-shalal-hash-baz = swift judgment on wicked; Shear-jashub = remnant preserved; Isaiah = ultimate salvation by Yahweh. This threefold pattern—judgment, preservation, salvation—structured Jewish understanding of covenant faithfulness. Medieval commentators (Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Kimchi) focused on historical referent: Isaiah's withdrawal from public ministry, sealing testimony among disciples, waiting for prophetic vindication when predictions fulfilled. They emphasized prophet's patient endurance despite rejection—model for Jewish communities suffering persecution. The Dead Sea Scrolls (Qumran) applied Isaiah's texts to their own community, seeing themselves as faithful remnant preserved in wilderness awaiting messianic deliverance—interpreting "I and the children" corporately as Teacher of Righteousness and disciples. First-century Jewish expectation didn't typically connect Isaiah 8:18 to Messiah—corporate interpretation predominated. Yet concept of Messiah gathering elect remnant was present (Psalms of Solomon 17, "he will gather a holy people"). Early Christian interpretation (Hebrews 2:13) innovated by applying Isaiah's words directly to Christ—individual rather than corporate, eschatological rather than merely historical. This sparked Jewish-Christian debate: Can individual fulfill prophecies addressed corporately to Israel? Christian hermeneutic answered affirmatively: Christ represents Israel perfectly (Matthew 2:15, quoting Hosea 11:1), accomplishes what Israel corporately failed, then creates new Israel (church) through His saving work. Modern Jewish scholarship acknowledges ancient Jewish texts sometimes applied Isaiah's Servant passages (including related texts like 8:17-18) messianically, though this interpretation diminished in medieval period partly due to Christian-Jewish polemics.

**Text Form:**
Hebrews 2:13 employs compound quotation formula introducing two Isaiah texts as Christ's words. The phrase "And again (*kai palin*)" signals second quotation following Psalm 22:22 cited in verse 12. The adverb *palin* ("again") indicates additional supporting evidence—writer accumulates OT testimonies demonstrating Christ's identification with believers. The first quotation (Isaiah 8:17b): "I will put my trust (*egō esomai pepoithōs*) in him (*ep' autō*)." The subject *egō* ("I") identifies speaker—Christ assuming Isaiah's words as His own. The future periphrastic construction *esomai pepoithōs* (future *eimi* plus perfect participle of *peithō*) emphasizes settled confidence continuing into future—"I will be in state of trust/confidence." The perfect participle *pepoithōs* (from *peithō*, "to persuade, convince, trust") indicates completed persuasion resulting in ongoing state—firm confidence based on reliable character. The prepositional phrase *ep' autō* ("in/upon him") identifies trust's object—God the Father. The syntax presents Christ as trusting Father—remarkable Christological statement showing Son's human faith during earthly ministry. Though eternally divine, Christ in human nature exercised faith, trusted Father's promises, submitted to Father's will (Luke 22:42, "not my will, but yours, be done"). The second quotation (Isaiah 8:18): "Behold (*idou*), I (*egō*) and the children (*kai ta paidia*) whom God has given me (*ha edōken moi ho theos*)." The imperative *idou* (from *eidon*, "to see") functions as attention-getter—"Look! See!" The conjunction *kai* joins "I" and "children" creating corporate unity—not separate but united entity. The noun *paidia* (neuter plural of *paidion*, "child, infant") refers to young children—emphasizing vulnerability, dependency, familial relationship. The relative clause "whom God has given me" (*ha edōken moi ho theos*) employs aorist active indicative *edōken* (from *didōmi*, "to give") indicating completed past action—God definitively gave. The dative *moi* ("to me") identifies recipient—believers as Father's gift to Son. The subject *ho theos* ("God") makes explicit what Isaiah's Hebrew implied (*YHWH*)—Father gives children to Son. The syntax emphasizes: (1) Corporate identity (*egō kai ta paidia*—I and children form unity); (2) Divine bestowal (*edōken*—given by God, not earned or seized); (3) Personal relationship (*moi*—given to me specifically). Hebrews' hermeneutical move: what Isaiah spoke historically about himself and biological children, Christ speaks eternally about Himself and spiritual children. The typological principle: OT faithful prefigured Christ; their relationships typologically pointed to Christ's relationship with redeemed. Isaiah's family embodying temporal message anticipates Christ's family embodying eternal gospel. The quotation strategy validates Hebrews' thesis (2:11): "he who sanctifies and those who are sanctified all have one source"—Christ and believers share common humanity, enabling familial relationship.

**Hermeneutical Use:**
Hebrews 2:13's application of Isaiah 8:17-18 to Christ employs Christocentric typological hermeneutic recognizing prophetic figures as anticipatory patterns fulfilled in Messiah. The interpretive method: (1) **Prosopological exegesis**: Reading OT texts as spoken by Christ—common NT practice (Romans 15:3 quotes Psalm 69:9 as Christ's words; Hebrews 10:5-7 quotes Psalm 40:6-8 as Christ entering world). This assumes Christological unity of Scripture—Christ speaks throughout OT through types/shadows. (2) **Typological correspondence**: Isaiah and sons typologically prefigure Christ and believers. The pattern: Isaiah = type; Christ = antitype. Isaiah's children = type; believers = antitype. Isaiah's symbolic family = Christ's spiritual family. The escalation: temporal → eternal; biological → spiritual; local → universal; symbolic → substantial. (3) **Analogical transfer**: What Isaiah experienced (rejection, withdrawal, patient faith) Christ experiences supremely (crucifixion, burial, resurrection awaiting parousia). What Isaiah represented (prophet-witness) Christ fulfills perfectly (ultimate Prophet revealing Father). (4) **Canonical-theological unity**: Hebrews sees organic connection between OT faithful and Christ—not discontinuity but progressive revelation culminating in Messiah. Isaiah trusted God amid hiddenness; Christ trusted Father through Gethsemane and Golgotha. Isaiah's family embodied message; Christ's church embodies gospel. The hermeneutical question: Can individual (Christ) fulfill corporate passages (Isaiah's "we")? Hebrews answers affirmatively through representative headship—Christ represents humanity as second Adam (1 Corinthians 15:45), recapitulates Israel's experience as true Israel (Matthew 2:15), fulfills corporate destiny personally then extends to people joined to Him. The validation: Jesus explicitly calls disciples "brothers" (Matthew 28:10; John 20:17), identifies with "least of these my brothers" (Matthew 25:40), claims Father gave disciples to Him (John 6:37; 17:6, 9, 12). This confirms Hebrews' interpretive move isn't eisegesis but draws on Jesus' own self-understanding. The theological implication: Christ's solidarity with humanity isn't accidental but essential—He must share human nature to represent humans, trust as human to pioneer faith (Hebrews 12:2, "founder and perfecter of our faith"), suffer as human to sympathize (Hebrews 4:15). The phrase "children God has given me" establishes double imputation/gift: Father gives believers to Son (sovereign election, John 6:37); Son gives believers to Father (presentation at judgment, Ephesians 5:27). The eternal covenant between Father and Son secures believers' salvation—not dependent on human faithfulness but divine promise-keeping.

**Theological Use:**
Hebrews 2:13's quotation establishes foundational doctrines: (1) **Christ's full humanity**: The statement "I will put my trust in him" indicates Christ exercised faith—controversial claim suggesting dependence incompatible with deity. Yet orthodox Christology affirms: Christ's divine nature knows all, needs nothing; Christ's human nature learned, trusted, obeyed (Hebrews 5:8, "learned obedience through what he suffered"). This grounds Reformed two-natures Christology—one person with divine and human natures, each retaining properties without confusion. (2) **Solidarity theology**: "I and the children" establishes Christ's identification with believers—not distant deity but incarnate brother sharing human experience. This undergirds union with Christ doctrine—mystical connection binding believers to Christ (Romans 6:5; Ephesians 1:3-14). (3) **Divine election**: "Children God has given me" expresses Father's gift to Son—grounding unconditional election (Ephesians 1:4-5). Believers aren't self-selected but divinely bestowed on Son before creation. This assures perseverance—those given by Father to Son remain secure (John 10:28-29). (4) **Covenant theology**: The Father-Son transaction (Father giving believers to Son) reflects covenant of redemption (pactum salutis)—eternal agreement wherein Father elects, Son redeems, Spirit applies salvation. This intratrinitarian covenant precedes creation, guarantees execution. (5) **Christ as pioneer**: Hebrews 2:10 calls Christ *archēgon* ("founder, pioneer") of salvation—going before, blazing trail, securing redemption enabling others to follow. Christ doesn't merely point the way; He IS the Way (John 14:6), opening access to Father through His blood (Hebrews 10:19-20). (6) **Sympathetic high priesthood**: Verses 17-18 explain Christ's priestly sympathy—"made like his brothers in every respect... merciful and faithful high priest... because he himself has suffered when tempted, he is able to help those who are being tempted." Christ's experiential knowledge of human weakness (except sin) qualifies Him to intercede compassionately. This refutes Docetism (heresy denying Christ's full humanity) and Apollinarianism (heresy denying Christ's human soul)—both undermining sympathy. (7) **Adoption theology**: Calling believers "children" (*paidia*) emphasizes familial relationship—not merely servants or subjects but adopted sons/daughters (Galatians 4:5-7; Romans 8:15-17). Adoption grants inheritance rights (Romans 8:17, "fellow heirs with Christ"), access to Father ("Abba, Father," Romans 8:15), eschatological glorification (Romans 8:23). (8) **Perseverance of saints**: If believers are Father's gift to Son, and Son loses none (John 6:39), salvation is secure—not dependent on human effort but divine promise-keeping. This doesn't promote presumption (Hebrews warns against apostasy, 6:4-6; 10:26-31) but assures genuine believers who persevere do so because kept by God's power (1 Peter 1:5).

**Rhetorical Use:**
Hebrews 2:13's rhetoric accumulates OT testimonies validating Christ's identification with humanity. The triple quotation (Psalm 22:22 in v. 12; Isaiah 8:17b and 8:18 in v. 13) creates persuasive weight—not single isolated text but multiple witnesses from different contexts (David, Isaiah) pointing to same reality: Messiah identifies with people. The rhetorical strategy: if two or three witnesses establish truth (Deuteronomy 19:15), how much more do multiple Scriptures? The first quotation (Psalm 22:22) presents Christ declaring God's name to brothers, singing praise amid congregation—portraying Christ as worship leader gathering redeemed community. The second quotation (Isaiah 8:17b) shows Christ trusting Father—modeling faith for believers. The third quotation (Isaiah 8:18) presents Christ with children given by God—demonstrating familial relationship. The cumulative effect: Christ isn't isolated individual but relational being—declaring Father to brothers, trusting Father amid suffering, standing with children Father gave Him. The rhetoric challenges Greco-Roman philosophical ideals: Stoicism prized self-sufficiency, emotional detachment, independence from others. Against this, Hebrews presents Christ as dependent (trusting Father), compassionate (identifying with brothers), communal (standing with children). This rhetoric appeals to Jewish-Christian readers: Christ fulfills OT patterns—what David and Isaiah foreshadowed, Jesus accomplishes. The possessive "children God has given me" creates emotional resonance—readers included in Father's gift to Son, assuring security in Christ's care. The rhetoric: you aren't self-made believers striving to maintain salvation; you're Father's love-gift to Son, eternally secure in covenant transaction. The demonstrative "Behold!" (*idou*) arrests attention—"Look at this reality: I and the children form united family!" The exclamatory force creates wonder: God's Son condescends to call sinners brothers, claim weak believers as precious gifts from Father. The overall rhetorical movement: (1) Establish Christ's solidarity (He calls us brothers, v. 11-12); (2) Demonstrate Christ's humanity (He trusts Father, v. 13a); (3) Assure security (we're Father's gift to Son, v. 13b); (4) Explain necessity (He became human to represent us, vv. 14-18); (5) Apply comfort (He sympathizes with weakness, v. 18). This sequence moves readers from doctrinal abstraction to personal assurance—Christ isn't merely theological concept but compassionate brother who trusts God for us, stands with us, intercedes for us.

**Christological Connection:**
[[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 2#Hebrews 2 . 13|Hebrews 2:13]]'s application of [[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 8#Isaiah 8 . 17|Isaiah 8:17-18]] to Christ establishes His dual identity: fully divine (claiming OT prophecies as His own words) and fully human (trusting Father, standing with brothers). Every phrase reveals Christological depth. The statement "I will put my trust in him" demonstrates Christ's human faith during earthly ministry. Though eternally possessing divine omniscience as second person of Trinity, Christ in human nature exercised genuine faith—trusting Father's promises, submitting to Father's will, enduring cross "for the joy that was set before him" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 12#Hebrews 12 . 2|Hebrews 12:2]]). This trust displayed throughout Gospels: Gethsemane prayer ("not my will, but yours," [[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 22#Luke 22 . 42|Luke 22:42]]), cross-cry ("Father, into your hands I commit my spirit," [[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 23#Luke 23 . 46|Luke 23:46]]), resurrection confidence ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 16#Psalm 16 . 10|Psalm 16:10]], "you will not abandon my soul to Hades," quoted [[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 2#Acts 2 . 27|Acts 2:27]]). Christ pioneered faith ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 12#Hebrews 12 . 2|Hebrews 12:2]], *archēgon kai teleiōtēn tēs pisteōs*, "founder and perfecter of faith")—blazing trail of trust through suffering to glory. Where Adam distrusted God's word ([[Readable Bible/01 - Genesis/Genesis 3#Genesis 3 . 1|Genesis 3:1-6]]), Christ trusted utterly. Where Israel doubted in wilderness ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 14#Numbers 14 . 11|Numbers 14:11]]), Christ remained faithful. Christ thus serves as second Adam recapitulating human destiny, true Israel accomplishing what nation failed. The phrase "I and the children" establishes Christ's solidarity with believers. Though infinitely above (divine nature), Christ condescended to share human nature ([[Readable Bible/50 - Philippians/Philippians 2#Philippians 2 . 6|Philippians 2:6-8]]), becoming "like his brothers in every respect" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 2#Hebrews 2 . 17|Hebrews 2:17]]). This solidarity enables representation—Christ as covenant head acts on behalf of those united to Him. The familial language ("children," "brothers") reflects intimacy: not master-slaves (though that's true, [[Readable Bible/45 - Romans/Romans 1#Romans 1 . 1|Romans 1:1]]) but family members sharing Father's love. Jesus declared: "whoever does the will of my Father in heaven is my brother and sister and mother" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 12#Matthew 12 . 50|Matthew 12:50]])—spiritual kinship surpasses biological. Post-resurrection, Jesus tells Mary Magdalene: "go to my brothers and say to them, 'I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God'" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 20#John 20 . 17|John 20:17]])—explicitly calling disciples "brothers," sharing Father-relationship. The designation "children God has given me" echoes Jesus' high priestly prayer (John 17) where He repeatedly speaks of those "you have given me" (vv. 6, 9, 11, 12, 24). This establishes believers' security—they're Father's love-gift to Son, held eternally in Son's care. Jesus promises: "All that the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never cast out... And this is the will of him who sent me, that I should lose nothing of all that he has given me, but raise it up on the last day" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 6#John 6 . 37|John 6:37]], 39). None given by Father to Son will perish—sovereign grace ensures perseverance. The typological escalation from Isaiah to Christ: Isaiah's children bore symbolic names pointing to temporal judgment/salvation; Christ's children bear His name ("Christians") participating in eternal redemption. Isaiah stood with biological offspring embodying prophetic message; Christ stands with spiritual offspring embodying gospel. Isaiah's family functioned as "signs and portents" ([[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 8#Isaiah 8 . 18|Isaiah 8:18]]) to 8th-century Judah; Christ's church functions as light to all nations ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 5#Matthew 5 . 14|Matthew 5:14]]; [[Readable Bible/50 - Philippians/Philippians 2#Philippians 2 . 15|Philippians 2:15]]). Isaiah trusted God amid rejection and withdrawal; Christ trusted Father through ultimate rejection (crucifixion) and withdrawal (burial) unto vindication (resurrection). The parallel: both prophet and Messiah experience hiddenness before revelation, suffering before glory, rejection before acceptance. Yet Christ infinitely surpasses: Isaiah's faith imperfect, Christ's perfect; Isaiah's suffering temporal, Christ's eternal consequences; Isaiah's vindication historical, Christ's eschatological. The christological pattern: Incarnation (assuming flesh and blood, v. 14) → Temptation (suffering when tempted, v. 18) → Trust (putting confidence in Father, v. 13a) → Death (tasting death for everyone, v. 9) → Resurrection (crowned with glory, v. 9) → Intercession (merciful high priest, v. 17) → Presentation (bringing many sons to glory, v. 10). At each stage, Christ stands with "children God has given Him"—identifying in incarnation, sympathizing in temptation, trusting for us, dying for us, rising as firstfruits, interceding perpetually, presenting us faultless before Father (Jude 24). The eschatological vision: [[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 7#Revelation 7 . 9|Revelation 7:9-10]] depicts "great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne and before the Lamb, clothed in white robes, with palm branches in their hands, crying out with a loud voice, 'Salvation belongs to our God who sits on the throne, and to the Lamb!'" This consummation fulfills [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 2#Hebrews 2 . 13|Hebrews 2:13]]'s vision: Christ standing with innumerable children Father gave Him, united family worshiping eternally. Where [[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 8#Isaiah 8 . 18|Isaiah 8:18]] presented prophet and three sons as signs to one nation, Christ presents redeemed multitude from every nation as sign of Father's electing love and Son's atoning accomplishment. The Christological core: Jesus IS Isaiah's greater son, Suffering Servant who trusted Father perfectly, stood with spiritual children confidently, accomplished redemption completely, now intercedes perpetually, will present finally—bringing many sons to glory through His pioneering faith, sympathetic priesthood, and vicarious sacrifice. [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 2#Hebrews 2 . 13|Hebrews 2:13]] thus establishes Christ's identification with humanity as neither incidental nor temporary but essential and eternal—He permanently assumed human nature, forever remains man while remaining God, eternally serves as mediator between God and humanity ([[Readable Bible/54 - 1 Timothy/1 Timothy 2#1 Timothy 2 . 5|1 Timothy 2:5]]), perpetually intercedes as sympathetic high priest ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 7#Hebrews 7 . 25|Hebrews 7:25]]), ultimately presents redeemed family spotless before Father ([[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 5#Ephesians 5 . 27|Ephesians 5:27]]), consummating Isaiah's vision of prophet-with-children into Christ-with-church for eternal ages.
