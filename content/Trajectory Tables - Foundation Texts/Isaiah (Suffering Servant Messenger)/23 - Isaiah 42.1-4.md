**Context:**
Isaiah 42:1-4 introduces the first of four Servant Songs (42:1-9; 49:1-13; 50:4-11; 52:13-53:12), presenting God's chosen Servant who will establish justice globally through gentle, persistent ministry. This oracle dates to Isaiah's 8th-century BC prophetic ministry during tumultuous period when Assyrian Empire threatened Judah, yet God reveals His redemptive plan extends beyond immediate crisis to encompass all nations. The passage opens with divine presentation: "Behold my servant" (*hēn 'aḇdî*)—demonstrative particle drawing attention to figure of supreme importance. God identifies Servant with three intimate terms: (1) "my servant" (*'aḇdî*, first-person possessive emphasizing covenant relationship); (2) "whom I uphold" (*'etmāḵ-bô*, indicating divine sustaining); (3) "my chosen, in whom my soul delights" (*bəḥîrî rāṣəṯāh nap̄šî*, expressing divine pleasure). The bestowal "I have put my Spirit upon him" (*nāṯattî rûḥî 'ālāyw*) parallels Messianic anointing passages (Isaiah 11:2; 61:1), identifying Servant as Spirit-empowered agent. The mission statement "he will bring forth justice to the nations" (*mišpāṭ lagôyim yôṣî'*) defines global scope—not merely Israel but *gôyim* (Gentiles). The Hebrew *mišpāṭ* encompasses more than judicial verdicts; it signifies righteous order, divine will implemented, covenant faithfulness establishing shalom. Verses 2-3a describe Servant's methodology through four negations and one affirmation, creating portrait of gentle persistence contrasting violent conquest: "He will not cry aloud or lift up his voice, or make it heard in the street" (v. 2)—no self-promoting propaganda or coercive force; "a bruised reed he will not break" (v. 3a)—respecting fragility rather than exploiting weakness; "a faintly burning wick he will not quench" (v. 3b)—preserving flickering hope rather than extinguishing it. The imagery: reed (*qāneh*) refers to marsh plants used for walking sticks or measuring rods—easily broken when damaged; wick (*pištāh*) made from flax fibers, when burning dimly (*kēhāh*) often snuffed out and replaced—Servant instead nurses it back to full flame. This reveals character: compassionate toward weak, patient with struggling, gentle with vulnerable. The positive declaration "he will faithfully bring forth justice" (v. 3c, *le'emeṯ yôṣî' mišpāṭ*) uses emphatic infinitive construct: truthfully, reliably, certainly He will accomplish mission. Verse 4 reinforces Servant's perseverance: "He will not grow faint or be discouraged" (v. 4a, *lō' yiḵheh wəlō' yārûṣ*)—using same adjectives applied to wick/reed but negated for Servant. What would break ordinary minister doesn't break this Servant. The temporal clause "till he has established justice in the earth" (v. 4b, *'aḏ-yāśîm bā'āreṣ mišpāṭ*) sets endurance parameter—mission continues until completion. The climactic declaration "and the coastlands wait for his law" (v. 4c, *ûləṯôrāṯô 'îyîm yəyaḥēlû*) reveals Gentile eagerness—distant nations (*'îyîm*, islands/coastlands representing remote territories) expectantly hope (*yāḥal*, expressing patient longing) for Servant's *tôrāh* (instruction/teaching). The theological significance: God's redemptive plan includes Gentiles from beginning; justice comes through gentle ministry not violent revolution; Spirit-empowerment enables mission; perseverance characterizes faithful service; ultimate global transformation inevitable. The NT identifies Jesus as this Servant: Matthew 12:18-21 quotes Isaiah 42:1-4 after Jesus withdraws from Pharisees' persecution and heals quietly, showing His gentle methodology fulfills prophecy. The Father's baptismal declaration "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased" (Matthew 3:17) echoes Isaiah 42:1's "my chosen, in whom my soul delights." Peter identifies Jesus as "the Holy and Righteous One" (Acts 3:14), connecting to Servant's justice mission. Paul's gospel to Gentiles (*gôyim*) fulfills "he will bring forth justice to the nations." The Servant Songs trace progressive revelation: first song (42:1-9) introduces gentle justice-bringer; second (49:1-13) reveals mission to restore Israel and be light to nations; third (50:4-11) describes obedient suffering; fourth (52:13-53:12) details vicarious atonement. Together they present Messiah's twofold mission: suffering servant and exalted king.

**Ninefold Analysis:**

**OT Context:**
Isaiah 42:1-4 introduces the first Servant Song within broader context of Isaiah 40-48, section proclaiming comfort to exiled Israel. Chapter 40 opened with "Comfort, comfort my people, says your God" (40:1), announcing end of Babylonian captivity and return to land. Yet Isaiah's vision extends beyond immediate historical deliverance to eschatological redemption encompassing all nations. Chapter 41 challenged idols' impotence, asserting Yahweh alone controls history: "Who stirred up one from the east... gives up nations before him?" (41:2)—referring to Cyrus whom God will raise as deliverer. Yet human deliverers like Cyrus accomplish only temporal political liberation; God's ultimate redemption requires greater agent. Chapter 42:1 shifts from Cyrus (called but not Spirit-filled) to Servant (chosen and Spirit-anointed). The literary structure creates deliberate contrast: Cyrus called "my shepherd" (44:28) and "anointed" (45:1) in functional sense, yet Servant called "my chosen, in whom my soul delights" (42:1)—intimate language reserved for unique relationship. The historical context: Isaiah prophesied during Assyrian crisis (8th century BC), yet foresaw Babylonian exile (6th century BC) and looked beyond to Messianic age. The Servant's identity generated scholarly debate: corporate Israel? faithful remnant? prophet Isaiah? Messiah? The text's tension: Servant called "Israel" in 49:3 yet has mission to restore Israel (49:5-6), suggesting both corporate and individual dimensions. Jewish interpretation often identified Servant collectively as nation Israel suffering among Gentiles; Christian interpretation identifies Jesus as individual fulfillment while acknowledging corporate aspects (church as Christ's body continues Servant mission). The canonical context: Isaiah 1-39 emphasized judgment on Judah's sin, Assyrian invasion, remnant preservation; Isaiah 40-55 emphasizes comfort, deliverance from Babylon, Servant's redemptive work; Isaiah 56-66 describes restored community's worship and Gentile inclusion. The four Servant Songs (42:1-9; 49:1-13; 50:4-11; 52:13-53:12) punctuate this section, progressively revealing Servant's identity, mission, suffering, and triumph. First song (42:1-4) establishes: (1) Divine election—"my servant... my chosen"; (2) Spirit empowerment—"I have put my Spirit upon him"; (3) Gentle methodology—not breaking bruised reed; (4) Global mission—"justice to the nations"; (5) Certain success—coastlands wait for his teaching. The vocabulary echoes creation and exodus: "Spirit" (*rûaḥ*) recalls Genesis 1:2 (Spirit hovering over waters); "bring forth" (*yāṣā'*) echoes exodus deliverance (bringing Israel out of Egypt); "justice" (*mišpāṭ*) connects to Sinai covenant establishing righteous order. The Servant thus inaugurates new creation and new exodus—eschatological redemption surpassing historical precedents. The contrast between Servant's methods (vv. 2-3) and typical conquest: ancient Near Eastern kings boasted of crushing enemies like reeds, extinguishing rebellious cities; Assyrian annals describe brutal subjugation—impaling captives, burning cities, enslaving populations. Servant's gentleness radically subverts power politics: instead of breaking weak, He strengthens them; instead of exploiting vulnerable, He protects them. This upside-down kingdom pattern becomes Jesus' teaching: "whoever would be great among you must be your servant" (Matthew 20:26). The promise "he will faithfully bring forth justice" (v. 3) uses emphatic construction (*le'emeṯ yôṣî' mišpāṭ*)—absolutely, certainly, reliably justice will be established. This assures readers God's plan won't fail despite appearances. When Israel experiences exile, oppression, apparent abandonment, Servant's mission continues toward inevitable completion. Verse 4's encouragement—Servant won't grow faint or be crushed—applies same imagery used for weak reed/wick but negated: what crushes ordinary people won't defeat this Servant. The persistence until establishing justice "in the earth" (*bā'āreṣ*) extends scope globally—not merely Judah or Israel but entire world. The climactic statement "coastlands wait for his law" reveals Gentile inclusion from beginning—not afterthought but original design. The Hebrew *'îyîm* (islands/coastlands) represented distant territories (Greece, Mediterranean islands, western Europe) beyond Israel's borders. The verb *yəyaḥēlû* ("wait, hope, expect") expresses eager anticipation—Gentiles aren't coerced but willingly seek Servant's *tôrāh* (instruction). This reverses typical Jewish-Gentile dynamics where Israel resented pagan oppressors; here Gentiles eagerly desire God's truth mediated through Servant.

**Jewish Backgrounds:**
Jewish interpretation of Isaiah 42:1-4 developed multiple trajectories. The Targum Jonathan paraphrases verse 1: "Behold my Servant, the Messiah, whom I bring near, my chosen one in whom my Memra takes pleasure." This identifies Servant as individual Messiah (not corporate Israel), using *Memra* (Word) as mediating divine agent—reflecting Jewish theology's development of intermediary figures. However, rabbinic tradition remained divided on Servant's identity. Rabbi Moshe Alshich (16th century) interpreted Servant as righteous remnant within Israel suffering for nation's sins. Ibn Ezra (12th century) identified Servant as prophet Isaiah himself. Rashi (11th century) applied corporately to Israel exiled among nations. The debate centered on tension: Servant called "Israel" (49:3) yet has mission to restore Israel (49:5-6)—individual or corporate? Rabbinic midrash (Yalkut Shimoni on Isaiah) interpreted "bruised reed he will not break" referring to Messiah's compassion toward poor and lowly, connecting to expectation that Messiah would champion oppressed. The Babylonian Talmud (Sanhedrin 98b) lists multiple Messianic names including "the leper scholar" based on Isaiah 53:4 ("stricken, smitten by God"), suggesting some rabbis identified Suffering Servant as Messiah. Yet after Christian-Jewish polemics intensified, corporate interpretation predominated—rejecting Christian claim that Jesus fulfilled Servant prophecies. Maimonides (12th century, Mishneh Torah) interpreted Servant corporately as Israel suffering in exile, awaiting future vindication. The Dead Sea Scrolls (1QIsaᵃ) preserve Isaiah text virtually identical to Masoretic Text, showing textual stability. Qumran community may have identified their Teacher of Righteousness with Servant figure, though evidence remains ambiguous. The Septuagint (LXX, 3rd-2nd century BC Greek translation) renders key terms: *'eḇeḏ* as *pais* ("servant, child"), *bāḥîr* as *eklektos* ("chosen, elect"), *rûaḥ* as *pneuma* ("spirit"). The LXX's influence on NT writers evident—Matthew 12:18 follows LXX wording. First-century Jewish expectation anticipated conquering Messiah defeating Rome, establishing political kingdom; suffering Servant imagery didn't fit this paradigm, contributing to Jewish rejection of Jesus. Josephus (1st century, Antiquities 10.35) references Isaiah's prophecies but doesn't explicitly discuss Servant Songs. Philo of Alexandria allegorizes Isaiah, seeing Servant as Logos mediating between transcendent God and material world. Medieval Jewish commentators emphasized Servant's mission to establish *mišpāṭ* (justice)—understanding this forensically (legal verdicts) and ethically (righteous living). The promise that "coastlands wait for his law" (*tôrāh*) generated discussion: Does this mean Gentiles will observe Mosaic law? Or universal ethical principles? Post-Talmudic tradition distinguished Noahide Laws (seven universal commandments) from Mosaic Torah (613 commandments for Israel). Modern Jewish scholarship (20th century) revisited Servant's identity: some maintained corporate interpretation; others acknowledged individual Messianic dimensions. The discovery that crucifixion victims' suffering matches Isaiah 53's description led some Jewish scholars to reconsider whether Jesus might fulfill prophecy, though this remains minority view.

**Text Form:**
Isaiah 42:1-4 employs rhetorical presentation structure with divine speech introducing Servant. Verse 1 opens with demonstrative particle *hēn* ("behold")—attention-getting device similar to *hinnēh*, signaling important announcement. The subject "my servant" (*'aḇdî*) uses first-person possessive suffix indicating intimate relationship. The verb "I uphold" (*'etmāḵ*) employs Qal imperfect first-person, expressing continuous divine support—not past event but ongoing reality. The relative clause "whom I uphold" creates asyndeton (no conjunction), producing tight identification between subject and divine action. The second designation "my chosen" (*bəḥîrî*) uses noun with possessive suffix derived from verb *bāḥar* ("to choose, elect"), emphasizing divine selection. The relative clause "in whom my soul delights" (*rāṣəṯāh nap̄šî*) employs perfect verb indicating completed satisfaction—God's pleasure settled, not contingent. The term *nep̄eš* (soul/life) represents God's essential being, making delight profoundly personal. The declaration "I have put my Spirit upon him" (*nāṯattî rûḥî 'ālāyw*) uses perfect verb (*nāṯattî*, "I have given") indicating accomplished bestowal. The object "my Spirit" (*rûḥî*) identifies divine presence/power, not merely human enthusiasm. The preposition *'al* ("upon") suggests external empowerment descending from above (cf. Joel 2:28-29). The purpose clause "he will bring forth justice to the nations" (*mišpāṭ lagôyim yôṣî'*) employs imperfect verb *yôṣî'* ("he will bring out") indicating future action proceeding from Spirit-empowerment. The object *mišpāṭ* ("justice, judgment, ordinance") represents righteous order established by divine decree. The indirect object *lagôyim* ("to the nations, Gentiles") uses preposition *lə* indicating direction/purpose—justice directed toward Gentiles, not merely Israel. Verse 2 shifts to negative descriptions using four negated verbs creating portrait by contrast: "He will not cry aloud" (*lō' yiṣ'aq*)—negated imperfect verb *ṣā'aq* (to cry out, summon) indicates absence of self-promotion; "or lift up his voice" (*wəlō' yiśśā'*)—negated imperfect of *nāśā'* (to lift, raise) indicates no loud public displays; "or make it heard in the street" (*wəlō' yašmîa' baḥûṣ qôlô*)—negated Hiphil imperfect of *šāma'* (to hear) indicates voice not broadcast publicly. The threefold negation emphasizes humility, avoiding spectacle. The noun *ḥûṣ* ("street, outside") represents public spaces where politicians/merchants advertise. Servant's ministry contrasts worldly power-seeking. Verse 3 employs two metaphors using nominal sentences: "a bruised reed he will not break" (*qāneh rāṣûṣ lō' yišbôr*)—object *qāneh* (reed, measuring rod) modified by passive participle *rāṣûṣ* ("crushed, bruised") describes damaged fragile plant; negated imperfect verb *yišbôr* (from *šāḇar*, "to break, shatter") indicates Servant won't destroy what's already damaged. The imagery: reeds used for walking sticks or measuring; when cracked/bruised, typically discarded—Servant instead preserves them. "A faintly burning wick he will not quench" (*ûp̄ištāh kēhāh lō' yəḵabbenāh*)—object *pištāh* (flax, wick) modified by adjective *kēhāh* ("dim, faint, weak") describes nearly extinguished lamp; negated Piel imperfect *yəḵabbenāh* (from *kāḇāh*, "to quench, extinguish") indicates Servant won't snuff out struggling light. The Piel stem intensifies—won't actively extinguish what barely burns. These metaphors portray gentle preservation versus harsh elimination. The positive declaration "he will faithfully bring forth justice" (*le'emeṯ yôṣî' mišpāṭ*) uses emphatic construction: preposition *lə* plus noun *'emeṯ* ("truth, faithfulness") functioning adverbially intensifying verb *yôṣî'* ("bring out, bring forth")—absolutely, reliably, certainly justice will emerge. The repetition of *yôṣî' mišpāṭ* from verse 1 creates inclusio bracketing description of Servant's gentle methods. Verse 4 mirrors verse 3's imagery but applies to Servant himself: "He will not grow faint" (*lō' yiḵheh*)—negated Qal imperfect of *kāhāh* (same root as *kēhāh*, "dim, faint" in v. 3); "or be discouraged" (*wəlō' yārûṣ*)—negated Qal imperfect of *rāṣaṣ* (same root as *rāṣûṣ*, "bruised" in v. 3). The wordplay: Servant won't become what He compassionately preserves. Where wick grows faint (*kēhāh*), Servant won't grow faint (*lō' yiḵheh*); where reed is bruised (*rāṣûṣ*), Servant won't be crushed (*lō' yārûṣ*). This creates thematic reversal—Servant's strength preserves weak without succumbing to weakness. The temporal clause "till he has established justice in the earth" (*'aḏ-yāśîm bā'āreṣ mišpāṭ*) uses preposition *'aḏ* ("until, as far as") plus Qal imperfect *yāśîm* (from *śîm*, "to put, place, set") indicating future completed action—justice firmly established. The location *bā'āreṣ* ("in the earth, land") expands scope globally. The climactic statement "and the coastlands wait for his law" (*ûləṯôrāṯô 'îyîm yəyaḥēlû*) employs chiastic structure placing object (*ləṯôrāṯô*, "for his instruction") before subject (*'îyîm*, "coastlands, islands") and verb (*yəyaḥēlû*, "they wait, hope"). The pronominal suffix *-ô* ("his") personalizes *tôrāh*—not generic law but Servant's specific instruction. The verb *yəyaḥēlû* (Piel imperfect of *yāḥal*, "to wait, hope") suggests eager anticipation with confidence—not passive resignation but active expectation.

**Hermeneutical Use:**
The NT interprets Isaiah 42:1-4 as direct prophecy of Jesus Christ, with Matthew 12:17-21 providing longest OT quotation in Matthew's Gospel. The context: Jesus healed on Sabbath, Pharisees plotted to destroy Him (Matthew 12:14); Jesus withdrew and healed many, ordering them not to make Him known (12:15-16). Matthew explains: "This was to fulfill what was spoken by the prophet Isaiah: 'Behold, my servant whom I have chosen, my beloved with whom my soul is well pleased. I will put my Spirit upon him, and he will proclaim justice to the Gentiles. He will not quarrel or cry aloud, nor will anyone hear his voice in the streets; a bruised reed he will not break, and a smoldering wick he will not quench, until he brings justice to victory; and in his name the Gentiles will hope'" (12:17-21). Matthew's hermeneutical method: typological-fulfillment reading—Isaiah's Servant prophecy finds actualization in Jesus. The identification rests on multiple correspondences: (1) Divine choosing—"my servant whom I have chosen" matches Father's election of Son (Matthew 3:17; 17:5); (2) Divine delight—"my beloved with whom my soul is well pleased" echoes baptismal declaration; (3) Spirit empowerment—"I will put my Spirit upon him" fulfilled at Jesus' baptism when Spirit descended like dove (Matthew 3:16); (4) Mission to Gentiles—"he will proclaim justice to the Gentiles" fulfilled in Jesus' ministry to Syrophoenician woman (Matthew 15:21-28), healing centurion's servant (Matthew 8:5-13), Great Commission to make disciples of all nations (Matthew 28:19-20); (5) Gentle methodology—"bruised reed he will not break" demonstrated in Jesus' compassion toward weak, sick, demon-possessed, social outcasts; (6) Humble demeanor—"will not quarrel or cry aloud" shown in Jesus' withdrawal from Pharisees' plots rather than political confrontation; (7) Certain victory—"until he brings justice to victory" anticipates crucifixion-resurrection establishing righteousness globally; (8) Gentile hope—"in his name the Gentiles will hope" fulfilled in church's growth among nations. The baptismal theophany (Matthew 3:13-17) deliberately echoes Isaiah 42:1: Father declares "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased" (3:17)—directly quoting Isaiah's "my chosen, in whom my soul delights." Spirit descends upon Jesus (3:16) fulfilling "I have put my Spirit upon him." The term "beloved" (*agapētos*) translates both *bāḥîr* ("chosen") and conveys unique filial relationship. The transfiguration (Matthew 17:1-8) repeats declaration: "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased; listen to him" (17:5)—reaffirming Jesus' Servant identity. Peter applies Servant language to Jesus in Acts 3:13, 26; 4:27, 30, calling Him "God's servant" (*pais theou*)—same term LXX uses for Isaiah's *'eḇeḏ*. The early church's liturgy (Didache, early 2nd century) refers to Jesus as "your servant (*pais*) Jesus." Paul's Christology reflects Servant pattern: Philippians 2:7 describes Jesus "taking the form of a servant (*morphēn doulou*), being born in the likeness of men," echoing Isaiah's Servant who emptied Himself for redemptive mission. The Gospels' portrait of Jesus consistently matches Isaiah 42's description: gentle with sinners (John 8:1-11, woman caught in adultery), compassionate toward weak (Matthew 9:36, "he had compassion on them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd"), patient with struggling disciples (restoring Peter after denial, John 21:15-19), unwilling to extinguish flickering faith (Luke 22:31-32, "I have prayed for you that your faith may not fail"). The hermeneutical principle: Jesus fulfills not merely by linguistic prediction but by embodying Servant's character, mission, and destiny. Where Israel corporately failed as God's servant (often indicted for unfaithfulness), Jesus perfectly accomplishes Servant role. Romans 15:8 declares Christ became "a servant to the circumcised to show God's truthfulness, in order to confirm the promises given to the patriarchs." The escalation: Isaiah's Servant brings justice to nations through teaching; Jesus establishes justice through atoning death (Romans 3:25-26, "to show God's righteousness... so that he might be just and the justifier"). Isaiah's Servant receives Spirit's empowerment; Jesus IS eternally one with Spirit (John 14:16-17, sending "another Helper," implying Jesus Himself first Helper). Isaiah's Servant awaited by coastlands; Jesus proclaimed universally (Colossians 1:23, "gospel that you heard, which has been proclaimed in all creation under heaven").

**Theological Use:**
Isaiah 42:1-4 establishes foundational doctrines developed throughout Scripture: (1) **Divine election**: "My servant... my chosen" demonstrates God's sovereign selection for redemptive purposes. Theology of election—God chooses not based on merit but divine pleasure—grounds assurance of salvation (Ephesians 1:4-5). Applied to Christ, reinforces eternal covenant between Father and Son (Psalm 2:7-8; John 17:4-5). Applied to believers, assures "those whom he predestined he also called" (Romans 8:30). (2) **Trinitarian cooperation**: Father speaks ("my servant"), Spirit empowers ("I have put my Spirit upon him"), Servant accomplishes mission—revealing tripersonal God working in unified redemptive plan. This challenges unitarian theology, confirms orthodox trinitarianism. (3) **Incarnational mission**: Servant identified intimately with God ("my chosen, in whom my soul delights") yet distinct person receiving Spirit—anticipates incarnation where Son assumes human nature while remaining divine. Servant's gentleness reflects divine character: God is compassionate, patient, preserving weak (Psalm 103:13-14). (4) **Universal atonement scope**: "Justice to the nations" and "coastlands wait for his law" establish redemption's global extent. This refutes narrow ethnocentrism, confirms Abrahamic promise (Genesis 12:3), anticipates Great Commission. Systematic theology distinguishes sufficiency (Christ's death sufficient for all) from efficiency (effective for elect), yet passage emphasizes universal offer. (5) **Substitutionary righteousness**: Servant "brings forth justice" (*mišpāṭ*)—not merely proclaims it but accomplishes it. Theological development: Christ establishes justice by satisfying divine law's demands through perfect obedience and penal substitution (Romans 3:21-26; 5:18-19). His righteousness imputed to believers, satisfying justice while granting mercy. (6) **Gentle pastoral care**: "Bruised reed he will not break" models ministry methodology—compassion toward weak, patience with struggling, restoration of damaged. Applied christologically: Jesus' earthly ministry; applied ecclesiologically: pastoral care of church leaders (1 Thessalonians 5:14, "help the weak, be patient with them all"); applied soteriologically: God preserves fragile faith until glorification (Philippians 1:6). (7) **Persevering grace**: "He will not grow faint or be discouraged till he has established justice" assures mission's completion despite opposition. Grounds perseverance of saints doctrine—Christ who began good work will complete it (Philippians 1:6); applied to believers' sanctification—God "who began a good work in you will bring it to completion" (Philippians 1:6). (8) **Eschatological certainty**: "Till he has established justice in the earth" promises inevitable global transformation. This grounds postmillennial/amillennial optimism about gospel's eventual triumph; even premillennialists acknowledge Christ's final victory establishing righteousness universally. Theology of history—not cyclical pagan view nor pessimistic decline, but teleological progression toward Christ's kingdom consummation. (9) **Christological offices**: Servant functions as Prophet (bringing *tôrāh*, instruction), Priest (establishing *mišpāṭ* through sacrifice), King (ruling with justice over nations)—anticipating Christ's threefold office developed in Reformed theology. Each office essential: Prophet reveals truth, Priest atones for sin, King governs righteously.

**Rhetorical Use:**
Isaiah 42:1-4's rhetoric moves readers from divine presentation to human response. The opening *hēn* ("behold") arrests attention—God Himself introduces someone of supreme importance. Ancient audiences familiar with royal court protocol would recognize presentation formula: sovereign introduces chosen representative. The rhetoric establishes authority: not Isaiah's personal opinion but divine revelation. The threefold identification—"my servant... my chosen... in whom my soul delights"—creates escalating intimacy: legal relationship (servant), elective relationship (chosen), affectionate relationship (delights). This progression rhetorically shapes readers' expectations: whoever this is, God deeply invests in him. The declaration "I have put my Spirit upon him" signals supernatural empowerment beyond human capacity—evoking exodus memory (Moses, Joshua receiving Spirit) and raising expectations for comparable mighty deeds. Yet verse 2's negations surprise: "He will not cry aloud... lift up voice... make it heard in street"—subverting expectation of powerful public figure. Ancient Near Eastern kings boasted loudly; prophets often thundered judgment; revolutionaries incited crowds—but this Servant operates quietly. The rhetorical effect: dissonance between divine endorsement and humble methodology provokes reflection—how can quietly working figure accomplish global mission? This prepares readers for gospel's scandal: salvation through crucified peasant rather than political king. The metaphors (bruised reed, smoldering wick) create emotional connection—readers identify with fragility, appreciate gentle preservation. Everyone experiences brokenness; everyone fears being discarded when usefulness fades. The assurance "he will not break... not quench" offers comfort: God's Servant won't exploit your weakness but will strengthen you. This rhetoric attracts rather than repels—drawing weary to rest (Matthew 11:28-30). The positive declaration "faithfully bring forth justice" (*le'emeṯ yôṣî' mišpāṭ*) pivots from method to outcome—despite humble means, certain result follows. The adverb *le'emeṯ* ("faithfully, certainly") rhetorically assures readers: don't judge mission by methodology; judge by outcome. This challenges pragmatic thinking: success isn't measured by noise, crowds, political power, but by actual justice established. Verse 4's encouragement—Servant won't grow faint or be crushed—creates confidence through wordplay connecting Servant's strength to weak people's preservation. The rhetoric: same words describing fragile reed/wick (*rāṣûṣ*, *kēhāh*) negated for Servant (*lō' yārûṣ*, *lō' yiḵheh*)—Servant absorbs what would crush others, enabling Him to preserve them. This anticipates cross theology: Christ bears curse so believers escape it (Galatians 3:13). The temporal clause "till he has established justice in the earth" creates open-ended timeline—rhetorically preparing readers for delayed consummation. Mission isn't quick political revolution but patient transformation. This counters revolutionary impatience, encouraging faithful endurance. The climax "coastlands wait for his law" reverses typical colonialism rhetoric—not conqueror imposing law on subjugated peoples, but distant nations eagerly desiring instruction. The verb *yəyaḥēlû* ("wait, hope, expect") suggests longing, anticipation—Gentiles aren't coerced but attracted. This rhetoric elevates Servant's *tôrāh* above all competing philosophies—not another regional religion but universal truth all humanity craves. The overall rhetorical movement: (1) Arrest attention (behold!); (2) Establish divine authority (my servant/chosen); (3) Subvert expectations (gentle not violent); (4) Create identification (you're the bruised reed); (5) Promise preservation (won't break you); (6) Assure completion (will establish justice); (7) Inspire hope (nations await). This sequence transforms readers from skeptics wondering how quiet servant accomplishes anything, to believers trusting God's upside-down methodology, to worshipers joining global chorus praising Servant's ultimate victory.

**Christological Connection:**
[[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 42#Isaiah 42 . 1|Isaiah 42:1-4]] finds perfect fulfillment in Jesus Christ, whom the Father identifies as "my beloved Son" echoing "my chosen, in whom my soul delights." Every aspect of Servant's description corresponds precisely to Jesus' identity and ministry. The title "my servant" (*'aḇdî*) anticipates Christ's self-understanding: "the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" ([[Readable Bible/41 - Mark/Mark 10#Mark 10 . 45|Mark 10:45]]). Where Adam grasped at equality with God ([[Readable Bible/01 - Genesis/Genesis 3#Genesis 3 . 5|Genesis 3:5]]), Christ "did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant" ([[Readable Bible/50 - Philippians/Philippians 2#Philippians 2 . 6|Philippians 2:6-7]]). The designation "whom I uphold" (*'etmāḵ-bô*) describes Father's sustaining throughout Christ's earthly ministry—strengthening in wilderness temptation ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 4#Matthew 4 . 11|Matthew 4:11]], angels ministered to Him), in Gethsemane ([[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 22#Luke 22 . 43|Luke 22:43]], angel strengthened Him), through crucifixion (sustained until declaring "It is finished," [[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 19#John 19 . 30|John 19:30]]). The declaration "my chosen" (*bəḥîrî*) establishes Christ's eternal election for redemptive mission. [[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 1#Ephesians 1 . 4|Ephesians 1:4]] declares believers chosen "in him before the foundation of the world"—Christ firstly chosen as covenant head, believers secondarily chosen in Him. The phrase "in whom my soul delights" (*rāṣəṯāh nap̄šî*) expresses Father's pleasure, declared audibly at baptism: "with whom I am well pleased" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 3#Matthew 3 . 17|Matthew 3:17]]), and transfiguration: "listen to him" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 17#Matthew 17 . 5|Matthew 17:5]]). This delight contrasts with Father's displeasure toward sin—Christ's perfect obedience satisfies divine justice. The Spirit's anointing "I have put my Spirit upon him" fulfills at Jesus' baptism when "he saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and coming to rest on him" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 3#Matthew 3 . 16|Matthew 3:16]]). Though Christ possessed Spirit eternally as God, His human nature received Spirit's empowerment for Messianic mission. Luke emphasizes Spirit's role: Jesus "full of the Holy Spirit" ([[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 4#Luke 4 . 1|Luke 4:1]]), "in the power of the Spirit" ([[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 4#Luke 4 . 14|Luke 4:14]]), declared "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me" ([[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 4#Luke 4 . 18|Luke 4:18]], quoting [[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 61#Isaiah 61 . 1|Isaiah 61:1]]). Every miracle performed, every demon cast out, every word spoken flowed from Spirit-empowerment. The mission "he will bring forth justice to the nations" (*mišpāṭ lagôyim yôṣî'*) defines Christ's global purpose. Though Jesus' earthly ministry focused on "lost sheep of the house of Israel" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 15#Matthew 15 . 24|Matthew 15:24]]), He consistently reached Gentiles: Syrophoenician woman's daughter healed ([[Readable Bible/41 - Mark/Mark 7#Mark 7 . 24|Mark 7:24-30]]), centurion's faith praised ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 8#Matthew 8 . 10|Matthew 8:10]]), Samaritans believing ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 4#John 4 . 39|John 4:39-42]]), Greeks seeking Him ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 12#John 12 . 20|John 12:20-21]]). His death accomplished justice cosmically—satisfying divine wrath ([[Readable Bible/45 - Romans/Romans 3#Romans 3 . 25|Romans 3:25-26]]), defeating Satan ([[Readable Bible/51 - Colossians/Colossians 2#Colossians 2 . 15|Colossians 2:15]]), reconciling all things ([[Readable Bible/51 - Colossians/Colossians 1#Colossians 1 . 20|Colossians 1:20]]). The Great Commission universalized mission: "make disciples of all nations" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 28#Matthew 28 . 19|Matthew 28:19]]). The gentle methodology—not crying aloud, not breaking bruised reeds, not quenching smoldering wicks—characterizes Jesus' entire ministry. Unlike militant Zealots seeking violent revolution, Jesus withdrew when crowds wanted to make Him king by force ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 6#John 6 . 15|John 6:15]]). Unlike harsh Pharisees crushing sinners under legal burdens ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 23#Matthew 23 . 4|Matthew 23:4]]), Jesus invited "all who labor and are heavy laden" to find rest ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 11#Matthew 11 . 28|Matthew 11:28]]). His encounters demonstrate this gentleness: to woman caught in adultery, "Neither do I condemn you; go, and sin no more" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 8#John 8 . 11|John 8:11]])—not condoning sin but extending grace; to Peter after denials, restoration not rejection ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 21#John 21 . 15|John 21:15-19]]); to doubting Thomas, patient demonstration not harsh rebuke ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 20#John 20 . 27|John 20:27]]). The bruised reeds Jesus preserved: paralytic lowered through roof ([[Readable Bible/41 - Mark/Mark 2#Mark 2 . 3|Mark 2:3-12]]), woman with hemorrhage touching garment ([[Readable Bible/41 - Mark/Mark 5#Mark 5 . 25|Mark 5:25-34]]), blind beggar Bartimaeus crying out ([[Readable Bible/41 - Mark/Mark 10#Mark 10 . 46|Mark 10:46-52]]), tax collectors and sinners eating with Him ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 9#Matthew 9 . 10|Matthew 9:10-13]]). The smoldering wicks He didn't quench: disciples' weak faith ("O you of little faith," [[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 6#Matthew 6 . 30|Matthew 6:30]], spoken correctively not condemningly), Peter's profession despite misunderstanding ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 16#Matthew 16 . 16|Matthew 16:16-23]]), disciples sleeping in Gethsemane ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 26#Matthew 26 . 40|Matthew 26:40-41]], "the spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak"). The assurance "faithfully bring forth justice" (*le'emeṯ yôṣî' mišpāṭ*) guarantees Christ's mission won't fail. Though crucifixion appeared as defeat, resurrection vindicated His righteousness. Ascension established Him at Father's right hand "far above all rule and authority and power and dominion" ([[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 1#Ephesians 1 . 21|Ephesians 1:21]]). Second coming will consummate justice when every knee bows, every tongue confesses Jesus is Lord ([[Readable Bible/50 - Philippians/Philippians 2#Philippians 2 . 10|Philippians 2:10-11]]). The perseverance "He will not grow faint or be discouraged" describes Christ's resolute obedience unto death. In Gethsemane, despite anguish producing sweat like drops of blood ([[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 22#Luke 22 . 44|Luke 22:44]]), He submitted: "not my will, but yours, be done" ([[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 22#Luke 22 . 42|Luke 22:42]]). On cross, enduring mockery, physical agony, spiritual abandonment, He persevered until declaring "It is finished" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 19#John 19 . 30|John 19:30]])—mission accomplished. [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 12#Hebrews 12 . 2|Hebrews 12:2]] describes Jesus "who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame"—sustained by eschatological vision of redeemed multitude. The scope "till he has established justice in the earth" awaits final fulfillment at parousia. Currently Christ reigns, subduing enemies progressively ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 15#1 Corinthians 15 . 25|1 Corinthians 15:25]]); ultimately "every island fled away, and no mountains were to be found" ([[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 16#Revelation 16 . 20|Revelation 16:20]])—total cosmic transformation. [[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 21#Revelation 21 . 1|Revelation 21:1]] declares "new heaven and a new earth"—justice fully established, righteousness dwelling ([[Readable Bible/61 - 2 Peter/2 Peter 3#2 Peter 3 . 13|2 Peter 3:13]]). The climactic promise "coastlands wait for his law" fulfills in gospel's global spread. Acts traces expansion from Jerusalem (Acts 1-7), to Judea/Samaria (Acts 8-9), to ends of earth (Acts 10-28)—Paul preaching in Rome, capital of Gentile world. Church history continues trajectory—gospel reaching Europe, Africa, Asia, Americas. [[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 7#Revelation 7 . 9|Revelation 7:9]] envisions "great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne"—coastlands' waiting satisfied in eschatological worship. The typological escalation: Isaiah's Servant establishes justice through teaching; Jesus establishes justice through atoning death. Isaiah's Servant receives Spirit's empowerment; Jesus eternally possesses Spirit as second person of Trinity, gives Spirit to church ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 20#John 20 . 22|John 20:22]]; [[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 2#Acts 2 . 1|Acts 2:1-4]]). Isaiah's Servant preserves bruised reeds; Jesus transforms them into "living stones" building spiritual house ([[Readable Bible/60 - 1 Peter/1 Peter 2#1 Peter 2 . 5|1 Peter 2:5]]). Isaiah's Servant won't grow faint; Jesus conquered death itself, now lives forever to intercede ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 7#Hebrews 7 . 25|Hebrews 7:25]]). The identification: Jesus IS Yahweh's Servant—chosen before creation, delighted in eternally, Spirit-filled perfectly, mission-accomplishing certainly, globally-worshiped ultimately. Every prophecy fulfilled, every promise accomplished, every expectation surpassed in Christ who embodies Isaiah's Servant perfectly, taking form of slave ([[Readable Bible/50 - Philippians/Philippians 2#Philippians 2 . 7|Philippians 2:7]]) to serve redemptive mission, making Himself obedient to point of death on cross ([[Readable Bible/50 - Philippians/Philippians 2#Philippians 2 . 8|Philippians 2:8]]), therefore highly exalted receiving name above every name ([[Readable Bible/50 - Philippians/Philippians 2#Philippians 2 . 9|Philippians 2:9]])—from humiliation to exaltation, from suffering to glory, from death to eternal life, Jesus fulfills Isaiah's Servant completely.
