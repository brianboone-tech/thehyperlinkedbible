**Context:**
Revelation 21:1-4 describes the eschatological consummation—new heavens and new earth where God dwells eternally with redeemed humanity, abolishing all consequences of fall. This vision climaxes Revelation's apocalyptic narrative tracing history from John's first-century context (chapters 1-3: seven churches) through tribulation judgments (chapters 4-19: seals, trumpets, bowls) to Christ's return and millennial kingdom (chapter 20) culminating in eternal state (chapters 21-22). The immediate context: Revelation 20 narrates Satan's final rebellion and defeat (vv. 7-10), great white throne judgment (vv. 11-15) where "Death and Hades were thrown into the lake of fire" (v. 14), and "if anyone's name was not found written in the book of life, he was thrown into the lake of fire" (v. 15). This final judgment separates righteous from wicked eternally, preparing for new creation. Chapter 21 opens: "Then I saw (*eidon*) a new (*kainon*) heaven and a new (*kainēn*) earth, for the first (*prōtos*) heaven and the first (*prōtē*) earth had passed away (*parēlthan*), and the sea was no more" (v. 1). The adjective *kainos* ("new") emphasizes qualitative novelty—not merely renovated but fundamentally transformed. Isaiah 65:17 and 66:22 prophesied: "For behold, I create new heavens and a new earth, and the former things shall not be remembered or come into mind"—promise John sees fulfilled. The phrase "passed away" (*parēlthan*, aorist active of *parerchomai*) indicates complete disappearance—not annihilation into non-existence but transformation beyond recognition. 2 Peter 3:10-13 explains: "the heavens will pass away with a roar, and the heavenly bodies will be burned up and dissolved... But according to his promise we are waiting for new heavens and a new earth in which righteousness dwells." The statement "the sea was no more" symbolically significant: in ancient thought, sea represented chaos (Genesis 1:2, "darkness over face of the deep"), danger (Jonah's storm), separation (exiled John on Patmos), evil (Revelation 13:1, beast from sea). New creation eliminates chaos, danger, separation—complete order, safety, unity. Verse 2 continues: "And I saw the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband." The "holy city" combines architectural (city—polis) and covenantal (Jerusalem—covenant capital) imagery. Yet this Jerusalem descends from heaven—not earthly rebuilding but divine gift. The bridal imagery recalls Old Testament marriage metaphor (Isaiah 54:5-6; 62:4-5; Ezekiel 16:8-14; Hosea 2:19-20) where God marries Israel. Revelation applies to church—"wife of the Lamb" (19:7; 21:9). The preparation "as bride adorned" emphasizes beauty, purity, readiness—church glorified, spotless (Ephesians 5:27). Verse 3 announces theological culmination: "And I heard a loud voice from the throne saying, 'Behold, the dwelling place (*skēnē*) of God is with man (*meta tōn anthrōpōn*). He will dwell (*skēnōsei*) with them, and they will be his people (*laoi autou*), and God himself will be with them as their God (*ho theos met' autōn estai*).'" This fulfills Leviticus 26:11-12: "I will make my dwelling among you... I will walk among you and will be your God, and you shall be my people"—covenant formula consummated. The verb *skēnoō* ("to tabernacle") recalls John 1:14: "the Word became flesh and dwelt [*eskēnōsen*, aorist of same verb] among us"—incarnation foreshadowed eternal indwelling. The plural "peoples" (*laoi*, some manuscripts) suggests multiethnic composition—"from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages" (7:9). Verse 4 describes blessings: "He will wipe away (*exaleipsei*) every tear (*pan dakryon*) from their eyes, and death (*thanatos*) shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning (*penthos*) nor crying (*kraugē*) nor pain (*ponos*) anymore, for the former things (*ta prōta*) have passed away (*parēlthan*)." Each negative ("no more") abolishes fall's consequences: death (Genesis 2:17; Romans 5:12), mourning (grief over death), crying (distress), pain (childbirth curse Genesis 3:16, general suffering). Isaiah 25:8 prophesied: "He will swallow up death forever; and the Lord GOD will wipe away tears from all faces"—fulfilled eschatologically. Isaiah 35:10 promised: "sorrow and sighing shall flee away"—now realized. The comprehensive negation—"former things passed away"—indicates complete discontinuity with cursed present order. The theological significance: redemptive history's goal isn't disembodied heavenly existence but renewed material creation where God dwells bodily with resurrected humanity—fulfilling creation's original purpose (Genesis 1-2) and surpassing it (greater intimacy, confirmed righteousness, death-proof glory).

**Ninefold Analysis:**

**OT Context:**
Isaiah's prophecies of new heavens and new earth provide foundational vision for Revelation 21. Isaiah 65:17-25 announces: "For behold, I create new heavens and a new earth, and the former things shall not be remembered or come into mind. But be glad and rejoice forever in that which I create; for behold, I create Jerusalem to be a joy, and her people to be a gladness. I will rejoice in Jerusalem and be glad in my people; no more shall be heard in it the sound of weeping and the cry of distress" (vv. 17-19). This follows judgment oracles (chapters 1-39) and comfort proclamations (chapters 40-66), culminating in eschatological hope. The context: exilic/post-exilic community experiencing disappointment—returned from Babylon but temple modest (Haggai 2:3), political subjugation continued, prophetic promises seemingly unfulfilled. Isaiah addresses this eschatological tension: present restoration (538 BC return from exile) partially fulfills, yet ultimate consummation awaits divine intervention creating qualitatively new reality. Isaiah 66:22 reinforces: "For as the new heavens and the new earth that I make shall remain before me, says the LORD, so shall your offspring and your name remain"—permanence contrasting temporary present order. The Levitical covenant formula—"I will be their God, they shall be my people" (Leviticus 26:12)—established tabernacle/temple theology: God dwelling among His people symbolized by glory-cloud filling sanctuary (Exodus 40:34-35; 1 Kings 8:10-11). Yet sin repeatedly caused God's glory to depart (Ezekiel 10:18-19; 11:22-23)—exile as ultimate departure. Ezekiel 37:26-28 promises restoration: "I will make a covenant of peace with them. It shall be an everlasting covenant with them. And I will set them in their land and multiply them, and will set my sanctuary in their midst forevermore. My dwelling place shall be with them, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people." Yet historical return from exile never achieved this fully—anticipating eschatological fulfillment. Isaiah 25:8 prophesies death's abolition: "He will swallow up death forever; and the Lord GOD will wipe away tears from all faces, and the reproach of his people he will take away from all the earth"—language Revelation 21:4 directly echoes. The canonical trajectory: creation (Genesis 1-2: original perfection) → fall (Genesis 3: curse introduced) → redemptive history (Exodus-Malachi: God working through Israel) → prophetic hope (Isaiah, Ezekiel: new creation promised) → NT fulfillment (Gospels: Christ inaugurates Kingdom) → consummation (Revelation: Kingdom fully come). The OT thus provides both language (new heavens/earth, wiping tears, dwelling with people) and theological framework (covenant faithfulness, resurrection hope, restored creation) for Revelation's climactic vision.

**Jewish Backgrounds:**
Jewish eschatology developed diverse expectations regarding world's future. Pharisees affirmed bodily resurrection, eternal life, final judgment (Josephus, Antiquities 18.14); Sadducees denied afterlife, focusing on Torah observance in present (Acts 23:8). Apocalyptic literature (Daniel, intertestamental writings like 1 Enoch, 2 Baruch, 4 Ezra) envisioned cosmic transformation—present evil age giving way to age to come (*'olam ha-ba*). The Dead Sea Scrolls (Qumran) anticipated War Scroll's final battle between Sons of Light and Sons of Darkness, culminating in divine victory establishing eternal righteousness. Rabbinic tradition (Mishnah, Talmud) debated resurrection details: Would resurrected bodies be physical? Would they eat/drink? Maimonides (12th century, Mishneh Torah) affirmed bodily resurrection but believed ultimate state would be purely spiritual—World to Come (*'olam ha-ba*) as disembodied souls enjoying intellectual communion with God. Against this, Christian teaching (following Jesus, 1 Corinthians 15, Revelation 21-22) emphasized permanent embodied existence in renewed creation—not escaping materiality but transforming it. Jewish texts rarely envisioned new heavens/earth; Isaiah's language often interpreted metaphorically (renewed Israel, restored temple) rather than cosmic recreation. The concept of God dwelling permanently with humanity challenged transcendence emphasis in later Judaism—maintaining divine holiness by spatial/conceptual separation. Yet biblical theology consistently affirmed God's desire for intimate presence: Eden's walking with Adam (Genesis 3:8), tabernacle's glory-cloud, temple's divine residence—all anticipating ultimate consummation. Messianic expectation varied: some anticipated Davidic king restoring Israel politically (Psalms of Solomon 17); others expected priestly Messiah (Qumran); few envisioned suffering servant dying for sins (despite Isaiah 53). Christian identification of Jesus as Messiah who dies, rises, ascends, returns to consummate Kingdom struck most Jews as implausible—contributing to rejection. Revelation's vision synthesizes themes: Davidic Messiah (Lion of Judah, 5:5), slain Lamb (sacrificial atonement, 5:6), returning King (19:11-16), eternal dwelling (21:3)—showing Christianity's Jewish roots while transcending contemporary Jewish categories through Christological fulfillment.

**Text Form:**
Revelation 21:1's syntax: *Kai eidon ouranon kainon kai gēn kainēn*—"And I saw heaven new and earth new." The conjunction *kai* continues visionary sequence throughout Revelation (*kai eidon*, "and I saw," occurs repeatedly). The aorist *eidon* (from *horaō*, "to see") indicates John's prophetic vision—not speculation but revealed reality. The adjective *kainos* ("new") precedes nouns for emphasis—quality, not merely temporal sequence. The explanatory *gar* clause ("for the first heaven and first earth passed away") provides rationale—new necessary because old ended. The additional statement "and the sea was no more" (*kai hē thalassa ouk estin eti*) employs emphatic negation—present tense *estin* with negative *ouk* plus adverb *eti* ("no longer, not anymore") stresses permanent absence. Verse 2's structure: *kai tēn polin tēn hagian Ierousalēm kainēn eidon katabainousan ek tou ouranou apo tou theou*—"and the city the holy, Jerusalem new, I saw descending out of heaven from God." The dual accusatives (*polin*/*Ierousalēm*) create apposition—city identified as Jerusalem. The participle *katabainousan* (present active of *katabainō*, "descending") suggests continuous action—ongoing descent or sustained state of having descended. The prepositional phrases (*ek tou ouranou*, "out of heaven"; *apo tou theou*, "from God") specify origin—divine gift, not human construction. The simile "prepared as bride adorned for her husband" employs perfect passive participle *hētoimasmenēn* ("having been prepared")—completed preparation with ongoing resultant state—plus perfect passive participle *kekosmēmenēn* ("having been adorned")—beauty resulting from deliberate arrangement. Verse 3's proclamation: *kai ēkousa phōnēs megalēs ek tou thronou legousēs*—"and I heard voice great from the throne saying." The genitive *phōnēs* governed by *ēkousa* (aorist of *akouō*, "hear") with partitive sense—hearing something characterized as "voice." The prepositional phrase *ek tou thronou* locates source—God's throne. The quotation employs demonstrative *idou* ("behold") drawing attention, followed by nominative *hē skēnē tou theou* ("the tabernacle of God") as subject. The statement *meta tōn anthrōpōn* ("with the humans") specifies location—among humanity. The future *skēnōsei* ("he will tabernacle") promises coming reality. The coordinated clauses *kai autoi laoi autou esontai* ("and they peoples of-him will-be") plus *kai autos ho theos met' autōn estai* ("and himself the God with them will-be") employ future *eimi* creating parallelism emphasizing reciprocal covenant relationship. Verse 4's promises employ future indicatives: *exaleipsei* ("he will wipe away"), then negations with future *estai*—*thanatos ouk estai eti* ("death not will-be any-longer"); *oute penthos oute kraugē oute ponos ouk estai eti* ("neither mourning nor crying nor pain not will-be any-longer"). The emphatic compound negation (*ouk... eti*, "not... any-longer") reinforces permanence. The climactic explanation *hoti ta prōta parēlthan* ("because the first-things passed-away") uses aorist *parēlthan* indicating completed disappearance—old order definitively ended enabling new order.

**Hermeneutical Use:**
Revelation 21's interpretation requires recognizing apocalyptic genre, Hebraic imagery, and canonical intertextuality. John draws extensively from OT—Isaiah's new creation, Ezekiel's temple vision (chapters 40-48), Zechariah's renewed Jerusalem—reconfiguring through Christological lens. The hermeneutical question: How literally should imagery be interpreted? Hyper-literalism produces confusion (cube-shaped city with streets and gates suggests architectural impossibility, 21:16-21); pure symbolism risks evacuating concrete hope. The balance: imagery depicts real future realities transcending present conceptual categories—John uses earthly analogies (city, bride, temple) to describe heavenly realities exceeding full comprehension. The new heaven/earth debate: Renovation or replacement? The verb *parerchomai* ("pass away") could suggest annihilation-replacement or transformation-renewal. Romans 8:19-21 favors renovation: "creation itself will be set free from its bondage to corruption"—implies continuity. 2 Peter 3:10-13's fire-language could indicate purification rather than obliteration. The practical implication: New creation vindicates materiality—bodies matter, earth matters, culture matters—opposing Gnostic escape-from-matter theology. The "no more sea" interpretation: Literal (no oceans on new earth—hydrological cycle altered?) or symbolic (chaos/evil eliminated)? Given Revelation's symbolic use (13:1, beast from sea; 17:15, waters representing peoples), likely primarily symbolic while not excluding literal implications. The bride-city imagery merges: New Jerusalem = bride = church (21:2, 9-10). This corporate solidarity—redeemed community collectively forming God's dwelling—reflects ancient Near Eastern thought where king's palace and city unified. The hermeneutical trajectory: Promise (Isaiah 65) → Inauguration (Christ's first advent establishing Kingdom) → Intermediate fulfillment (church as temple, 1 Corinthians 3:16) → Consummation (Revelation 21, full dwelling). This already-not yet tension characterizes NT eschatology—Kingdom present spiritually, awaiting physical manifestation.

**Theological Use:**
Revelation 21:1-4 establishes eschatological doctrines: (1) **New creation**—not merely renovated but qualitatively transformed, surpassing original creation's glory. This grounds Christian ecology—earth's destiny isn't destruction but glorification, motivating stewardship. (2) **Bodily resurrection**—eternal state involves physical bodies in material creation, not disembodied souls in ethereal heaven. This validates embodiment against Platonic dualism. (3) **Beatific vision**—"God himself will be with them" (v. 3)—direct unmediated communion, fulfilling Psalm 16:11, "in your presence there is fullness of joy." This is salvation's goal—knowing God (John 17:3). (4) **Covenant consummation**—"they will be his people, and God himself will be their God" (v. 3) fulfills Levitical promise (26:12), Jeremiah's new covenant (31:33), establishing eternal relationship. (5) **Death's abolition**—"death shall be no more" (v. 4) accomplishes what Christ's resurrection inaugurated (1 Corinthians 15:26, "last enemy destroyed is death"). This conquers humanity's ultimate fear. (6) **Suffering's end**—"no more mourning nor crying nor pain" (v. 4) reverses fall's curse (Genesis 3:16-19), vindica God's goodness against problem of evil. Theodicy's resolution: present suffering is temporary, future glory eternal (Romans 8:18). (7) **Universal redemption**—new creation implies cosmic redemption, not merely human salvation. Creation's groaning (Romans 8:22) ends; all things reconciled (Colossians 1:20). (8) **Eternal security**—"former things passed away" (v. 4) precludes reversal—no second fall possible. Confirmed righteousness, death-proof immortality characterize glorified state. (9) **Progressive eschatology**—not cyclical (Greek eternal return) but teleological (moving toward predetermined goal). History has meaning, direction, consummation. This grounds Christian hope during suffering—present trials "light momentary affliction preparing eternal weight of glory" (2 Corinthians 4:17).

**Rhetorical Use:**
Revelation 21's rhetoric moves readers from present suffering to future glory, from earthly groaning to heavenly rejoicing. John's repeated "I saw" (*eidon*) creates vicarious participation—readers envision with him. The cumulative imagery—new heaven, new earth, holy city, bride, God's dwelling—overwhelms with beauty, evoking wonder. The throne-voice's proclamation (v. 3) uses demonstrative *idou* ("behold!") arresting attention, followed by staccato declarations creating anticipation. The fourfold negation in verse 4—"death... mourning... crying... pain... no more"—systematically eliminates fall's consequences, each negation landing with force. Ancient audiences facing persecution (Nero, Domitian) found comfort: present suffering temporary, future glory certain. Modern readers experiencing pain, loss, injustice receive same assurance. The bridal imagery (v. 2) creates emotional resonance—wedding joy, beauty, purity, celebration—applied to church's destiny. Women in patriarchal cultures, viewing marriage as life's pinnacle, found validation; men understood covenantal commitment. The rhetorical question implied: If present reality includes suffering, and future reality excludes all suffering, how should believers endure? Answer: "I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory that is to be revealed to us" (Romans 8:18). The rhetoric aims not escapism but perseverance—grounding present obedience in future hope. The architectural imagery (city with foundations, Hebrews 11:10) creates stability—permanent, secure, unshakable—contrasting nomadic wandering. Abraham "lived in tents" (Hebrews 11:9) seeking better country; believers receive that country (Hebrews 11:16). The overall rhetorical movement: Vision (what John saw) → Proclamation (what heaven declares) → Application (therefore endure). This sequence transforms suffering's meaning—not purposeless pain but birth-pangs preceding new creation (Romans 8:22).

**Christological Connection:**
[[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 21#Revelation 21 . 1|Revelation 21:1-4]] consummates redemptive work accomplished by Jesus Christ, whose incarnation, death, resurrection, ascension, and return culminate in new creation where He reigns eternally. Every element traces to Christ's person and work. The "new heaven and new earth" fulfills Christ's proclamation: "Behold, I am making all things new" ([[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 21#Revelation 21 . 5|Revelation 21:5]])—Christ personally creates new order, exercising divine prerogative ([[Readable Bible/51 - Colossians/Colossians 1#Colossians 1 . 16|Colossians 1:16]], "by him all things were created"). The old order's passing results from Christ's victory: death defeated through resurrection ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 15#1 Corinthians 15 . 54|1 Corinthians 15:54-57]]), Satan bound through crucifixion ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 12#John 12 . 31|John 12:31]]), curse reversed through atonement ([[Readable Bible/48 - Galatians/Galatians 3#Galatians 3 . 13|Galatians 3:13]]). The new Jerusalem descending from heaven represents church as Christ's bride ([[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 5#Ephesians 5 . 25|Ephesians 5:25-27]]). Christ loved church, gave Himself for her, sanctifies and cleanses her, presents her glorious, without spot or wrinkle—perfectly pure. [[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 19#Revelation 19 . 7|Revelation 19:7-9]] describes marriage supper of Lamb—Christ and church united eternally. The city's descent from God (v. 2) indicates Christ's mediatorial role—presenting redeemed community to Father ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 15#1 Corinthians 15 . 24|1 Corinthians 15:24-28]]), yet remaining eternally united with her. The dwelling of God with man (v. 3) fulfills Immanuel ("God with us," [[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 1#Matthew 1 . 23|Matthew 1:23]]). Christ's incarnation pioneered permanent divine dwelling in humanity—[[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 1#John 1 . 14|John 1:14]], "Word became flesh and dwelt [*eskēnōsen*, tabernacled] among us." What incarnation began, new creation consummates—God eternally dwelling bodily with humanity through Christ's permanent assumption of human nature. The covenant formula "they will be his people, God will be their God" (v. 3) achieves through Christ's mediatorial work—new covenant established by His blood ([[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 22#Luke 22 . 20|Luke 22:20]]), applying [[Readable Bible/24 - Jeremiah/Jeremiah 31#Jeremiah 31 . 31|Jeremiah 31:31-34]]'s promise. Christ is covenant head ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 8#Hebrews 8 . 6|Hebrews 8:6]], mediator of better covenant), securing relationship between God and redeemed. The wiping away of tears (v. 4) reflects Christ's compassion—Jesus wept at Lazarus's tomb ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 11#John 11 . 35|John 11:35]]), showing identification with human sorrow. His resurrection conquered death producing tear-source, His return will eliminate tears forever. The abolition of death (v. 4) accomplishes what Christ's resurrection inaugurated—"death is swallowed up in victory" ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 15#1 Corinthians 15 . 54|1 Corinthians 15:54]], quoting [[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 25#Isaiah 25 . 8|Isaiah 25:8]]). Christ destroyed death's power ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 2#Hebrews 2 . 14|Hebrews 2:14]], "through death destroyed the one who has the power of death"), holds death's keys ([[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 1#Revelation 1 . 18|Revelation 1:18]]), will cast death into lake of fire ([[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 20#Revelation 20 . 14|Revelation 20:14]]). The end of mourning, crying, pain (v. 4) reverses curse Christ bore—"man of sorrows acquainted with grief" ([[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 53#Isaiah 53 . 3|Isaiah 53:3]]), experiencing ultimate pain on cross. His vicarious suffering purchased freedom from suffering—"by his wounds you have been healed" ([[Readable Bible/60 - 1 Peter/1 Peter 2#1 Peter 2 . 24|1 Peter 2:24]]). The promise "former things passed away" (v. 4) fulfills [[Readable Bible/47 - 2 Corinthians/2 Corinthians 5#2 Corinthians 5 . 17|2 Corinthians 5:17]]: "if anyone is in Christ, he is new creation. The old has passed away; behold, the new has come"—realized individually at conversion, corporately at consummation. Christ is firstfruits ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 15#1 Corinthians 15 . 20|1 Corinthians 15:20]], 23)—His resurrection guarantees ours, His glorification ensures ours, His dwelling with Father secures ours. The Christological pattern: Incarnation (taking flesh) → Atonement (bearing curse) → Resurrection (conquering death) → Ascension (entering heaven) → Intercession (preparing place, [[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 14#John 14 . 2|John 14:2-3]]) → Return (bringing Kingdom fully) → Consummation (dwelling eternally). At each stage, Christ accomplishes elements necessary for Revelation 21's fulfillment. The final vision shows Christ central—Revelation 21-22 depicts city whose temple is "the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb" (21:22), whose light is "the glory of God" with "lamp the Lamb" (21:23), whose throne is "throne of God and of the Lamb" (22:1, 3). Christ's eternality as God guarantees new creation's eternality; Christ's humanity glorified previews redeemed humanity's destiny. Isaiah's vision of new creation finds ultimate fulfillment not merely in restored cosmos but in Christ-centered cosmos where Lamb once slain reigns eternally, sharing throne with Father, receiving worship from every creature, illuminating city with uncreated light, serving as temple where God dwells immediately with humanity. The typological escalation: Eden (temporary paradise, lost through disobedience) → Israel (partial theocracy, mixed obedience) → Christ (perfect obedience, securing redemption) → New Jerusalem (eternal paradise, confirmed righteousness, death-proof immortality). Where Isaiah prophesied God creating new heavens/earth, Revelation reveals Christ accomplishes this—He who created original ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 1#John 1 . 3|John 1:3]], "all things made through him") recreates final, He who sustains universe by word of power ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 1#Hebrews 1 . 3|Hebrews 1:3]]) transforms it into eternal dwelling, He who conquered death eliminates it, He who bore curse reverses it, He who descended (incarnation) causes city to descend (new Jerusalem), He who tabernacled temporarily ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 1#John 1 . 14|John 1:14]]) enables God to tabernacle permanently ([[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 21#Revelation 21 . 3|Revelation 21:3]]), accomplishing Isaiah's vision through His vicarious suffering, victorious resurrection, and glorious return—consummating redemptive history in new creation where Christ reigns eternally with Father and Spirit, surrounded by redeemed humanity from every nation, dwelling in perfect righteousness, experiencing unmediated communion, enjoying eternal life, worshiping perpetually, reigning forever—fulfilling all prophecies, accomplishing all purposes, satisfying all longings, vindicating all suffering, answering all questions, healing all wounds, wiping all tears, abolishing all death, establishing all righteousness, consummating all history in Christ who makes all things new.
