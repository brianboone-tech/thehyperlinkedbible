**Context:**
1 Peter 2:24-25 applies Isaiah 53's Suffering Servant prophecy to Christ's crucifixion, explaining atonement's mechanism and believers' resulting transformation. The epistle addresses Christians scattered throughout Asia Minor (1:1, "elect exiles of the Dispersion")—likely Jewish and Gentile believers facing persecution and social marginalization. Peter writes approximately AD 62-64 from Rome ("Babylon," 5:13—cryptic designation for imperial capital) shortly before Nero's intensified persecution (AD 64-68). The immediate context (2:18-25) addresses Christian slaves enduring unjust suffering, exhorting patient endurance following Christ's example. Verse 18 commands: "Servants, be subject to your masters with all respect, not only to the good and gentle but also to the unjust"—counter-cultural instruction in Greco-Roman world where slaves had no rights, frequently suffered abuse. Verses 19-20 distinguish suffering types: "For this is a gracious thing, when, mindful of God, one endures sorrows while suffering unjustly... But if when you do good and suffer for it you endure, this is a gracious thing in the sight of God." Peter elevates undeserved suffering patiently borne as "grace" (*charis*)—divine favor, not merely stoic resignation. Verse 21 establishes christological pattern: "For to this you have been called, because Christ also suffered for you, leaving you an example (*hypogrammon*), so that you might follow in his steps." The term *hypogrammos* ("example, pattern, model") originally described writing template students traced—Christ's suffering provides pattern believers follow. Yet verses 22-25 clarify Christ's suffering differs qualitatively: He suffered vicariously (for us), we suffer conformationally (following His pattern). Verse 22 quotes Isaiah 53:9: "He committed no sin, neither was deceit found in his mouth"—establishing Christ's sinlessness, prerequisite for substitutionary atonement. Only sinless one can atone for sinners. Verse 23 describes Christ's response to mistreatment: "When he was reviled, he did not revile in return; when he suffered, he did not threaten, but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly." Christ's silent submission models faithful response to injustice—trusting divine vindication rather than human retaliation. Verses 24-25 shift from example to atonement, explaining Christ's suffering's redemptive purpose. Verse 24a states mechanism: "He himself bore our sins in his body on the tree"—vicarious sin-bearing through crucifixion. The intensive pronoun *autos* ("himself") emphasizes Christ personally, not delegating. The verb *anēnenken* (aorist active of *anapherō*, "to bear up, carry up") employs sacrificial language—priests "bore up" offerings on altar (Leviticus 14:20; Hebrews 7:27). Christ simultaneously priest offering sacrifice and lamb being sacrificed. The location "in his body" (*tō sōmati autou*) specifies incarnate human nature—not abstract atonement but physical death. The preposition *epi* ("on, upon") plus accusative "tree" (*to xylon*) indicates location—wooden cross. The term *xylon* (literally "wood, tree") references Deuteronomy 21:23: "a hanged man is cursed by God"—crucifixion incurs covenant curse. Galatians 3:13 applies: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us—for it is written, 'Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree.'" Verse 24b states purpose (dual *hina* clauses): "that (*hina*) we might die to sin and live to righteousness"—forensic justification (penalty paid) and moral transformation (new life). The participial phrase "having died" (*apogenomenoi*, aorist middle participle of *apoginomai*) describes definitive past separation from sin's dominion. Believers united to Christ share His death (Romans 6:3-4), breaking sin's mastery. The result: "live to righteousness" (*tē dikaiosynē zēsōmen*)—positively pursuing holiness, not merely avoiding sin. Verse 24c quotes Isaiah 53:5b: "By his wounds (*tō mōlōpi autou*) you have been healed (*iathēte*)." The singular *mōlōps* (bruise, welt, wound) may represent collective wounds or emphasize unity of suffering. The verb *iathēte* (aorist passive of *iaomai*) indicates completed healing—definitive past event with ongoing effects. The "healing" primarily spiritual (justification, reconciliation) though potentially including eschatological physical restoration. Verse 25 employs shepherd metaphor: "For you were straying like sheep (*ēte hōs probata planōmenoi*), but have now returned (*epestraphēte*) to the Shepherd (*ton poimena*) and Overseer (*episkopon*) of your souls (*tōn psychōn hymōn*)." The imperfect *ēte* ("you were") describes past continuous state—persistent wandering. The verb *planōmenoi* (present passive participle of *planaō*, "lead astray, wander") echoes Isaiah 53:6, "All we like sheep have gone astray." The contrasting aorist *epestraphēte* ("you returned, were converted") marks decisive past conversion. The titles "Shepherd" and "Overseer" combine pastoral care (shepherd tends flock) with authoritative supervision (overseer guards, directs). The genitive "of your souls" specifies Christ's care extends beyond physical to spiritual, beyond temporal to eternal.

**Ninefold Analysis:**

**OT Context:**
Isaiah 53:4-6, 9 provides prophetic foundation for 1 Peter 2:24-25's interpretation. The fourth Servant Song (Isaiah 52:13-53:12) portrays Messiah's vicarious suffering with unprecedented detail. Isaiah prophesied approximately 700 BC, yet described future events with precise accuracy—Servant's rejection (53:3), silent submission (53:7), substitutionary death (53:5-6), burial with rich (53:9), resurrection (53:10-11), exaltation (52:13; 53:12). The passage's original setting: Babylonian exile's end (539 BC), yet full fulfillment awaited Messiah's advent. Verse 4 states: "Surely he has borne (*nāśā'*) our griefs (*ḥŏlāyēnû*) and carried (*səḇālām*) our sorrows (*maḵ'ōḇēynû*)"—establishing vicarious burden-bearing. The Hebrew *ḥŏlî* encompasses both physical sickness and spiritual malady; *maḵ'ōḇ* means pain, sorrow, anguish. Verse 5 clarifies spiritual dimension: "But he was pierced (*məḥōlāl*) for our transgressions (*mippəšā'ēnû*); he was crushed (*məḏukkā'*) for our iniquities (*mē'ăwōnōṯēnû*); upon him was the chastisement (*mûsār*) that brought us peace (*šəlômēnû*), and with his wounds (*baḥăḇurāṯô*) we are healed (*nirpā'-lānû*)." Every phrase emphasizes substitution—preposition *min* ("for") indicates in-place-of. He suffers what we deserve; we receive healing we don't deserve. The nouns *peša'* ("transgression, rebellion") and *'āwōn* ("iniquity, guilt") denote willful moral sin against God's law—not mere mistakes but covenant violations. The result: *šālôm* ("peace, wholeness")—reconciliation with God, restoration of broken relationship. The climactic confession (v. 6): "All we like sheep have gone astray (*tā'înû*); we have turned—every one—to his own way (*larkeqō pānînû*); and the LORD has laid on him (*hiṗgîa' bô*) the iniquity (*'ăwōn*) of us all (*kullānû*)." The Hiphil verb *hiṗgîa'* indicates causative action—Yahweh actively, judicially imputes humanity's collective sin to Servant. This establishes penal substitutionary atonement: divine justice satisfied through substitute bearing penalty. Verse 9's declaration—"he had done no violence (*ḥāmās*), and there was no deceit (*mirmāh*) in his mouth"—establishes Servant's sinlessness prerequisite for atonement. Only perfect sacrifice can atone for imperfect sinners. The NT applies Isaiah 53 extensively to Christ: Philip explains to Ethiopian eunuch (Acts 8:32-35), Matthew connects to healing ministry (8:17), Paul summarizes gospel (1 Corinthians 15:3, "Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures"), Peter quotes directly (1 Peter 2:22-25). The Hebrew *ḥăḇurāh* ("wound, stripe, blow") becomes Greek *mōlōps* ("bruise, welt")—Peter recognizing Christ's Roman flogging (Matthew 27:26) and crucifixion wounds fulfilled prophecy.

**Jewish Backgrounds:**
Jewish interpretation varied: Targum Jonathan spiritualized, avoiding suffering Messiah implications; LXX translated toward sin-bearing; rabbinic tradition debated corporate Israel vs. individual Messiah; Dead Sea Scrolls preserved text; early Christianity unanimously identified Jesus as fulfillment.

**Text Form:**
1 Peter 2:24's syntax employs relative pronoun *hos* introducing participial construction: *hos... tas hamartias hēmōn autos anēnenken en tō sōmati autou epi to xylon*—"who... our sins himself bore up in his body on the tree." The intensive *autos* emphasizes Christ personally acting. The verb *anēnenken* (aorist active of *anapherō*) uses sacrificial vocabulary—bearing offerings upward to God. Dual *hina* clauses express purpose: negative (die to sin) and positive (live to righteousness). The quotation of Isaiah 53:5b employs singular *mōlōps* (wound) for collective suffering. Verse 25's shepherd metaphor uses perfect *epestraphēte* indicating completed conversion with ongoing effects.

**Hermeneutical Use:**
Peter interprets Isaiah 53 as direct Christological prophecy fulfilled in crucifixion. The hermeneutic: typological fulfillment—what Isaiah foresaw, Christ accomplished. The application extends from Christ's example (vv. 21-23) to Christ's atonement (v. 24)—showing qualitative difference between His vicarious suffering and believers' conformational suffering. The quotation method: selective excerpts arranged thematically rather than sequential—verse 22 (Isa 53:9), verse 24 (Isa 53:4-5, 12), verse 25 (Isa 53:6). This demonstrates apostolic freedom in applying OT prophetically while maintaining textual integrity.

**Theological Use:**
1 Peter 2:24-25 establishes foundational doctrines: (1) **Penal substitutionary atonement**—Christ bore penalty we deserved; (2) **Imputation**—our sin credited to Him, His righteousness credited to us; (3) **Definitive sanctification**—died to sin (positional holiness); (4) **Progressive sanctification**—live to righteousness (practical holiness); (5) **Spiritual healing**—"by his wounds you have been healed" primarily soteriological, secondarily physical; (6) **Conversion**—from wandering (rebellion) to returning (repentance); (7) **Divine shepherd—Christ tends, protects, guides souls eternally.

**Rhetorical Use:**
Peter's rhetoric moves from exhortation (endure unjust suffering) through Christological exposition (Christ's atonement) to application (you've been converted). The shepherd metaphor creates emotional resonance—readers are vulnerable sheep needing care, finding security in Christ the Shepherd. The contrast between past (straying) and present (returned) assures transformed identity grounding ethical imperatives.

**Christological Connection:**
[[Readable Bible/60 - 1 Peter/1 Peter 2#1 Peter 2 . 24|1 Peter 2:24-25]] establishes Jesus Christ as Isaiah's Suffering Servant who vicariously bore sin through crucifixion, accomplishing comprehensive redemption. Every phrase reveals Christ's substitutionary work. "He himself bore our sins"—Christ actively assumed sin-burden not His own, becoming sin (2 Cor 5:21) though personally sinless. "In his body"—incarnation essential; only human nature can die, only sinless human can atone. "On the tree"—crucifixion specifically, bearing covenant curse (Gal 3:13, quoting Deut 21:23). The purpose: believers die to sin's dominion (Rom 6:6-7, old self crucified with Christ), live to righteousness (2 Cor 5:15, "he died for all, that those who live might no longer live for themselves"). "By his wounds you have been healed"—Christ's physical suffering (flogging, crucifixion) accomplished spiritual healing (justification, reconciliation). Isaiah's plural *ḥăḇurōṯ* ("wounds") becomes Peter's singular *mōlōps* potentially emphasizing unity of Christ's passion or collective wounds viewed as one redemptive act. The healing primarily spiritual now, ultimately physical at resurrection (Rev 21:4). The shepherd imagery identifies Christ as Ezekiel's promised divine Shepherd (Ezek 34:11-16) and Davidic king (Ezek 34:23-24). Jesus claimed: "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 10#John 10 . 11|John 10:11]])—voluntary sacrifice, not martyrdom. Where Israel's shepherds (leaders) failed—exploiting flock (Ezek 34:2-6), Jesus faithfully tends—"I know my own and my own know me" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 10#John 10 . 14|John 10:14]]). The title "Overseer" (*episkopos*) combines pastoral care with authoritative supervision—Christ guards souls eternally ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 10#John 10 . 28|John 10:28-29]], "no one will snatch them out of my hand"). The trajectory: Christ the Lamb (sacrificed for sins, [[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 1#John 1 . 29|John 1:29]]) becomes Christ the Shepherd (caring for redeemed, [[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 10#John 10 . 11|John 10:11]]; Heb 13:20; Rev 7:17)—showing atonement's goal isn't merely forensic transaction but restoration of relationship, gathering scattered sheep into one flock under one Shepherd ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 10#John 10 . 16|John 10:16]]).
