**Context:**
Isaiah 6:1-8 records the prophet's inaugural call vision in "the year that King Uzziah died" (c. 740 BC), marking a pivotal transition in Judah's history. Uzziah's 52-year reign brought prosperity but ended in divine judgment for presumptuous worship (2 Chronicles 26:16-21). In this moment of national crisis, Isaiah receives direct vision of Yahweh enthroned in heavenly temple, surrounded by seraphim crying "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts; the whole earth is full of his glory!" (v. 3). The threefold "holy" (*qāḏôš*) emphasizes God's absolute transcendent purity—the foundation of Isaiah's theology throughout his ministry. Isaiah's response reveals prophetic calling's essential elements: (1) **Vision of God's majesty** - seeing Yahweh's glory produces overwhelming awe; (2) **Confession of unworthiness** - "Woe is me! For I am lost; for I am a man of unclean lips" (v. 5), recognizing personal and corporate sinfulness; (3) **Divine cleansing** - seraph touches Isaiah's lips with burning coal from altar, declaring "your guilt is taken away, and your sin atoned for" (v. 7); (4) **Voluntary response to commission** - when God asks "Whom shall I send?" Isaiah answers "Here am I! Send me" (v. 8). The name יְשַׁעְיָהוּ (*Yeshayahu*, "Yahweh is salvation") embodies the prophet's message. This vision establishes Isaiah's authority—he speaks as one who has stood in God's council, seen His glory, been cleansed by His grace, and commissioned by His voice. The passage's Christological significance emerges through multiple connections: John 12:41 explicitly states Isaiah "saw [Christ's] glory and spoke of him"; the seraphim's anthem anticipates Revelation 4:8; the cleansing coal prefigures Christ's purifying work; the voluntary "Here am I" finds ultimate expression in Christ's incarnational mission.

**Ninefold Analysis:**

**OT Context:**
Isaiah 6 stands as the prophet's inaugural throne-room vision, though positioned after chapters 1-5 which contain oracles from his ministry. The temporal marker "in the year that King Uzziah died" (v. 1) situates the vision at critical juncture—approximately 740 BC. Uzziah (also called Azariah) reigned 52 years, bringing military success and economic prosperity, but ended life as leper excluded from temple for presuming to burn incense, a priestly prerogative (2 Chronicles 26:16-21). His death creates leadership vacuum and theological crisis: if earthly king fails through pride, where is stability? God answers by revealing true King—Yahweh enthroned eternally, sovereign over nations. Isaiah sees "the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up" (v. 1). The Hebrew אֲדֹנָי (*'ăḏōnāy*) emphasizes divine sovereignty. The throne's elevation (*rûm wəniśśā'*, "high and lifted up") will reappear in Isaiah 52:13 describing Suffering Servant's exaltation—same Hebrew roots connecting God's throne and Servant's destiny. The temple setting merges earthly and heavenly—Isaiah likely stands in Jerusalem temple when vision begins, but glimpses heavenly reality behind earthly copy (cf. Hebrews 8:5). The Lord's train (*šûl*) fills temple (v. 1)—imagery of royal robes spreading through sanctuary, emphasizing majesty and glory filling all space. Verse 2 introduces seraphim (*śərāp̄îm*, "burning ones"), six-winged beings stationed above God's throne. Their six wings serve three purposes: two cover faces (cannot gaze directly at divine glory), two cover feet (modesty/reverence before holiness), two used for flight (ready service). The seraphim's antiphonal cry—"Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts; the whole earth is full of his glory!" (v. 3)—establishes Isaiah's central theological conviction: God's holiness. The threefold repetition (*qāḏôš qāḏôš qāḏôš*) intensifies to superlative degree, emphasizing absolute transcendent purity distinguishing Yahweh from all creation. The title "LORD of hosts" (*YHWH ṣəḇā'ôṯ*) presents God as commander of heavenly armies—sovereign over angelic beings and earthly nations. The declaration "whole earth is full of his glory" (*məlō' ḵol-hā'āreṣ kəḇôḏô*) asserts God's immanence balancing His transcendence—though infinitely holy, His presence permeates creation. Verse 4's physical response—foundations shake, house fills with smoke—demonstrates God's glory's overpowering effect on material reality. Isaiah's reaction (v. 5) reveals profound self-awareness: "Woe is me! For I am lost; for I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips; for my eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts!" The exclamation "woe" (*'ôy*) typically introduces prophetic judgment oracles against others; here Isaiah pronounces judgment on himself. The verb "I am lost" (*niḏmêṯî*) means "silenced, destroyed, undone"—recognition that sinful human cannot survive encounter with holy God (cf. Exodus 33:20). The specific confession—"unclean lips"—addresses both personal sin and corporate guilt. Lips represent speech, and speech reveals heart (Matthew 12:34). Isaiah acknowledges his words and his people's words fail God's holiness standard. The focus on lips anticipates cleansing (v. 6-7) and commissioning to speak (v. 8-13). Verses 6-7 describe purification: one seraph flies with burning coal (*riṣpāh*) taken from altar with tongs, touches Isaiah's mouth, declaring "Behold, this has touched your lips; your guilt is taken away, and your sin atoned for." The altar represents sacrificial system—coal burning on altar where offerings consumed symbolizes God's acceptance of substitutionary atonement. The searing coal purifies what was defiled. The seraph's declaration employs legal/cultic vocabulary: "guilt is taken away" (*sār 'ăwōneḵā*, "your iniquity has departed") and "sin atoned for" (*təḵupper*, Piel form of *kāp̄ar*, "covered, expiated"). This gracious cleansing precedes commission—only purified messenger can proclaim holy God's word. Verse 8 shifts to divine council scene: "And I heard the voice of the Lord saying, 'Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?'" The plural "us" (cf. Genesis 1:26) suggests divine council or trinitarian mystery. God's question doesn't express ignorance but invites voluntary response—prophetic calling requires willing acceptance. Isaiah's reply—"Here am I! Send me" (*hinnēnî šəlāḥēnî*)—mirrors Abraham's response (Genesis 22:1), Moses' response (Exodus 3:4), and Samuel's response (1 Samuel 3:4). The phrase "here am I" (*hinnēnî*) expresses total availability and readiness. Verses 9-13 (not in this passage's range but completing context) contain sobering commission: Isaiah will preach but people will not understand, see but not perceive—judicial hardening follows persistent rejection. This establishes prophetic ministry's tragic dimension: faithful proclamation may harden rather than soften resistant hearts, yet prophet must continue speaking God's word regardless of reception.

**Jewish Backgrounds:**
Jewish interpretation of Isaiah 6 emphasized several key elements. The Targum Jonathan paraphrases verse 1: "I saw the glory of the LORD sitting on a throne high and lifted up in the highest heavens," making explicit the heavenly location. Rabbinic tradition (Babylonian Talmud, Megillah 10b) taught that Isaiah's vision occurred on Yom Kippur (Day of Atonement), connecting the burning coal's cleansing to annual atonement ritual. The Talmud (Yebamot 49b) contrasts Moses and Isaiah: Moses saw through "clear glass" (direct vision), Isaiah through "dim glass" (veiled vision)—yet rabbinic tradition debated whether Isaiah's vision was superior because he "saw the King" while Moses only saw God's "back" (Exodus 33:23). Jewish mystical tradition (Hekhalot literature, Merkavah mysticism) developed throne-visions as template for mystical ascent—practitioners sought to replicate Isaiah's and Ezekiel's experiences, ascending through heavenly palaces to behold divine throne. The seraphim generated extensive speculation: Are they angels? Higher than cherubim or lower? Rabbinic literature (Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer 4) describes seraphim as standing in highest heaven, continuously singing God's praises. The threefold "Holy" became central to Jewish liturgy: the Kedushah (sanctification prayer) recites Isaiah 6:3 in daily worship, connecting earthly synagogue to heavenly throne-room. Medieval Jewish commentators (Rashi, Ibn Ezra) interpreted the temporal marker "year King Uzziah died" morally: Uzziah's prideful presumption contrasts with Isaiah's humble submission. Uzziah sought priestly privilege illegitimately (2 Chronicles 26); Isaiah receives prophetic calling legitimately through divine grace. The Zohar (medieval Kabbalah) interprets seraphim's six wings allegorically: representing six directions (north, south, east, west, up, down), symbolizing God's omnipresence. Maimonides (Guide for the Perplexed 3:2) rationalizes the vision as prophetic imagination rather than literal sight—the prophet's intellect receives divine emanation, forming mental images communicating spiritual truth. He classifies Isaiah among highest prophetic rank (only Moses superior). First-century Jewish expectation sometimes identified the Suffering Servant of Isaiah 53 as corporate Israel, sometimes as Messiah ben Joseph (suffering messianic figure), or sometimes as the prophet Isaiah himself suffering for his people. The Dead Sea Scrolls (1QIsaª) preserve Isaiah text nearly identical to Masoretic Text, showing Isaiah 6's centrality and textual stability. Josephus (Antiquities 10.35) references Isaiah seeing future events, emphasizing predictive prophecy. Philo allegorizes Isaiah's vision, seeing seraphim as divine Logos mediating between transcendent God and material world.

**Text Form:**
Isaiah 6:1-8 employs first-person narrative vision report, standard form for prophetic call narratives (cf. Jeremiah 1, Ezekiel 1-3). Verse 1's temporal formula—"In the year that King Uzziah died"—uses construct phrase (*bišnaṯ-môṯ*) creating specific historical anchor. The verbs employ wayyiqtol (narrative past) sequence: "I saw" (*wā'er'eh*), establishing eyewitness testimony. The description "Lord sitting upon throne, high and lifted up" uses three participles intensifying majesty: *yôšēḇ* (sitting, continuous state), *rām* (high), *wəniśśā'* (lifted up, exalted). The phrase "train filled the temple" (*šûlāyw məlē'îm*) uses Piel participle emphasizing fullness—divine presence pervades sanctuary. Verse 2's description employs circumstantial clause: "Above him stood the seraphim" (*śərāp̄îm 'ōməḏîm mimmā'al lô*). The participle *'ōməḏîm* (standing) suggests continuous posture of readiness. The specification "each had six wings" uses distributive "each one to each one" (*'eḥāḏ 'eḥāḏ*) emphasizing individual attention to detail. The threefold wing-function employs imperfect verbs indicating continuous/repeated action: "with two he would cover" (*yəḵasseh*), describing habitual reverent posture. Verse 3's antiphonal call uses perfect verb *wəqārā'* ("and he called") introducing direct quotation. The threefold "Holy" employs nominal repetition for intensification (Hebrew lacks superlative degree, uses repetition instead). The declaration's structure creates synonymous parallelism: "the LORD of hosts" (divine name/title) parallels "the whole earth is full of his glory" (divine immanence). Verse 4's response uses plural subject with singular verb: "the foundations of the thresholds shook" (*wayyānū'û*), personifying architecture's response to divine voice. The filling smoke (*'āšān*) recalls Sinai theophany (Exodus 19:18) and temple dedication (1 Kings 8:10-11). Verse 5's outcry employs exclamatory particle *'ôy* plus first-person pronoun *lî* ("woe to me!"). The verb "I am lost" (*niḏmêṯî*) uses Niphal (passive/reflexive) stem suggesting both divine action and self-awareness. The causal *kî* clause ("for I am a man of unclean lips") provides reason. The phrase "I dwell in the midst of" (*bəṯôḵ*) creates both spatial and covenantal identification with sinful community. The climactic clause "for my eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts" (*kî 'eṯ-hammelek YHWH ṣəḇā'ôṯ rā'û 'ênāy*) employs word order inversion placing "the King" at sentence beginning for emphasis—the sight of divine majesty produces terror for sinner. Verses 6-7 shift to third-person narrative describing seraph's action. The verb sequence uses wayyiqtol chain: "flew" (*wayyā'op̄*), "touched" (*wayyagga'*), "said" (*wayyō'mer*), narrating rapid cleansing ritual. The demonstrative "this" (*zeh*) combined with perfect verb "has touched" (*nāḡa'*) presents completed action. The declarations "your guilt is taken away" (*sār*) and "your sin atoned for" (*təḵupper*) employ perfect verbs indicating accomplished facts—purification complete. Verse 8's divine speech uses imperfect verb *'ešmā'* ("I heard") introducing new scene. God's question employs cohortative mood: "Whom shall I send?" (*'eṯ-mî 'ešlāḥ*), inviting rather than commanding. The second question shifts to first-person plural: "who will go for us?" (*ûmî yēleḵ-lānû*), suggesting divine council deliberation. Isaiah's response employs independent pronoun *hinnēnî* ("behold me, here am I") expressing emphatic availability, followed by imperative *šəlāḥēnî* ("send me")—volunteering before commission specified.

**Hermeneutical Use:**
The NT interprets Isaiah 6 as prophetic vision of Christ's pre-incarnate glory. John 12:37-41 provides explicit christological reading: after noting Jewish unbelief despite Jesus' miracles, John quotes Isaiah 53:1 ("Lord, who has believed our report?") and Isaiah 6:10 (hardening oracle), then declares: "Isaiah said these things because he saw his glory and spoke of him" (v. 41). The antecedent of "his glory" is Jesus Christ mentioned in verse 36—John identifies Yahweh on the throne whom Isaiah saw as the pre-incarnate Word. This interpretation follows Jewish identification of "the Angel of the LORD" appearances in OT as theophanies of second person of Trinity. The seraphim's anthem "Holy, holy, holy" finds liturgical continuation in Revelation 4:8, where four living creatures surrounding God's throne proclaim "Holy, holy, holy, is the Lord God Almighty, who was and is and is to come"—perpetual worship begun by seraphim continues before Lamb's throne. The cleansing coal touching Isaiah's lips prefigures Christ's purifying work. As coal from sacrificial altar cleansed Isaiah, Christ's sacrificial death cleanses believers: "the blood of Jesus his Son cleanses us from all sin" (1 John 1:7). Isaiah's confession "I am a man of unclean lips" finds answer in Christ who "knew no sin" yet "became sin" for us (2 Corinthians 5:21), enabling our purification. Isaiah's voluntary response "Here am I! Send me!" anticipates Christ's incarnational mission. Hebrews 2:13 quotes Isaiah 8:18 ("Behold, I and the children God has given me") as Christ's words—showing Isaiah's prophetic self-identification prefigures Christ's identification with redeemed humanity. Hebrews 10:5-7 presents Christ's submission: "when Christ came into the world, he said... 'Behold, I have come to do your will, O God'"—the ultimate "Here am I! Send me!" Christ volunteers for mission requiring not just prophetic proclamation but substitutionary death. Isaiah's sending to preach judgment to hard-hearted Israel (Isaiah 6:9-13) finds fulfillment in Jesus' parabolic teaching hardening unbelievers while revealing truth to disciples (Matthew 13:10-17). Jesus quotes Isaiah 6:9-10 explaining parables' dual purpose: concealing from proud, revealing to humble. Acts 28:25-27 applies Isaiah 6:9-10 to Jewish rejection of Paul's gospel, showing pattern continues—prophetic word hardens those refusing to hear. The progression from Isaiah's experience to Christ follows typological escalation: Isaiah sees God's glory and is commissioned to speak; Christ IS God's glory incarnate (John 1:14) and IS God's Word made flesh. Isaiah receives temporary purification for prophetic ministry; Christ provides eternal purification through His blood for priestly ministry (Hebrews 9:14). Isaiah volunteers for mission knowing it brings rejection; Christ volunteers knowing it brings crucifixion. Isaiah's lips are cleansed to speak God's word; Christ's lips never needed cleansing—He is truth itself (John 14:6).

**Theological Use:**
Isaiah 6:1-8 establishes foundational doctrines: (1) **Divine transcendence and holiness**: God's absolute moral purity separates Him from creation—the threefold "Holy" emphasizes His otherness. This grounds biblical ethics (be holy as God is holy, Leviticus 11:44-45; 1 Peter 1:15-16) and worship (approach God with reverence and awe, Hebrews 12:28-29). (2) **Divine immanence**: Though transcendent, God fills earth with glory—He is near, active in history, present in creation. This prevents deism while maintaining God's distinctness from creation. (3) **Human sinfulness before holy God**: Isaiah's terror—"I am lost"—reveals fallen humanity's state. Confrontation with God's holiness exposes moral defilement. Apart from grace, no one survives divine encounter (Exodus 33:20). This establishes universal need for atonement. (4) **Substitutionary atonement**: The burning coal from altar represents sacrificial system's efficacy. As coal cleanses Isaiah's lips, sacrifice cleanses guilt. This typologically points to Christ's atoning death—"without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness of sins" (Hebrews 9:22). (5) **Prophetic calling and authority**: True prophets receive divine commission, see God's glory, experience cleansing, volunteer willingly. This distinguishes genuine prophecy from false (Jeremiah 23:21-22). Biblical authority rests on divine origin, not human wisdom. (6) **Trinitarian hint**: The plural "us" in "who will go for us?" suggests divine plurality within unity—mysterious plurality later revealed as Trinity. Church fathers cited this alongside Genesis 1:26 as OT Trinity hints. (7) **Judicial hardening**: God's sovereignty includes hardening those who persistently reject truth (Romans 9:18). Isaiah's commission anticipates Jesus' parabolic teaching and Israel's unbelief—God's word accomplishes His purposes even when rejected (Isaiah 55:10-11). (8) **Grace preceding works**: God initiates—Isaiah doesn't volunteer until after cleansing. Salvation is God's work from beginning to end (Ephesians 2:8-9). Cleansing precedes commissioning; justification precedes sanctification. (9) **Eschatological worship**: The seraphim's perpetual "Holy, holy, holy" models eternal worship believers will join (Revelation 4:8). Present worship anticipates future consummation when redeemed humanity stands before God's throne. Systematic theology develops: holiness as God's essential attribute; sin as violation of divine holiness; atonement as necessary for reconciliation; grace as God's initiative in salvation; sanctification as progressive cleansing; glorification as final transformation enabling direct vision of God (1 John 3:2).

**Rhetorical Use:**
Isaiah 6:1-8's rhetoric moves readers from awe to terror to relief to obedience. Verse 1's throne vision establishes divine authority—God reigns despite earthly king's death. The imagery—"high and lifted up," train filling temple—evokes majesty demanding reverence. Modern readers often sentimentalize God's approachability; Isaiah 6 corrects with healthy fear. The seraphim's description creates distance: if angels cannot look at God's glory but must cover faces, how much less can humans presume familiarity? The antiphonal "Holy, holy, holy" functions liturgically, inviting readers to join heavenly worship while recognizing they worship infinitely pure Being. The cosmic response—shaking foundations, smoke-filled temple—demonstrates God's power affecting material realm. This refutes naturalistic worldview treating God as distant abstraction; divine presence has observable physical effects. Isaiah's terrified confession "Woe is me!" models appropriate response to God's holiness. The rhetoric challenges readers: Have you recognized your sinfulness before holy God? The specific "unclean lips" focuses conviction on speech sins—gossip, lying, blasphemy, harsh words. James 3:2-12 develops this: tongue sets "on fire the entire course of life." Isaiah's corporate confession—"I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips"—prevents individualistic religion. We're implicated in community's guilt; our sin affects others, their sin affects us. This grounds corporate confession in liturgy (cf. Daniel 9:4-19, Nehemiah 1:6-7). The cleansing scene provides gospel comfort: God provides what He demands. Isaiah cannot cleanse himself; grace must intervene. The burning coal's pain (cauterizing) combined with purity models sanctification—painful but necessary purification. The seraph's declaration "your guilt is taken away, your sin atoned for" assures readers: God's atonement is effective, complete, legally binding. Guilt removed; sin covered; believer stands justified. The commission's question—"Whom shall I send?"—invites readers into mission. God doesn't coerce but invites voluntary response. Isaiah's "Here am I! Send me!" provides model: willing availability, eager obedience. The rhetoric moves from passive reception (vision, cleansing) to active participation (volunteering), reflecting salvation pattern—God acts, we respond in faith; justification received passively, sanctification pursued actively. The passage's overall rhetorical effect: (1) Elevate view of God—transcendent, holy, glorious; (2) Humble view of self—sinful, unclean, dependent on grace; (3) Grateful reception of atonement—cleansing is gift, not achievement; (4) Responsive obedience—"send me" flows from "your sin atoned for." This sequence—vision of God, conviction of sin, assurance of atonement, commission to service—provides template for conversion narrative and ongoing discipleship. The rhetoric aims not merely to inform but transform: readers should exit encounter with holy God changed—humbled, cleansed, commissioned.

**Christological Connection:**
[[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 6#Isaiah 6 . 1|Isaiah 6:1-8]] finds ultimate fulfillment in Jesus Christ, who IS the vision Isaiah saw, accomplishes the cleansing Isaiah received, and perfectly models the obedience Isaiah offered. [[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 12#John 12 . 41|John 12:41]]'s statement "Isaiah said these things because he saw [Christ's] glory" identifies the LORD on the throne as pre-incarnate Christ—second person of Trinity manifest in OT theophanies. Isaiah's vision wasn't merely of Yahweh generically but specifically of the Son before incarnation. This connects to [[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 1#John 1 . 18|John 1:18]]: "No one has ever seen God; the only God, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known." OT "sightings" of God were Christophanies—visible manifestations of the Son who would become flesh. The throne imagery establishes Christ's deity and sovereignty. Where Isaiah saw the LORD "high and lifted up" (*rām wəniśśā'*), [[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 52#Isaiah 52 . 13|Isaiah 52:13]] prophesies Servant will be "raised and lifted up and highly exalted" (same Hebrew roots *rûm wəniśśā'*)—connecting God's exalted throne to Servant's exaltation through suffering. Christ descends from throne to earth ([[Readable Bible/50 - Philippians/Philippians 2#Philippians 2 . 6|Philippians 2:6-8]]), suffers crucifixion, then ascends back to Father's right hand ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 1#Hebrews 1 . 3|Hebrews 1:3]]), eternally enthroned as God-man mediator. The seraphim's anthem "Holy, holy, holy" proclaims Christ's holiness. Though incarnate in human flesh, Christ remained perfectly holy—"without sin" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 4#Hebrews 4 . 15|Hebrews 4:15]]). The threefold repetition anticipates trinitarian revelation: Father holy, Son holy, Spirit holy—one God in three persons equally worthy of worship. [[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 4#Revelation 4 . 8|Revelation 4:8]]'s continuation of this worship shows redeemed humanity joining angels in eternal "Holy, holy, holy" addressed to "the Lord God Almighty"—Father, Son, and Spirit. Isaiah's confession "I am a man of unclean lips" contrasts with Christ's purity. Where Isaiah needed cleansing, Christ never sinned in word: "No deceit was found in his mouth" ([[Readable Bible/60 - 1 Peter/1 Peter 2#1 Peter 2 . 22|1 Peter 2:22]], quoting [[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 53#Isaiah 53 . 9|Isaiah 53:9]]). Christ's lips spoke only truth ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 14#John 14 . 6|John 14:6]]), proclaimed good news ([[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 4#Luke 4 . 18|Luke 4:18-19]]), and offered intercession ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 7#Hebrews 7 . 25|Hebrews 7:25]]). Yet Christ identified with sinners—"numbered with the transgressors" ([[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 53#Isaiah 53 . 12|Isaiah 53:12]])—so that His purity could cover our uncleanness. The cleansing coal from altar prefigures Christ's atoning sacrifice. As altar-fire consumed offerings and coal from that altar cleansed Isaiah, Christ's sacrificial death "cleanses us from all sin" ([[Readable Bible/62 - 1 John/1 John 1#1 John 1 . 7|1 John 1:7]]). The imagery: Christ is both sacrifice burned on altar AND coal applying that sacrifice's benefits. He is Lamb slain ([[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 5#Revelation 5 . 6|Revelation 5:6]]) and Great High Priest applying atonement ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 9#Hebrews 9 . 11|Hebrews 9:11-14]]). The seraph's declaration "your guilt is taken away, your sin atoned for" finds ultimate accomplishment in Christ's finished work. He "bore our sins in his body on the tree" ([[Readable Bible/60 - 1 Peter/1 Peter 2#1 Peter 2 . 24|1 Peter 2:24]]); "by his wounds you have been healed" ([[Readable Bible/60 - 1 Peter/1 Peter 2#1 Peter 2 . 24|1 Peter 2:24]], quoting [[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 53#Isaiah 53 . 5|Isaiah 53:5]]). Where Isaiah received temporary cleansing for prophetic ministry, believers receive eternal cleansing through Christ's blood ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 9#Hebrews 9 . 14|Hebrews 9:14]]; 10:22). Isaiah's voluntary "Here am I! Send me!" anticipates Christ's incarnational mission. Before creation, the Son agreed to become incarnate, suffer, and save ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 40#Psalm 40 . 6|Psalm 40:6-8]]; [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 10#Hebrews 10 . 5|Hebrews 10:5-7]]). In Gethsemane, Christ prayed "not my will, but yours, be done" ([[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 22#Luke 22 . 42|Luke 22:42]])—ultimate submission surpassing Isaiah's. Isaiah volunteered knowing he'd be rejected ([[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 6#Isaiah 6 . 9|Isaiah 6:9-13]]); Christ volunteered knowing He'd be crucified. Isaiah's calling required speaking God's word; Christ IS God's Word ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 1#John 1 . 1|John 1:1]], 14). Isaiah mediated revelation; Christ IS revelation—"the exact imprint of [God's] nature" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 1#Hebrews 1 . 3|Hebrews 1:3]]). The commissioning question—"Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?"—finds answer not merely in prophets but supremely in the Son. [[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 17#John 17 . 18|John 17:18]] records Jesus' prayer: "As you sent me into the world, so I have sent them into the world." The Father sends the Son; the Son sends disciples. Isaiah's prophetic lineage culminates in Christ who commissions apostles to proclaim gospel. The escalation: Isaiah proclaimed coming salvation; Christ accomplished salvation; apostles proclaimed accomplished salvation. Isaiah's ministry brought mixed response—hardening and remnant ([[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 6#Isaiah 6 . 9|Isaiah 6:9-13]]); Christ's ministry likewise produced division ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 9#John 9 . 39|John 9:39]]; 12:37-40). But where Isaiah's word could only expose sin, Christ's word grants life ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 6#John 6 . 63|John 6:63]]). The contrast: Isaiah needed cleansing before speaking; Christ cleanses through His speaking ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 15#John 15 . 3|John 15:3]]; [[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 5#Ephesians 5 . 26|Ephesians 5:26]]). Isaiah saw God's glory veiled; Christ manifested God's glory unveiled ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 1#John 1 . 14|John 1:14]]; [[Readable Bible/47 - 2 Corinthians/2 Corinthians 4#2 Corinthians 4 . 6|2 Corinthians 4:6]]). Isaiah trembled before the Holy One; Christ IS the Holy One ([[Readable Bible/41 - Mark/Mark 1#Mark 1 . 24|Mark 1:24]]; [[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 3#Acts 3 . 14|Acts 3:14]]). The typological pattern: Isaiah's vision → Christ's incarnation; Isaiah's cleansing → Christ's atonement; Isaiah's commission → Christ's mission; Isaiah's proclamation → Christ's gospel. Where Isaiah said "Here am I," Christ became "Immanuel—God with us" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 1#Matthew 1 . 23|Matthew 1:23]]). Where Isaiah's lips needed purifying, Christ's lips spoke grace and truth ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 1#John 1 . 14|John 1:14]], 17). Where Isaiah volunteered to speak for God, Christ IS God speaking ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 1#Hebrews 1 . 2|Hebrews 1:2]]). Isaiah 6 establishes prophetic office pointing to Christ who is ultimate Prophet revealing God fully, ultimate Priest cleansing completely, ultimate King reigning eternally. Every prophet from Isaiah to Malachi prepared the way; Christ is the Way ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 14#John 14 . 6|John 14:6]]). Every sacrifice from altar anticipated; Christ is final Sacrifice ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 10#Hebrews 10 . 10|Hebrews 10:10-14]]). Every throne-vision glimpsed God's glory; Christ IS "the radiance of the glory of God" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 1#Hebrews 1 . 3|Hebrews 1:3]]). The seraphim's cry "the whole earth is full of his glory" awaits eschatological fulfillment when "the earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD as the waters cover the sea" ([[Readable Bible/35 - Habakkuk/Habakkuk 2#Habakkuk 2 . 14|Habakkuk 2:14]])—accomplished when Christ returns and "every eye will see him" ([[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 1#Revelation 1 . 7|Revelation 1:7]]). [[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 6#Isaiah 6 . 1|Isaiah 6:1-8]] thus establishes prophetic calling pointing to Christ who fulfills, surpasses, and consummates all prophetic ministry—He is the Prophet of whom Moses spoke ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 18#Deuteronomy 18 . 15|Deuteronomy 18:15]]), whom Isaiah saw enthroned, whom all Scripture testifies, making Isaiah's vision not merely historical account but christological preview of God's eternal plan revealed in Jesus.
