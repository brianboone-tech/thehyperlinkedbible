**Context:**
Psalm 110 is David's prophetic oracle concerning the Messiah, presenting the most quoted OT passage in the NT. Verse 1 establishes the Messiah's divine sonship and royal authority through Yahweh's invitation to sit at His right hand until all enemies are subjugated. This session at God's right hand represents completed priestly work (contrasting with standing Levitical priests) and ongoing royal reign, uniting the dual offices later revealed in verse 4. Jesus' question to the Pharisees regarding this verse—"If David calls him Lord, how is he his son?" (Matthew 22:41-46)—exposes the mystery of Messiah's divine-human nature. The psalm's combination of royal enthronement (v. 1) with eternal Melchizedekian priesthood (v. 4) creates the theological foundation for Christ's unique ministry.

**Ninefold Analysis:**

**OT Context:**
Psalm 110 stands uniquely among the Psalms as David's prophetic oracle (*nə'um*) concerning one he calls "my Lord" (*'ăḏōnî*), creating a puzzle: how can David's descendant be David's superior? The verse's structure presents Yahweh addressing David's Lord, inviting Him to sit at the position of highest honor and authority—God's right hand. This session continues "until" (*'aḏ*) a specific goal is accomplished: enemies become a footstool, symbolizing complete subjugation (ancient kings literally placed feet on defeated enemies' necks, Joshua 10:24). The combination of divine invitation, exalted position, and promised victory establishes the Messiah's unique status. The verb "sit" (*šēḇ*) contrasts with priestly standing—Levitical priests never sat because their work was never finished (Hebrews 10:11). But here Yahweh commands sitting, implying completed atoning work. The psalm was likely composed for royal coronations but transcends any historical Davidic king, pointing beyond Solomon and his successors to the ultimate Davidic heir. Its placement in Book V of the Psalter (Psalms 107-150) and its frequent pairing with Psalm 2 in Jewish tradition emphasize messianic themes of divine sonship and universal dominion.

**Jewish Backgrounds:**
Ancient Jewish interpretation recognized Psalm 110's messianic significance. The Targum on Psalms renders "the LORD said to my Lord" as "The LORD said in his decree to give me [David] the kingdom," attempting to resolve the puzzle of David calling his son "Lord." The Talmud (b. Sanhedrin 38b) explicitly identifies this psalm as messianic, debating the identity of "my Lord." Midrash Tehillim interprets the session at God's right hand as Messiah's exaltation above angels. The Dead Sea Scrolls (11QMelch) connect Psalm 110 with Melchizedek as heavenly deliverer. Rabbinic literature struggled with verse 1's implication that someone other than God could sit in heaven—b. Hagigah 15a records Rabbi Akiba's controversial suggestion that one throne is for God and one for David (Messiah), which was rejected by other rabbis who insisted the second throne was for justice or mercy, not a second divine figure. This rabbinic discomfort reveals the verse's radical claim: David's Lord shares God's throne, implying deity. When Jesus quoted this psalm to the Pharisees (Matthew 22:41-46), they couldn't answer because to acknowledge Messiah's superiority to David challenged their expectation of a merely human deliverer, while to deny it contradicted their own scriptures.

**Text Form:**
The verse opens with the prophetic oracle formula *nə'um YHWH* ("oracle of the LORD"), used elsewhere for divine speech (Numbers 24:3-4, 15-16; 2 Samuel 23:1). This formula establishes direct divine revelation. The structure presents two subjects (Yahweh and David's Lord) and two actions (Yahweh speaks, David's Lord sits). The command "sit" (*šēḇ*) in imperative mood conveys invitation rather than servitude—God invites His Messiah to share His throne, an unthinkable honor for a mere human. The phrase "at my right hand" (*lîmînî*) indicates the position of highest honor, reserved for the most favored (1 Kings 2:19; Psalm 45:9). The temporal clause "until I make" (*'aḏ-'āšîṯ*) establishes duration—the session continues throughout the period of subjugating enemies, implying ongoing royal authority during this time. The metaphor "footstool for your feet" (*hăḏōm lə-raḡlêḵā*) uses vivid imagery of ancient victory rituals (Joshua 10:24) to depict total conquest. The psalm's poetic parallelism in subsequent verses develops the themes of willing people (v. 3), eternal priesthood (v. 4), conquering king (v. 5-6), and ultimate victory (v. 7).

**Hermeneutical Use:**
The NT employs Psalm 110:1 more than any other OT verse (quoted or alluded to over 30 times), demonstrating its centrality to apostolic christology. Jesus' own use (Matthew 22:41-46; Mark 12:35-37; Luke 20:41-44) establishes the hermeneutical key: Messiah must be both David's son (human descent) and David's Lord (divine nature). Peter at Pentecost (Acts 2:34-35) interprets Christ's ascension as fulfillment—Jesus now sits at God's right hand. Paul applies the "enemies under feet" to Christ's ongoing conquest (1 Corinthians 15:25; Ephesians 1:20-22). Hebrews weaves Psalm 110:1 throughout its argument (1:3, 13; 8:1; 10:12-13; 12:2), connecting the session at God's right hand with Christ's completed priestly work—He sat down because sacrifice was finished. The hermeneutical principle: OT royal psalms reach their true fulfillment in Christ, who surpasses all previous Davidic kings. The psalm's combination of royal (v. 1) and priestly (v. 4) offices reveals God's intention to unite what the Mosaic system separated—kingship from Judah, priesthood from Levi—in one person, Messiah.

**Theological Use:**
This verse establishes foundational doctrines: (1) Christ's deity—David's Lord shares God's throne, a prerogative of deity alone; (2) Christ's session—sitting at God's right hand indicates completed atoning work and ongoing royal authority; (3) Christ's exaltation—highest position in the universe (Philippians 2:9-11); (4) Christ's ongoing reign—"until" indicates present activity, subjugating enemies progressively; (5) Christ's ultimate victory—all enemies will be made His footstool; (6) Christ's dual nature—both David's son (humanity) and David's Lord (deity); (7) Christ's mediatorial role—seated at God's right hand, He mediates between God and humanity. Systematic theology develops the doctrine of Christ's three offices (prophet, priest, king) from this psalm combined with verse 4. The session at God's right hand grounds the doctrine of Christ's present ministry—He is not passively in heaven but actively reigning, interceding, and conquering. Soteriology draws on this verse for assurance: if Christ reigns until all enemies are defeated, believers' salvation is secure because He continues His work until complete victory.

**Rhetorical Use:**
The psalm's opening creates immediate dramatic tension: Yahweh speaks to someone David calls "my Lord." This literary technique invites readers to ask, "Who is this superior to David?" The imperative "sit" conveys royal authority and completed work—no ongoing striving but confident rest. The "right hand" imagery resonates with ancient Near Eastern royal protocol where the most honored guest sat at the king's right side. The "until" creates eschatological expectation—the present session continues toward future consummation. The footstool metaphor provides vivid, concrete imagery for abstract theological truth—complete subjugation of all opposition. The rhetorical effect when Jesus quotes this verse to the Pharisees (Matthew 22:44) is devastating: either acknowledge Messiah's deity (David's Lord) or deny Scripture's plain meaning. The apostles' repeated use of this verse in preaching (Acts 2:34) and teaching (Hebrews throughout) demonstrates its rhetorical power—it proves from Israel's own scriptures that Messiah must be divine, must complete atoning work (sitting, not standing), and must reign until all enemies submit. The psalm's combination of present reality (seated now) and future promise (enemies will be footstool) provides both assurance and anticipation for believers.

**Christological Connection:**
[[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 110#Psalm 110 . 1|Psalm 110:1]] unveils Christ's present ministry following His ascension. Peter declares at Pentecost that David's prophecy finds fulfillment: "Being therefore exalted at the right hand of God... David did not ascend into the heavens, but he himself says, 'The Lord said to my Lord, "Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies your footstool"'" ([[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 2#Acts 2 . 33|Acts 2:33-35]]). Christ's session at the Father's right hand represents the culmination of His earthly work and inauguration of His heavenly ministry. Hebrews emphasizes this repeatedly: "After making purification for sins, he sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 1#Hebrews 1 . 3|Hebrews 1:3]]). The sitting contrasts with Levitical priests who "stand daily at their service, offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins. But when Christ had offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins, he sat down at the right hand of God" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 10#Hebrews 10 . 11|Hebrews 10:11-12]]). The session signifies finished atoning work—nothing more needed for salvation. Yet Christ's session is not passive. Paul reveals that Christ "is at the right hand of God, who indeed is interceding for us" ([[Readable Bible/45 - Romans/Romans 8#Romans 8 . 34|Romans 8:34]]). He applies His finished work's benefits, advocates for believers, and progressively subdues all opposition. The "until" clause indicates ongoing conquest: "He must reign until he has put all his enemies under his feet" ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 15#1 Corinthians 15 . 25|1 Corinthians 15:25]]). Every spiritual victory—conversions, church growth, Satan's defeat in individual lives—constitutes progressive fulfillment of enemies becoming footstool. The ultimate consummation occurs when "the last enemy to be destroyed is death" ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 15#1 Corinthians 15 . 26|1 Corinthians 15:26]]), at Christ's return when every knee bows ([[Readable Bible/50 - Philippians/Philippians 2#Philippians 2 . 10|Philippians 2:10]]). The psalm's combination of royal authority (session) and priestly ministry ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 110#Psalm 110 . 4|Psalm 110:4]]) uniquely characterizes Christ. Where the Mosaic system separated kingship (Judah) from priesthood (Levi), Christ unites both—He is the priest-king after Melchizedek's order, reigning from heaven's throne while interceding for His people. This explains why God exalted Him "far above all rule and authority and power and dominion" and "put all things under his feet" ([[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 1#Ephesians 1 . 20|Ephesians 1:20-22]]). His session at the right hand guarantees believers' security—if He reigns until all enemies are conquered, our salvation depends on His ongoing victory, not our fluctuating performance. The vision of "Jesus, the founder and perfecter of our faith, who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame, and is seated at the right hand of the throne of God" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 12#Hebrews 12 . 2|Hebrews 12:2]]) provides endurance for suffering saints—our champion occupies heaven's throne, progressively conquering all opposition, until the day He returns to consummate His victory and present the perfected kingdom to the Father.
