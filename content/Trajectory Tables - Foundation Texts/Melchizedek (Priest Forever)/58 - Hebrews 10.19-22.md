**Context:**
Hebrews 10:19-22 transitions from theological exposition (chapters 7-10:18) to practical exhortation (10:19-13:25). The "therefore" (*oun*) draws application from Christ's superior priesthood and once-for-all sacrifice. Verses 19-20 declare believers' privilege: "we have confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus, by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh." The language evokes Day of Atonement imagery—entering the Most Holy Place through the veil—but democratizes it: not just high priest annually but all believers continuously. Verse 21 identifies the enabling reality: "since we have a great priest over the house of God." The "great priest" (*hiereus megas*) recalls Christ's Melchizedekian priesthood developed in chapter 7. Verse 22 issues the invitation: "let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith, with our hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience and our bodies washed with pure water." The imperatives move from doctrine to duty, from indicative to imperative, from Christ's work to believers' response.

**Ninefold Analysis:**

**OT Context:**
The passage builds on tabernacle's spatial theology. Exodus 26:31-33 describes the veil (*parokhet*): "You shall make a veil of blue and purple and scarlet yarns and fine twined linen... And you shall hang the veil from the clasps, and bring the ark of the testimony in there within the veil. And the veil shall separate for you the Holy Place from the Most Holy." This thick, ornate curtain created barrier between holy place (where priests ministered daily) and Most Holy Place (where God's presence dwelt above the ark). Only the high priest could pass through, only on Day of Atonement (*Yom Kippur*), only with blood (Leviticus 16:2, 12-15). The veil's function: protect God's holiness by restricting sinful humanity's access. Unauthorized entrance meant death (Leviticus 16:2). The priestly washing rituals appear throughout Pentateuch. At ordination, priests were "washed with water" (Exodus 29:4; 40:12; Leviticus 8:6). Before approaching altar or entering tent of meeting, priests washed hands and feet at bronze laver "so that they may not die" (Exodus 30:19-21). The sprinkling rituals provided ceremonial purification: blood sprinkled on altar (Leviticus 1:5), on priest (Exodus 29:21), on people (Exodus 24:8), on defiled persons (Numbers 19:18-19). These external washings foreshadowed internal cleansing prophesied in Ezekiel 36:25-27: "I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean from all your uncleannesses... And I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you."

**Jewish Backgrounds:**
Jewish ritual purity laws created complex system of washings. The Mishnah tractates Mikvaot and Yadayim detail purification requirements. Before temple worship, Jews immersed in *mikveh* (ritual bath). Priests performed elaborate washings before service. The high priest's Day of Atonement preparation included multiple washings (Mishnah Yoma 3:2-4). These external purifications symbolized need for internal cleansing but couldn't achieve it: "For it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins" (Hebrews 10:4). Philo spiritualized temple imagery, seeing veil as barrier between material and spiritual realms. For Hebrews' original readers, the claim that believers have "confidence to enter the holy places" would have been shocking—only high priest, only annually, could enter Most Holy Place. Hebrews democratizes access: Christ's sacrifice opened way for all believers to approach God's presence continuously. The torn veil at Jesus' crucifixion (Matthew 27:51; Mark 15:38; Luke 23:45) visibly demonstrated abolished barrier—torn "from top to bottom," signifying God's action, not human effort. Some rabbinic sources (Josephus, Jewish War 5.219) describe the temple veil as four inches thick, requiring 300 priests to manipulate—its supernatural tearing proved God removed the barrier.

**Text Form:**
Verse 19 begins with inferential *oun* ("therefore"), drawing application from preceding theology. The participial phrase *echontes oun... parrēsian* ("having therefore... confidence") emphasizes possession—believers currently have this confidence. The object *eis tēn eisodon tōn hagiōn* ("for the entrance into the holy places") uses articular infinitive expressing purpose. The instrumental *en tō haimati Iēsou* ("by the blood of Jesus") identifies means of access. Verse 20's relative clause *hēn enekainisen hēmin hodon prosphatonkai zōsan* ("which he opened for us, a new and living way") uses aorist *enekainisen* ("inaugurated, dedicated") for completed past action. The adjectives *prosphatonkai zōsan* ("new and living") characterize the way—*prosphatos* means "recently slain, fresh" (etymologically "newly slaughtered"), applied metaphorically to mean "new, recent"; *zōsan* means "living, life-giving." The prepositional phrase *dia tou katapetasmatos, tout' estin tēs sarkos autou* ("through the curtain, that is, through his flesh") creates identification: the veil represents Christ's flesh. Verse 21 uses causal participle *echontes hiereus megas* ("having a great priest") explaining ground for confidence. The phrase *epi ton oikon tou theou* ("over the house of God") identifies sphere of Christ's priestly authority. Verse 22 issues threefold exhortation using *proserchomai* ("let us draw near") with accompanying participles describing proper manner: *meta alēthinēs kardias* ("with a true heart"), *en plērophoria pisteōs* ("in full assurance of faith"), *rerrantismenoi tas kardias* ("having hearts sprinkled") and *leloumenoi to sōma* ("having bodies washed"). The perfect passive participles *rerrantismenoi* and *leloumenoi* indicate completed purification providing basis for approach.

**Hermeneutical Use:**
The passage demonstrates typological fulfillment of tabernacle imagery. The hermeneutical moves: (1) Identify OT type—veil restricting access to God's presence; (2) Recognize type's function—protecting divine holiness, demonstrating human sinfulness; (3) Show Christ as antitype—His flesh is the veil, His death tore it; (4) Apply to believers—unrestricted access now available through Christ; (5) Issue exhortation—draw near with confidence. The veil-flesh identification requires theological interpretation: Christ's incarnate body was veil concealing deity ("whoever has seen me has seen the Father," John 14:9); His torn flesh (crucifixion) corresponds to torn veil (Matthew 27:51); His death removed barrier between God and humanity. The "new and living way" contrasts with old way—new versus ancient, living versus deadly (unauthorized entrance brought death). The washing and sprinkling imagery requires spiritual interpretation: not literal water but Spirit's regeneration (Titus 3:5), Christ's blood purifying conscience (Hebrews 9:14). The interpretive principle: OT physical rituals foreshadowed NT spiritual realities; Christ fulfilled types, enabling access previously forbidden.

**Theological Use:**
This passage establishes crucial doctrines: (1) Believer's access—direct approach to God through Christ; (2) Confidence (*parrēsia*)—boldness replacing fear, based on Christ's work; (3) Christ's blood as means—His death provides entrance; (4) New covenant inaugurated—"new and living way" opened; (5) Veil-flesh identification—Christ's incarnation and death removed barrier; (6) Christ's ongoing priesthood—"great priest over house of God" enables continuous access; (7) Internal purification—hearts sprinkled, consciences cleansed; (8) Full assurance (*plērophoria*)—certainty replacing doubt; (9) Believer priesthood—all draw near, not just designated intermediaries. Systematic theology develops priesthood of all believers: unlike OT where only priests approached God, NT grants all believers priestly access. Soteriology emphasizes subjective application of objective atonement: Christ's blood (objective) purifies conscience (subjective), enabling genuine worship. Pneumatology connects: Spirit applies Christ's work, regenerates heart, enables faith. Ecclesiology emerges: church is "house of God" over which Christ serves as great priest.

**Rhetorical Use:**
The rhetoric transitions from exposition to exhortation. The "therefore" signals application time—doctrine understood must become practice lived. The "we have confidence" creates solidarity—author includes himself with readers, sharing privilege and responsibility. The "to enter the holy places" uses shocking imagery—what was forbidden (only high priest, only annually) now is granted (all believers, continuously). This creates emotional impact: imagine previously excluded person now invited to approach king's throne. The "by the blood of Jesus" grounds confidence objectively—not personal worthiness but Christ's sacrifice. The "new and living way" uses vivid language—*prosphatos* ("fresh, newly slain") evokes Christ's recent sacrifice; "living" (*zōsan*) promises vitality versus death. The veil-flesh identification creates theological depth—Christ's incarnation and crucifixion accomplished access. The "great priest over the house of God" provides assurance—superior priest guarantees acceptance. The threefold "let us" exhortation (vv. 22-24: draw near, hold fast, consider) creates momentum moving from worship to perseverance to mutual encouragement. The "true heart... full assurance of faith" addresses wavering readers—genuine, confident faith required. The "hearts sprinkled... bodies washed" uses familiar imagery for spiritual reality—internal cleansing enabling external worship.

**Christological Connection:**
[[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 10#Hebrews 10 . 19|Hebrews 10:19-22]] applies Christ's Melchizedekian priesthood pastorally: "Therefore, brothers, since we have confidence (*parrēsian*) to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus..." The "therefore" (*oun*) draws inference from preceding chapters—Christ's superior priesthood (chapter 7), ministry in heavenly sanctuary (8:1-5), once-for-all sacrifice (9:11-10:18) together create new reality: believers now have "confidence to enter the holy places." The noun *parrēsia* means "boldness, freedom of speech, confidence"—no longer fearful distance but bold approach. The destination: "the holy places" (*ta hagia*)—the sanctuary where God's presence dwells, specifically the Most Holy Place. Under old covenant, entrance was severely restricted: only high priest, only on Day of Atonement, only with blood, carrying smoke to veil God's glory "so that he may not die" ([[Readable Bible/03 - Leviticus/Leviticus 16#Leviticus 16 . 2|Leviticus 16:2]], 13). Unauthorized entrance meant death—Nadab and Abihu died offering unauthorized fire ([[Readable Bible/03 - Leviticus/Leviticus 10#Leviticus 10 . 1|Leviticus 10:1-2]]); Uzzah died touching ark ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 6#2 Samuel 6 . 6|2 Samuel 6:6-7]]). The barrier was real, deadly, necessary—protecting holy God from sinful humans. But Christ's sacrifice changed everything: "by the blood of Jesus" (*en tō haimati Iēsou*). The instrumental preposition *en* indicates means—His blood provides access. Not animal blood (temporary, external cleansing) but divine-human blood (eternal, internal purification). His blood "speaks a better word than the blood of Abel" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 12#Hebrews 12 . 24|Hebrews 12:24]])—Abel's blood cried for vengeance; Christ's blood cries for mercy. This blood "purifies our conscience from dead works to serve the living God" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 9#Hebrews 9 . 14|Hebrews 9:14]]), enabling genuine worship. Verse 20 describes the access route: "by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh." The aorist *enekainisen* ("inaugurated, dedicated, opened") indicates Christ's definitive action—He established this way by His death. The adjectives "new and living" (*prosphatonkai zōsan*) characterize it. The word *prosphatos* etymologically means "recently slain"—vivid reminder of Christ's sacrifice. Applied to "way," it means "new, recent, fresh"—contrasting with old, worn, obsolete Levitical system. The "living" (*zōsan*) way contrasts with death-threatening old way—unauthorized approach brought death, but Christ's way brings life. The path goes "through the curtain, that is, through his flesh" (*dia tou katapetasmatos, tout' estin tēs sarkos autou*). This enigmatic phrase requires unpacking. The veil (*katapetasma*) separated holy place from Most Holy Place ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 26#Exodus 26 . 31|Exodus 26:31-33]]), symbolizing barrier between humanity and God. When Jesus died, "the curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 27#Matthew 27 . 51|Matthew 27:51]])—supernatural tearing (top to bottom indicates God's action) at exact moment of Christ's death. The tearing symbolized abolished barrier—access now open. The identification "that is, through his flesh" (*tout' estin tēs sarkos autou*) creates veil-flesh correspondence. Multiple layers of meaning emerge: (1) Christ's incarnate flesh (*sarx*) was veil concealing deity—"God was manifest in the flesh" ([[Readable Bible/54 - 1 Timothy/1 Timothy 3#1 Timothy 3 . 16|1 Timothy 3:16 KJV]]); (2) His crucified flesh (torn at death) corresponds to torn veil; (3) His death removed barrier, opening access. The path to God goes through Christ's torn flesh—His death is the way, not obstacle but entrance. Verse 21 provides second ground for confidence: "since we have a great priest over the house of God." The participle *echontes* ("having") indicates possession—believers currently have this priest. The title "great priest" (*hiereus megas*) recalls Melchizedekian priesthood—superior to Aaronic priesthood by virtue of divine oath (7:20-22), permanent duration (7:23-24), sinless character (7:26), effective intercession (7:25), heavenly ministry (8:1-2). This "great priest" serves "over the house of God" (*epi ton oikon tou theou*)—the *oikos* (house/household) referring to God's people, the church. Christ's priestly authority over God's household guarantees believers' access and acceptance. Verse 22 issues the exhortation: "let us draw near (*proserchomai*) with a true heart in full assurance of faith, with our hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience and our bodies washed with pure water." The verb *proserchomai* means "to approach, come to, draw near"—used in LXX for priests approaching altar or worshipers approaching God. Here it's democratized—all believers approach. The manner of approach: "with a true heart" (*meta alēthinēs kardias*)—genuine, sincere, not hypocritical. "In full assurance of faith" (*en plērophoria pisteōs*)—complete certainty, not wavering doubt. The noun *plērophoria* means "full conviction, complete confidence"—believers approach knowing they'll be received, not uncertain whether God accepts. This assurance rests on Christ's finished work, not personal merit. The purification described: "with our hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience" (*rerrantismenoi tas kardias apo syneidēseōs ponēras*). The perfect passive participle *rerrantismenoi* ("having been sprinkled") indicates completed action with ongoing results—believers have been purified, remain pure. The imagery draws from OT sprinkling rituals ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 19#Numbers 19 . 18|Numbers 19:18-19]]; [[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 24#Exodus 24 . 8|Exodus 24:8]]) but applies spiritually to "hearts"—internal moral center, not external body. The cleansing is "from an evil conscience" (*apo syneidēseōs ponēras*)—guilt-laden awareness of sin is removed. Christ's blood "purifies our conscience from dead works" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 9#Hebrews 9 . 14|Hebrews 9:14]]), liberating from paralyzing guilt. The second purification: "our bodies washed with pure water" (*leloumenoi to sōma hydati katharō*). Again, perfect passive participle *leloumenoi* ("having been washed") indicates completed purification. The imagery evokes priestly ordination washing ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 29#Exodus 29 . 4|Exodus 29:4]]) and daily cleansing at laver ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 30#Exodus 30 . 19|Exodus 30:19-20]]), but applies spiritually—likely baptism signifying regeneration's washing ([[Readable Bible/56 - Titus/Titus 3#Titus 3 . 5|Titus 3:5]]; [[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 5#Ephesians 5 . 26|Ephesians 5:26]]). The "pure water" (*hydati katharō*) fulfills [[Readable Bible/26 - Ezekiel/Ezekiel 36#Ezekiel 36 . 25|Ezekiel 36:25]]'s prophecy: "I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean." The combination—hearts sprinkled, bodies washed—indicates total purification: internal (conscience cleansed) and external (life transformed). The practical implications prove transformative. First, confidence: believers approach God boldly, not fearfully, because Christ's blood provides access. No longer excluded outsiders but welcomed children. Second, assurance: full certainty of faith replaces doubt—God receives because Christ intercedes. Third, purity: cleansed conscience and washed life enable genuine worship—no longer paralyzed by guilt or defiled by sin. Fourth, privilege: what was impossible under old covenant (direct access to God's presence) is now every believer's inheritance. The high priest entered Most Holy Place once yearly with fear; believers enter God's presence continually with confidence. Fifth, responsibility: granted such access, believers must "draw near"—not neglecting privilege or taking it for granted but actively approaching God in worship, prayer, and obedience. This passage refutes any notion of distant, unapproachable God. Christ's priesthood demolished barriers, His blood opened access, His flesh provided way, His intercession guarantees welcome. The eternal priesthood "after the order of Melchizedek" functions precisely to enable believers' confident approach to God. The once-forbidden Most Holy Place is now believers' constant destination, Christ their great high priest ensures their acceptance, His blood their perpetual purification. This is the gospel's glory—not excluded but invited, not fearful but confident, not guilty but cleansed, not alone but represented by great priest who "always lives to make intercession" for those who draw near to God through Him.
