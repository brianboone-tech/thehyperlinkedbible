**Context:**

Hebrews 10:19-22 marks the transition from theological exposition (8:1-10:18) to practical exhortation (10:19-13:25), applying Christ's superior high priestly work to believers' daily experience. Following the declaration that Christ's single sacrifice has "perfected for all time those who are being sanctified" (10:14) and the promise of complete forgiveness under the new covenant (10:16-17), the author now draws the practical conclusion: "Therefore, brothers, we have confidence to enter the holy places." The passage transforms Old Testament restriction into New Testament access—where only the high priest could enter the Most Holy Place, only once yearly, with fear and elaborate preparation, now all believers can "draw near" continuously "with a true heart in full assurance of faith." The means of access is "the blood of Jesus" (v. 19), which has opened "a new and living way" through the veil, identified as "his flesh" (v. 20). The torn veil at Christ's crucifixion (Matthew 27:51) becomes the theological explanation for believers' access—Christ's death simultaneously removed the barrier and opened the way. The passage employs cultic imagery (blood, sprinkling, washing) to describe believers' purification, connecting to both Levitical ritual and Christian baptism. The threefold exhortation that follows—"let us draw near" (v. 22), "let us hold fast" (v. 23), "let us consider" (v. 24)—rests on this foundation: Christ's work is complete; our access is secure; therefore, we respond with worship, perseverance, and mutual encouragement.

**Ninefold Analysis:**

**OT Context:**
The passage draws heavily on Leviticus 16's Day of Atonement ritual, where the veil (*paroket*) separated the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place, symbolizing the barrier between humanity and God's immediate presence. Only the high priest could pass through this veil, and only once yearly, with strict protocols: proper garments, incense cloud, blood to sprinkle (Leviticus 16:2-16). Any unauthorized approach meant death (Leviticus 16:2). The veil's construction (Exodus 26:31-33)—"cherubim skillfully worked into it"—recalled Eden's cherubim guarding the way to the tree of life (Genesis 3:24), reinforcing themes of exclusion and restricted access. The Old Testament consistently presents God's presence as simultaneously desirable and dangerous—Israel wants nearness (Exodus 33:15) but fears proximity (Exodus 20:18-19). The tabernacle/temple system provided mediated access through priests and sacrifices while maintaining protective distance. The "sprinkling" imagery comes from purification rituals (Numbers 19; Leviticus 14) where ceremonial defilement required blood or water application. The author of Hebrews transforms these restriction symbols into access reality through Christ.

**Jewish Backgrounds:**
First-century Judaism regarded the temple veil with reverence and fear. Josephus describes it as an enormous curtain embroidered with a panorama of the heavens, separating the realm of God from the realm of humanity. The Mishnah details protocols for the high priest's entry on Yom Kippur, emphasizing danger and divine encounter. Rabbinic tradition recounts legends of ropes tied to the high priest's ankle so his body could be retrieved if he died in God's presence—though these stories likely post-date the Second Temple. The temple's destruction in AD 70 eliminated the physical veil, creating theological crisis for Judaism: How can atonement occur without temple, priesthood, and sacrifices? The author of Hebrews, writing before or shortly after the temple's destruction, argues that the veil's purpose was already fulfilled in Christ's death—Matthew, Mark, and Luke record that the temple veil tore from top to bottom at Christ's crucifixion (Matthew 27:51; Mark 15:38; Luke 23:45), a supernatural act signifying that God Himself opened access through Christ's death.

**Text Form:**
The passage employs carefully structured rhetoric with three layers: (1) Statement of privilege ("we have confidence... we have a great priest," vv. 19-21), (2) Exhortation to response ("let us draw near," v. 22), (3) Description of qualification ("hearts sprinkled... bodies washed," v. 22). The Greek construction *echontes... kai... prosercchōmetha* ("having... and... let us draw near") links privilege to response—because we have confidence and a great priest, therefore let us draw near. The word *parrēsia* ("boldness, confidence") denotes freedom of speech, frank openness—what slaves lack before masters, what subjects lack before tyrants, believers now possess before God. The phrase "new and living way" uses vivid contrasts: *prosphaton* (lit. "freshly slain") paradoxically modifies "living"—the way is both death-opened and life-giving. The perfect participles *rerantismenoi* ("having been sprinkled") and *lelousmenoi* ("having been washed") indicate completed actions with continuing effects—believers were cleansed and remain clean.

**Hermeneutical Use:**
The author interprets the veil typologically as representing Christ's flesh—the barrier that had to be torn for access to God's presence. This interpretive move sees Christ's incarnate body simultaneously as the means of approach (through His humanity He represents us) and the barrier that was removed (through His death the way opened). The hermeneutical warrant lies in the veil's tearing at the crucifixion (Matthew 27:51), which the author understands not as coincidence but divine testimony: God tore the veil to signal that Christ's death opened permanent access. The "sprinkling" and "washing" imagery receives dual interpretation, referring both to Old Testament purification rituals (typologically) and Christian baptism (practically). The movement from shadow to substance continues: earthly veil prefigured Christ's body; restricted access prefigured universal access; annual entry prefigured continuous drawing near. The hermeneutical principle assumes that Old Testament barriers were divinely designed pointers to Christ, who would remove them.

**Theological Use:**
These verses establish crucial truths for believers' relationship with God: (1) Access to God's presence is now available to all believers, not restricted to one priest on one day; (2) This access is secured through "the blood of Jesus"—His sacrificial death as the necessary means; (3) The "new and living way" is inaugurated through Christ's flesh—His incarnation, death, and resurrection; (4) Believers can approach with full confidence (*parrēsia*) and complete assurance (*plērophoria*), not uncertainty or fear; (5) Internal purification ("hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience") and external identification ("bodies washed with pure water") prepare believers for approaching God; (6) The "great priest over the house of God" (v. 21) represents believers continuously before the Father. Systematic theology draws on this passage for doctrines of access to God, assurance of salvation, the priesthood of all believers, baptism's significance, and justification (hearts cleansed from guilty conscience).

**Rhetorical Use:**
The passage's rhetoric moves from privilege to exhortation, from indicative (what is true) to imperative (what to do). The opening "Therefore, brothers" (*oun, adelphoi*) signals transition from doctrine to practice, from what Christ accomplished to how believers should respond. The strong affirmation—"we have confidence"—creates assurance and courage. The description of Christ's work in vivid terms ("blood of Jesus," "new and living way," "through his flesh") grounds confidence in objective reality, not subjective feeling. The metaphor of the veil as Christ's flesh is startling and memorable—what blocked access (veil) was destroyed through what provided access (flesh). The call to "draw near" uses inclusive first-person plural—"let us"—creating community and shared responsibility. The qualifications listed (hearts sprinkled, bodies washed) assure readers that they meet the requirements through Christ's work and baptismal identification, not through personal merit. The rhetoric thus encourages bold approach to God based on Christ's finished work.

**Canonical Context:**
This passage synthesizes multiple biblical themes: Eden's lost access (Genesis 3:24) now restored; Sinai's terrifying distance (Exodus 19:12-13, 21-24; 20:18-19) now replaced with welcomed nearness; the tabernacle's restricted access (Leviticus 16:2) now opened to all; Ezekiel's promise of cleansing and new heart (Ezekiel 36:25-27) now fulfilled. The torn veil (Matthew 27:51; Mark 15:38; Luke 23:45) receives theological interpretation as Christ's flesh torn to open access. Paul's language of access to the Father (Ephesians 2:13, 18; 3:12) parallels Hebrews' exhortation. John's affirmation that Jesus is "the way" (John 14:6) and Peter's declaration that Christ brings us to God (1 Peter 3:18) resonate with Hebrews' imagery. The canonical trajectory moves from exclusion (cherubim guarding Eden) through mediated access (priests and sacrifices) to direct access (all believers drawing near through Christ). What began with "You shall not go up into the mountain" (Exodus 19:12) culminates in "Let us draw near" (Hebrews 10:22).

**Narrative Function:**
Within Hebrews' structure, these verses pivot from exposition to exhortation. The book has argued Christ's superiority (1:1-4:13), expounded His high priesthood (4:14-7:28), explained His sacrifice's effectiveness (8:1-10:18), and now applies these truths practically (10:19-13:25). The passage provides the theological foundation for the threefold exhortation: draw near (v. 22), hold fast (v. 23), and consider one another (v. 24). The "Therefore" (*oun*) connects all preceding argumentation to what follows—because Christ accomplished what has been described, believers should respond as commanded. The passage creates narrative transition from Christ's work (completed) to believers' response (ongoing). The description of believers' qualification ("hearts sprinkled... bodies washed") assures readers they can confidently approach, addressing potential hesitation or doubt. The narrative moves from Christ's achievement (past) to believers' access (present) to their responsibility (future exhortations).

**Intertextual Connections:**
The "confidence to enter the holy places" contrasts with Leviticus 16:2's warning to Aaron not to enter "at any time... lest he die." The "blood of Jesus" echoes the Last Supper: "This is my blood of the covenant, poured out for many for forgiveness of sins" (Matthew 26:28). The "new way" fulfills Jeremiah 31:31-34's new covenant promise. The veil imagery connects to Exodus 26:31-33 (veil construction) and especially Matthew 27:51 (veil torn). The identification of veil with "flesh" may allude to John's "the Word became flesh" (John 1:14) and "I am the way" (John 14:6). The "sprinkled... from an evil conscience" language recalls Numbers 19's purification ritual and Ezekiel 36:25 ("I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean"). The "washed with pure water" connects to baptismal language throughout the New Testament (Acts 22:16, "wash away your sins"; Ephesians 5:26, "washing of water with the word"; Titus 3:5, "washing of regeneration"). The "great priest over the house of God" echoes 3:6 ("Christ is faithful over God's house as a son") and Numbers 12:7 ("Moses is faithful in all my house").

**Christological Connection:**

[[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 10#Hebrews 10 . 19|Hebrews 10:19-22]] presents the practical benefits of Christ's high priestly work: believers now possess what was previously unthinkable—"confidence to enter the holy places." The word *parrēsia* ("boldness, confidence") indicates free speech, unrestricted access, the privilege of approaching without fear of rejection or punishment—a stark contrast to the Old Testament's restriction where unauthorized approach meant death ([[Readable Bible/03 - Leviticus/Leviticus 16#Leviticus 16 . 2|Leviticus 16:2]]). This confidence rests entirely on "the blood of Jesus"—not believers' worthiness, religious performance, or moral achievement, but Christ's sacrificial death as the sole basis for access. The phrase "by the blood" (*en tō haimati*) is instrumental: the blood is the means, the price, the effective agent opening access. Christ has "inaugurated for us a new and living way" (v. 20)—the verb *enekainisen* indicates a once-for-all dedication or opening ceremony, like the tabernacle's inauguration (Numbers 7) or the temple's dedication (1 Kings 8). What Moses inaugurated through animal sacrifices and ritual, Christ inaugurated through His own death. This way is "new" (*prosphaton*, lit. "freshly slain")—recently opened, not long-existing; and "living" (*zōsan*)—not a dead ritual but a dynamic, life-giving reality. The paradox is intentional: the way was opened through death (Christ's crucifixion) but is itself living (Christ's resurrection ensures it remains open). The author's identification of the veil with Christ's "flesh" (*sarx*) provides stunning theological insight: the veil that separated humanity from God's presence corresponds to Christ's human body, which had to be torn (in death) for the way to open. The tearing of the temple veil at Christ's crucifixion ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 27#Matthew 27 . 51|Matthew 27:51]]) becomes God's visual sermon: the barrier is removed, access is opened, the way is clear. But Christ is simultaneously the torn barrier and the opened way—His flesh was torn to open access; His incarnate life provides the path to approach. As John declares, "I am the way" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 14#John 14 . 6|John 14:6]])—Christ doesn't merely show the way; He is the way. The passage's confidence rests on the "great priest over the house of God" (v. 21)—not a dead ancestor but the living, exalted Christ who "always lives to make intercession" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 7#Hebrews 7 . 25|Hebrews 7:25]]) for those who draw near through Him. The call to "draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith" (v. 22) presupposes that believers meet the entrance requirements, but these requirements have been met for them: "hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience" refers to internal purification through Christ's blood (compare 9:14, "purify our conscience from dead works"); "bodies washed with pure water" likely alludes to baptism as the outward sign of inward cleansing. The perfect tense participles (*rerantismenoi*, *lelousmenoi*—"having been sprinkled," "having been washed") indicate completed actions with continuing effects—believers were cleansed at conversion/baptism and remain clean in God's sight. The trajectory extends from the Old Testament's terrifying restriction—"Take care not to go up into the mountain or touch the edge of it... whoever touches the mountain shall be put to death" ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 19#Exodus 19 . 12|Exodus 19:12]])—through Christ's opening of the "new and living way" to the New Testament's welcoming invitation—"Let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith" (v. 22). What Aaron could do once yearly with fear, believers can do continually with confidence. What required elaborate preparation under the old covenant requires only faith's response under the new covenant, because Christ has made the necessary preparation through His once-for-all sacrifice. The access secured is not merely to an earthly sanctuary but to "the holy places" (plural *hagia*, v. 19)—heaven itself, God's immediate presence, the true sanctuary where Christ now ministers. Because Christ has entered there as our forerunner and high priest, and because He has opened the way through His blood, believers can approach with complete assurance, not presumption but confidence grounded in Christ's finished work.
