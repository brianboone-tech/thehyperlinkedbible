**Context:**

Hebrews 4:14-16 forms a theological hinge connecting the book's exposition of Christ's superiority (chapters 1-4) with its detailed treatment of His high priestly ministry (chapters 5-10). Following the warning to enter God's rest (4:1-13) and the reminder that God's word judges hearts (4:12-13), these verses introduce the central theme of Christ's priesthood with pastoral warmth and theological precision. The passage presents Jesus as "a great high priest who has passed through the heavens," surpassing Aaron who entered only the earthly Holy of Holies. Yet this transcendent priest remains accessible, able to "sympathize with our weaknesses" because He was "tempted in every respect as we are, yet without sin." The rhetorical structure moves from affirmation ("we have a great high priest") to exhortation ("let us hold fast our confession") to encouragement ("let us with confidence draw near"). The "throne of grace" replaces the mercy seat of the old covenant, now accessible not once yearly through Aaron but continually through Jesus. This passage simultaneously elevates Christ (He passed through the heavens) and brings Him near (He sympathizes with our weakness), resolving what seems a paradox: the transcendent God becomes the accessible Mediator through the incarnate, tempted, yet sinless Son.

**Ninefold Analysis:**

**OT Context:**
The passage draws heavily on Leviticus 16's Day of Atonement imagery, where the high priest alone entered God's presence in the Most Holy Place once yearly, passing through the veil with atoning blood. The author's Jewish readers would immediately grasp the comparison: Aaron entered an earthly sanctuary; Jesus entered heaven itself. Aaron went "inside the veil" (Leviticus 16:2); Jesus passed "through the heavens" (v. 14). The mercy seat (*kapporeth*) where Aaron sprinkled blood becomes "the throne of grace" where believers now approach confidently. But a crucial difference emerges: Leviticus 16 prescribes strict limitations on access—only the high priest, only once yearly, only with blood, only after elaborate purification. Hebrews announces these barriers have been removed through Christ's superior priesthood. The Old Testament's distance and restriction give way to the New Testament's access and confidence. Yet the passage also connects to the suffering servant of Isaiah 53:4-5, who bore our griefs and was acquainted with our sorrows, able to sympathize because He experienced human weakness.

**Jewish Backgrounds:**
First-century Judaism held the high priesthood in highest regard, especially the dramatic annual Yom Kippur ceremony when the high priest entered God's presence on behalf of all Israel. Josephus describes the high priest's emergence from the Holy of Holies as the year's most anxious moment—if he survived, his prayers were accepted. Rabbinic tradition emphasized the high priest's mediatorial uniqueness: he alone bore the breastplate with Israel's tribal names, he alone spoke the divine Name (*YHWH*), he alone entered the inner sanctuary. Yet Judaism also recognized the high priesthood's human limitations. The Mishnah (Yoma 1:1) describes the high priest's seven-day sequestration before Yom Kippur, his need for purification, his own sin offering. Rabbinic interpretation of Isaiah 53 (before it became controversial in Jewish-Christian debate) sometimes applied it to the priesthood's burden of bearing Israel's iniquities. The author of Hebrews writes to Jewish Christians familiar with this priestly theology, showing them how Christ fulfills and surpasses every aspect of the Aaronic priesthood.

**Text Form:**
The passage employs a carefully structured rhetorical pattern: affirmation ("Since we have..."), dual exhortation ("let us hold fast... let us draw near"), and supporting rationale ("For we do not have..."). The Greek participle *dielēlythota* ("having passed through") is perfect tense, indicating completed action with continuing results—Christ's ascension is finished; His heavenly ministry continues. The phrase "in every respect" (*kata panta*, v. 15) is comprehensive and emphatic—Jesus' temptations covered the full range of human testing. The construction "yet without sin" (*chōris hamartias*) uses the adverb *chōris* (apart from, without) to emphasize Jesus' absolute sinlessness—He experienced temptation's full force but never yielded. The imperative "let us draw near" (*prosercḗmetha*, v. 16) uses the present tense, indicating continuous action—not once yearly like Aaron, but continually. The compound phrase "throne of grace" juxtaposes divine sovereignty (*throne*) with unmerited favor (*grace*), showing that God's power now operates for believers' benefit, not judgment.

**Hermeneutical Use:**
The author employs typological interpretation, seeing Aaron's high priestly ministry as a shadow anticipating Christ's reality. The movement from earthly sanctuary to heavenly reality follows the pattern established earlier in Hebrews: the old covenant provided "a copy and shadow of the heavenly things" (8:5). The hermeneutical move is both contrast (Christ is superior) and continuity (Christ fulfills the type). Where Aaron "passed through" the veil into the earthly Holy of Holies, Christ "passed through the heavens"—the language is similar, but the realities are categorically different. The perfect tense indicates Christ has completed what Aaron repeatedly attempted. The passage also demonstrates progressive revelation: the Old Testament revealed humanity's need for priestly mediation and atonement; the New Testament reveals the perfect mediator and final atonement in Christ. The writer's hermeneutic assumes that Scripture's historical institutions (priesthood, sacrifice, sanctuary) were divinely designed to prefigure Christ's work and that understanding Christ illuminates the Old Testament's meaning.

**Theological Use:**
This passage establishes foundational truths for Christ's priestly work: (1) Jesus is the "great high priest"—superior to Aaron and all his successors; (2) He "passed through the heavens"—His ministry occurs in the true sanctuary, heaven itself; (3) His priesthood rests on His incarnation and human experience—He "sympathizes" through genuine shared humanity; (4) His sinlessness qualifies Him uniquely—He experienced temptation's full force without yielding; (5) His priesthood provides believers confident access to God's throne; (6) What we receive is "mercy and grace"—undeserved favor, not earned reward. Systematic theology draws on these verses for Christology (Christ's two natures—fully divine, fully human), soteriology (how Christ's work saves), and practical theology (believers' access to God through prayer). The passage resolves a central soteriological question: How can the transcendent, holy God be approached by sinful humans? Answer: Through the mediating work of the great high priest who is both truly God (passed through the heavens) and truly human (sympathizes with our weaknesses).

**Rhetorical Use:**
The passage's rhetoric moves from doctrine to application, from Christ's achievement to believers' response. The opening "Since we have" (*echontes*, v. 14) appeals to a reality already possessed—the great high priest is ours now. The repeated "let us" (*echōmen*, v. 16) creates dual exhortation: holding fast (perseverance) and drawing near (access). The inclusion of "our confession" (v. 14) connects to the readers' initial faith commitment, urging them not to abandon what they originally believed. The vivid description of Jesus' humanity—"tempted in every respect as we are"—creates emotional connection and comfort. The dramatic invitation to approach "the throne of grace" with "confidence" (*parrēsia*, bold freedom of speech) contrasts sharply with Old Testament restrictions. The promise of finding "mercy and grace to help in time of need" addresses immediate, practical concerns. The rhetoric thus combines theological precision (Christ's dual qualification as divine and human), pastoral warmth (He understands our struggles), and practical encouragement (we can approach Him confidently).

**Canonical Context:**
These verses form the centerpiece of Hebrews' theological argument and connect to the broader New Testament witness to Christ's priesthood. The "great high priest" title appears uniquely in Hebrews, though the concept permeates John (Jesus as the way to the Father), Romans (Christ's intercession), and 1 John (Jesus as advocate). The theme of Christ's sympathetic identification connects to the incarnation accounts (Philippians 2:5-8; John 1:14) and the temptation narratives (Matthew 4:1-11; Luke 4:1-13). The "throne of grace" replaces Old Testament imagery of God's inaccessible throne (Isaiah 6:1-5; Ezekiel 1:26-28) with New Testament access through Christ. Paul's language of believers approaching God "with confidence through our faith in him" (Ephesians 3:12) parallels Hebrews' invitation. The trajectory runs from Old Testament distance and fear (Exodus 19:12-13; 20:18-19) through Christ's mediating work to New Testament access and confidence. What began with "Let us make a sanctuary for me, that I may dwell in their midst" (Exodus 25:8) culminates in "let us draw near... to the throne of grace" (v. 16).

**Narrative Function:**
Within Hebrews' argument, this passage transitions from warning (4:1-13) to exposition (5:1-10:18). Having warned readers about unbelief's danger using Israel's wilderness failure as example, the author now presents the remedy: Jesus, the great high priest who secures our entry into God's rest. The passage serves multiple narrative functions: (1) Introduces the central theme (Christ's priesthood) that will dominate chapters 5-10; (2) Provides pastoral comfort after stern warning—Christ sympathizes, believers can approach confidently; (3) Establishes the basis for later detailed argument about Melchizedekian priesthood; (4) Creates suspense—how exactly does Jesus' priesthood work? The answers unfold in subsequent chapters. The imperatives ("hold fast," "draw near") move readers from passive reception to active response, from doctrine to practice.

**Intertextual Connections:**
The "passed through the heavens" language connects to ancient Jewish cosmology (multiple heavens) and Paul's reference to the "third heaven" (2 Corinthians 12:2). The sympathy theme echoes the Suffering Servant's bearing our griefs (Isaiah 53:4) and Paul's assertion that Christ "knew no sin" yet became sin for us (2 Corinthians 5:21). The temptation reference connects to the Gospel accounts—wilderness temptation (Matthew 4:1-11), Gethsemane's anguish (Luke 22:42-44), and the cross's reproach (Matthew 27:39-44). The "throne of grace" transforms Psalm 47:8 ("God sits on his holy throne") and Isaiah 6:1 (seeing the Lord "sitting upon a throne") from terrifying to welcoming. The call to "draw near" fulfills James 4:8 ("Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you") and reverses the Old Testament prohibition: "Take care that you do not go up into the mountain or touch the edge of it... whoever touches the mountain shall be put to death" (Exodus 19:12). The promise of mercy connects to Psalm 103:13 ("As a father shows compassion to his children, so the LORD shows compassion to those who fear him") and Lamentations 3:22-23 ("The steadfast love of the LORD never ceases; his mercies never come to an end").

**Christological Connection:**

[[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 4#Hebrews 4 . 14|Hebrews 4:14-16]] presents Christ's priestly work through the lens of His two natures—fully divine and fully human—showing how both qualify Him uniquely as mediator between God and humanity. The phrase "great high priest who has passed through the heavens" emphasizes His divine transcendence and completed ascension: unlike Aaron who entered an earthly tent made with hands, Jesus entered "heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 9#Hebrews 9 . 24|Hebrews 9:24]]). His "greatness" consists in His eternal Sonship ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 1#Hebrews 1 . 1|Hebrews 1:1-3]]), His superiority to angels (1:4-2:18), and His faithful ministry surpassing Moses (3:1-6). Yet paradoxically, this transcendent priest remains intimately accessible because He "sympathizes with our weaknesses." The incarnation accomplished what Aaron's mere humanity could not: Christ became "like his brothers in every respect, so that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 2#Hebrews 2 . 17|Hebrews 2:17]]). His temptations were real, comprehensive ("in every respect as we are"), and severe—Gethsemane's agony demonstrated the intensity of His testing ([[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 22#Luke 22 . 44|Luke 22:44]]). Yet His sinlessness was absolute ("without sin"), qualifying Him to be both sacrifice and priest, both offering and offerer. Where Aaron needed atonement for his own sins before ministering for others ([[Readable Bible/03 - Leviticus/Leviticus 16#Leviticus 16 . 6|Leviticus 16:6]], 11), Christ "knew no sin" ([[Readable Bible/47 - 2 Corinthians/2 Corinthians 5#2 Corinthians 5 . 21|2 Corinthians 5:21]]), needing no preliminary purification. This combination—genuine humanity without sin—makes Him the perfect mediator: sympathetic through shared experience, effective through moral perfection. The passage's twin imperatives flow from this Christological foundation: "hold fast our confession" because we have a great high priest whose transcendent ministry never fails; "draw near with confidence" because He understands our weaknesses through incarnate experience. The "throne of grace" replaces the mercy seat of Leviticus 16, but with stunning transformation: what Aaron accessed once yearly through animal blood, believers access continually through Jesus' blood; what inspired terror ([[Readable Bible/03 - Leviticus/Leviticus 16#Leviticus 16 . 2|Leviticus 16:2]]—"Tell Aaron not to come at any time into the Holy Place... so that he may not die") now invites confidence; what required elaborate ritual preparation now requires only faith. The promise to "receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need" assures believers that Christ's intercession actively aids them—He not only sympathizes but intervenes, not only understands but assists. His heavenly priesthood means God's sovereign power ("throne") operates for believers' benefit through unmerited favor ("grace"). What Aaron's ministry could never accomplish—perfecting the conscience, securing permanent access, guaranteeing eternal salvation—Christ achieves through His superior priesthood. The trajectory extends from Leviticus 16's annual ritual through Christ's once-for-all entry into heaven to believers' daily access to the throne of grace, where they find the risen, interceding, sympathetic Savior ready to help in every moment of need.
