### [[Readable Bible/15 - Ezra/Ezra 3#Ezra 3 . 3|Ezra 3:3-7]]

**Context**: The post-exilic community, despite fear of surrounding peoples and lacking a rebuilt temple, immediately sets up the altar and reinstates the daily burnt offerings "as it is written in the Law of Moses." This demonstrates the enduring authority of the Aaronic sacrificial system and Israel's understanding that covenant relationship with God requires blood sacrifice. Their priority—altar before temple, sacrifice before structure—shows that priestly mediation through blood was non-negotiable for restored Israel.

**Hebrew Key Terms**:
- עֹלוֹת (*olot*) - "burnt offerings" (plural, continual sacrifices)
- תָּמִיד (*tamid*) - "regular/continual" (perpetual daily offering)
- כָּתוּב (*katuv*) - "written" (canonical authority)
- מוֹעֵד (*moed*) - "appointed feast" (sacred calendar)

**Christological Connection**: Ezra 3 reveals both the glory and futility of the Aaronic system. The glory: even after seventy years of exile, even without a temple standing, Israel knew covenant relationship required priestly mediation through blood—they could not approach God without sacrifice. The futility: despite the cessation during exile proving these sacrifices ineffective to maintain God's presence (the temple was destroyed!), they resume the same system, hoping repetition will succeed where previous generations failed. This demonstrates what [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 10#Hebrews 10 . 1|Hebrews 10:1-4]] argues explicitly—the law's sacrifices were "a reminder of sins every year" but "can never take away sins." Christ fulfills what Ezra's restoration only foreshadowed: permanent atonement that doesn't require daily repetition. Where Ezra's priests "stood daily" offering sacrifices ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 10#Hebrews 10 . 11|Hebrews 10:11]]), Christ "sat down" having accomplished eternal redemption ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 10#Hebrews 10 . 12|Hebrews 10:12]]). The post-exilic restoration, faithful as it was, still pointed beyond itself to the coming priest whose single offering would accomplish what centuries of daily sacrifice could not.
