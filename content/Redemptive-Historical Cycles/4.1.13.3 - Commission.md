# 4.1.13.3 - Commission (Kings - United and Divided Monarchy)

## Overview: The Royal Calling to Shepherd God's People

The commission given to Israel's kings represents the fullest articulation of the Adamic mandate within the Old Testament theocracy. The king is not merely a political ruler but a covenant mediator, a corporate representative who stands between God and the people, called to embody righteousness, execute justice, guard the sanctuary, expand God's kingdom, and shepherd the flock of Israel. This royal commission finds its foundational expression in [[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 17#Deuteronomy 17 . 14|Deuteronomy 17:14-20]], its covenantal elaboration in 2 Samuel 7, and its practical demonstration in the reigns of David and Solomon.

The commission entails both privileges and responsibilities. The king is God's adopted son ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 7#2 Samuel 7 . 14|2 Samuel 7:14]]), the anointed one through whom God rules His people. He sits on "the throne of the LORD" ([[Readable Bible/13 - 1 Chronicles/1 Chronicles 29#1 Chronicles 29 . 23|1 Chronicles 29:23]]), exercises dominion over enemies, administers justice and righteousness, builds and guards the temple-sanctuary, and serves as the channel through which God's blessing flows to Israel and the nations. But he must do so in radical dependence on God's word, in covenant faithfulness, and in humble service—functioning as a shepherd-king after the pattern of David rather than as an autonomous monarch "like the nations."

The tragic failure of Israel's kings to fulfill this commission—even David and Solomon fall short—demonstrates that no mere human can bear the weight of this calling. The commission itself is not flawed; the kings are. This creates the eschatological longing for the true King, the ultimate Son of David, who will perfectly fulfill every aspect of the royal commission and establish God's kingdom forever. The commission to Israel's kings thus becomes a prophetic outline of the work of the Messiah.

## The Deuteronomic Charter: Laws for the King

### The Context: Anticipating Kingship

Moses, in his final address to Israel before they enter the Promised Land, anticipates the eventual establishment of kingship. Deuteronomy 17:14-20 provides the constitutional framework for how Israel's king must operate within the covenant structure. Significantly, God does not initiate kingship here; rather, He regulates it, knowing that Israel will request a king "like all the nations that are around me" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 17#Deuteronomy 17 . 14|Deuteronomy 17:14]]).

This is crucial: kingship in Israel is permissible but must be fundamentally different from pagan monarchy. The Israelite king is not absolute sovereign but covenant servant. He does not rule autonomously but under God's law. He is not chosen by dynastic succession or military prowess alone but by divine election: "you may indeed set a king over you whom the LORD your God will choose" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 17#Deuteronomy 17 . 15|Deuteronomy 17:15]]).

### Three Prohibitions: What the King Must Not Do

The Deuteronomic charter begins with three negative commands that define the king by contrast with pagan monarchs:

**1. The king must be from among Israel's brothers**: "One from among your brothers you shall set as king over you. You may not put a foreigner over you, who is not your brother" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 17#Deuteronomy 17 . 15|Deuteronomy 17:15]]).

This prohibition ensures covenant solidarity. The king must share in Israel's history, identity, and covenant obligations. He is not a distant autocrat but a fellow Israelite who knows the struggles, hopes, and calling of the people. He is a representative figure, a corporate personality, who embodies the nation.

Beale notes that this requirement prepares for the ultimate king who is truly our "brother," the Son of God who becomes the Son of Man, fully identified with those He represents ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 2#Hebrews 2 . 11|Hebrews 2:11-12]], 17).

**2. The king must not multiply horses or cause the people to return to Egypt**: "Only he must not acquire many horses for himself or cause the people to return to Egypt in order to acquire many horses, since the LORD has said to you, 'You shall never return that way again'" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 17#Deuteronomy 17 . 16|Deuteronomy 17:16]]).

Horses represent military power and self-reliance. Egypt represents the house of bondage from which God delivered Israel. To multiply horses or to return to Egypt for military alliance is to trust in human strength rather than divine deliverance. It reverses the Exodus, the foundational act of God's salvation.

This prohibition is designed to keep the king dependent on God rather than military might. As [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalms 20#Psalms 20 . 7|Psalm 20:7]] declares, "Some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of the LORD our God." The king's security must rest not in armaments but in covenant faithfulness.

Solomon spectacularly violates this command. He accumulates 1,400 chariots and 12,000 horsemen ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 10#1 Kings 10 . 26|1 Kings 10:26]]) and establishes a trade in horses from Egypt ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 10#1 Kings 10 . 28|1 Kings 10:28-29]]), demonstrating his drift toward self-reliance and away from covenant dependence.

**3. The king must not multiply wives or excessive wealth**: "And he shall not acquire many wives for himself, lest his heart turn away, nor shall he acquire for himself excessive silver and gold" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 17#Deuteronomy 17 . 17|Deuteronomy 17:17]]).

Multiple wives represent political alliances (foreign princesses cementing treaties) and sensual excess. The danger is explicitly stated: "lest his heart turn away." Foreign wives will bring foreign gods, corrupting the king's covenant loyalty. Solomon's 700 wives and 300 concubines ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 11#1 Kings 11 . 3|1 Kings 11:3]]) turn his heart after other gods ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 11#1 Kings 11 . 4|1 Kings 11:4]]), fulfilling this warning precisely.

Excessive silver and gold represent economic exploitation and the creation of a royal elite class distinct from the people. The king is not to enrich himself at the expense of his brothers. Deuteronomy's vision is of a king who remains fundamentally one with the people, not an oriental despot living in luxury while his subjects suffer.

These three prohibitions—no foreign king, no multiplication of horses, no multiplication of wives or wealth—are designed to prevent the king from becoming "like the nations." They aim to preserve the unique character of theocratic kingship: covenantal, dependent, brotherly, focused on God rather than human power.

### The Positive Commission: What the King Must Do

After the prohibitions come the positive requirements, which constitute the king's active commission:

**1. Write and read the law continually**: "And when he sits on the throne of his kingdom, he shall write for himself in a book a copy of this law, approved by the Levitical priests. And it shall be with him, and he shall read in it all the days of his life" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 17#Deuteronomy 17 . 18|Deuteronomy 17:18-19]]).

The king's first task is not military conquest or administrative organization but personal engagement with God's word. He must personally copy the law (not merely possess a copy made by scribes), ensuring deep familiarity with its contents. This copy must be approved by the Levitical priests, placing the king under the authority of the covenant mediators and preventing royal manipulation of the text.

The law must be "with him"—constantly accessible—and he must "read in it all the days of his life"—continual meditation, not occasional consultation. This is the same pattern commended to Joshua: "This Book of the Law shall not depart from your mouth, but you shall meditate on it day and night, so that you may be careful to do according to all that is written in it" ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 1#Joshua 1 . 8|Joshua 1:8]]).

The king is to be the model Torah-keeper, the chief student of God's word, the living embodiment of covenant faithfulness. He does not stand above the law but under it. He does not make law but submits to it. He is the visible demonstration that Israel is a people ruled by divine instruction, not human whim.

Psalm 1 describes the blessed man who meditates on the law day and night, and this is the ideal king—rooted in God's word, fruitful in his reign, prospering in his way.

**2. Learn to fear the Lord and keep all His commands**: "that he may learn to fear the LORD his God by keeping all the words of this law and these statutes, and doing them" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 17#Deuteronomy 17 . 19|Deuteronomy 17:19]]).

The purpose of constant engagement with the law is not academic knowledge but existential transformation. The king must "learn to fear the LORD"—develop a heart posture of reverence, awe, trust, and obedience. This fear is not terror but covenant love, the recognition that Yahweh alone is God and that His commands are good, wise, and life-giving.

Keeping all the words of the law is comprehensive obedience. The king cannot pick and choose which commands to obey or modify God's requirements according to political expediency. He is bound by the same covenant obligations as the humblest Israelite. This is radical: in the ancient Near East, kings were often considered above the law or even the source of law. But Israel's king is subject to divine law.

**3. Avoid pride and turning aside from the commandments**: "that his heart may not be lifted up above his brothers, and that he may not turn aside from the commandment, either to the right hand or to the left" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 17#Deuteronomy 17 . 20|Deuteronomy 17:20]]).

Pride is the perennial temptation of power. The king, elevated above his brothers in position and honor, must guard against considering himself inherently superior. He is not a different kind of being but remains one of the brothers, chosen for service, not for self-exaltation.

"That he may not turn aside from the commandment, either to the right hand or to the left" employs the language of Torah obedience used throughout Deuteronomy (5:32, 28:14). The king must walk the straight path of covenant faithfulness, neither adding to God's commands (turning to the right) nor subtracting from them (turning to the left).

**4. Ensure dynastic longevity through obedience**: "so that he may continue long in his kingdom, he and his children, in Israel" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 17#Deuteronomy 17 . 20|Deuteronomy 17:20]]).

The promise of dynastic continuity is explicitly tied to covenant obedience. Unlike pagan monarchies where succession is secured by military power or political intrigue, Israel's kingship endures through faithfulness to God's word. This connects directly to the Davidic covenant's conditional aspects: individual kings will be disciplined for sin, but the dynasty continues because of God's promise.

The mention of "his children" anticipates multi-generational faithfulness. The king is to model Torah obedience so that his sons follow the same path. This creates the expectation of a lasting dynasty, fulfilled ultimately in the eternal Son of David.

### Theological Significance of the Deuteronomic Charter

Kline emphasizes that Deuteronomy 17 establishes the king as covenant servant rather than autonomous sovereign. The king is subordinated to the prophetic word (the law mediated through Moses and administered by the Levitical priests). He exercises derivative authority, ruling on behalf of the divine King. This creates a system of checks and balances: prophets confront kings with God's word (Nathan to David, Elijah to Ahab), and the law stands in judgment over royal actions.

Beale sees the charter as defining the king's Adamic role. Like Adam, the king is placed in a position of authority but under divine command. He must meditate on God's word (as Adam heard God's instruction), exercise dominion in humble obedience (not autonomous self-assertion), and avoid the pride that led to the original fall. The king is the new Adam figure who, if faithful, will succeed where Adam failed; if unfaithful, will recapitulate Adam's fall.

The charter's requirements find their ultimate fulfillment in Jesus Christ, who perfectly embodies every element: He is the true brother who shares our nature, He trusts in God rather than human power (refusing Satan's offer of worldly kingdoms), He remains undivided in heart toward God, He meditates on and perfectly obeys God's word, He walks humbly as servant-king, and His kingdom is established forever through His perfect righteousness.

## The Davidic Covenant: The King as Son of God

### The Commission Inherent in Sonship

When God establishes His covenant with David in 2 Samuel 7, He defines the king's commission in relational, filial terms: "I will be to him a father, and he shall be to me a son" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 7#2 Samuel 7 . 14|2 Samuel 7:14]]).

This father-son language establishes the Davidic king as God's unique representative, His adopted son, the one through whom He exercises royal authority in the world. The king is not merely a political functionary but a covenant son, enjoying intimate relationship with God and representing God to the people.

Psalm 2, the great royal coronation psalm, elaborates this sonship: "I will tell of the decree: The LORD said to me, 'You are my Son; today I have begotten you. Ask of me, and I will make the nations your heritage, and the ends of the earth your possession. You shall break them with a rod of iron and dash them in pieces like a potter's vessel'" ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 2#Psalm 2 . 7|Psalm 2:7-9]]).

The decree declares the king as God's Son, begotten through the act of enthronement (the coronation is the "today" of begetting to kingship). The commission inherent in this sonship is global dominion—"the nations your heritage, the ends of the earth your possession." This is the Abrahamic promise channeled through the Davidic king: blessing and authority extending to all nations.

Psalm 89 further develops the sonship theme: "He shall cry to me, 'You are my Father, my God, and the Rock of my salvation.' And I will make him the firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth" ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 89#Psalm 89 . 26|Psalm 89:26-27]]). The king's filial relationship with God ("You are my Father") grounds his supremacy among earthly kings ("the highest of the kings of the earth"). His commission is to rule the nations as God's firstborn son, the preeminent heir.

Beale observes that this sonship language connects the Davidic king to Adam, who was also God's son ([[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 3#Luke 3 . 38|Luke 3:38]]), created in God's image to exercise dominion. The king is the corporate Adam, the representative son who is commissioned to fulfill the original Adamic mandate: exercise dominion over the earth, subdue chaos, extend God's rule, and bring creation under God's righteous reign.

The ultimate fulfillment is Jesus, declared to be "the Son of God in power according to the Spirit of holiness by his resurrection from the dead" ([[Readable Bible/45 - Romans/Romans 1#Romans 1 . 4|Romans 1:4]]), who receives "all authority in heaven and on earth" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 28#Matthew 28 . 18|Matthew 28:18]]) and commissions His church to disciple the nations.

### Specific Commissions in the Davidic Narrative

Throughout the David and Solomon narratives, specific aspects of the kingly commission are articulated:

**1. Shepherd God's people**: When the elders of Israel come to anoint David king, they recall God's word: "You shall be shepherd of my people Israel, and you shall be prince over Israel" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 5#2 Samuel 5 . 2|2 Samuel 5:2]]).

The shepherd metaphor is central to the kingly commission. The king is not a distant autocrat but a shepherd who knows his flock, guides them to pasture, protects them from predators, and lays down his life for the sheep. Ezekiel 34 elaborates this commission, condemning the shepherds of Israel who feed themselves rather than the flock ([[Readable Bible/26 - Ezekiel/Ezekiel 34#Ezekiel 34 . 2|Ezekiel 34:2-10]]) and promising that God will raise up "one shepherd, my servant David" to feed them ([[Readable Bible/26 - Ezekiel/Ezekiel 34#Ezekiel 34 . 23|Ezekiel 34:23]]).

Jesus explicitly claims this commission: "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 10#John 10 . 11|John 10:11]]). He is the true David who shepherds God's people with perfect care.

**2. Execute justice and righteousness**: "So David reigned over all Israel. And David administered justice and equity to all his people" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 8#2 Samuel 8 . 15|2 Samuel 8:15]]).

Justice (mishpat) and righteousness (tsedaqah) are the foundational requirements of kingship. The king must ensure that the oppressed receive justice, that the poor are defended, that the wicked are punished, and that equity prevails in society. This is not optional; it defines the king's role.

Psalm 72, Solomon's royal psalm, elaborates: "Give the king your justice, O God, and your righteousness to the royal son! May he judge your people with righteousness, and your poor with justice!... For he delivers the needy when he calls, the poor and him who has no helper. He has pity on the weak and the needy, and saves the lives of the needy. From oppression and violence he redeems their life, and precious is their blood in his sight" ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 72#Psalm 72 . 1|Psalm 72:1-2, 12-14]]).

The prophets repeatedly condemn Israel's kings for failing this commission. [[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 1#Isaiah 1 . 17|Isaiah 1:17]] commands: "learn to do good; seek justice, correct oppression; bring justice to the fatherless, plead the widow's cause." [[Readable Bible/24 - Jeremiah/Jeremiah 22#Jeremiah 22 . 3|Jeremiah 22:3]] similarly: "Do justice and righteousness, and deliver from the hand of the oppressor him who has been robbed. And do no wrong or violence to the resident alien, the fatherless, and the widow."

Jesus fulfills this commission perfectly. He proclaims good news to the poor, liberty to captives, recovery of sight to the blind, and freedom for the oppressed ([[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 4#Luke 4 . 18|Luke 4:18-19]]). His kingdom is one of justice and righteousness forever.

**3. Build the house for God's name**: God tells David, "He shall build a house for my name, and I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 7#2 Samuel 7 . 13|2 Samuel 7:13]]).

Building the temple is not merely a construction project but the establishment of the place where God's name dwells, the sanctuary where heaven and earth meet, the center from which blessing flows to the world. The king's commission includes creating and maintaining sacred space, ensuring that God's presence has a dwelling among His people.

David gathers materials, organizes personnel, and provides plans ([[Readable Bible/13 - 1 Chronicles/1 Chronicles 22#1 Chronicles 22 . 1|1 Chronicles 22-29]]), but Solomon executes the building ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 6#1 Kings 6 . 1|1 Kings 6-8]]). The temple becomes the focal point of Israel's worship and the physical manifestation of God's covenant commitment to dwell with His people.

Jesus announces, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 2#John 2 . 19|John 2:19]]). John explains: "He was speaking about the temple of his body" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 2#John 2 . 21|John 2:21]]). Jesus is the ultimate fulfillment of the temple-building commission—He is the temple, the place where God fully dwells in bodily form, the meeting point of heaven and earth.

**4. Extend God's kingdom to the nations**: Psalm 72 envisions the king's reign extending globally: "May he have dominion from sea to sea, and from the River to the ends of the earth!... May all kings fall down before him, all nations serve him!" ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 72#Psalm 72 . 8|Psalm 72:8, 11]]).

The king's commission is not merely national but international. Through the Davidic king, the Abrahamic promise of blessing to all nations is to be realized. The nations are to come to Jerusalem, recognize Yahweh as God, and submit to His anointed king.

Solomon's reign prefigures this commission. The Queen of Sheba comes from afar to hear his wisdom ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 10#1 Kings 10 . 1|1 Kings 10:1-13]]), and "the whole earth sought the presence of Solomon to hear his wisdom, which God had put into his mind" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 10#1 Kings 10 . 24|1 Kings 10:24]]). But this remains incomplete—Solomon marries foreign women rather than converting them, and the nations remain largely outside God's covenant people.

Jesus fulfills this commission through the Great Commission: "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Go therefore and make disciples of all nations" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 28#Matthew 28 . 18|Matthew 28:18-19]]). Through the gospel, people from every nation are brought into the kingdom of the Son of David.

**5. Meditate on and obey God's law**: Psalm 119, the great Torah psalm, presents the ideal of one who delights in God's law, meditates on it continually, and orders his life by it. This is the kingly ideal established in Deuteronomy 17.

David, despite his failures, is characterized as a man who delights in God's law. Psalm 1 describes the blessed man who meditates on the law day and night, and tradition associates this psalm with David. His psalms repeatedly express love for God's commandments and commitment to obey them.

Jesus perfectly fulfills this commission. He declares, "I have come down from heaven, not to do my own will but the will of him who sent me" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 6#John 6 . 38|John 6:38]]). He keeps His Father's commandments perfectly ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 15#John 15 . 10|John 15:10]]) and lives by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 4#Matthew 4 . 4|Matthew 4:4]]).

## Specific Royal Responsibilities in the Monarchic Period

### Military Leadership: Defeating God's Enemies

The king is commissioned to lead Israel's armies against their enemies. This is not mere political warfare but covenant warfare—defending God's people and extending God's kingdom against forces of chaos and rebellion.

David's military victories are framed as God-given: "And the LORD gave victory to David wherever he went" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 8#2 Samuel 8 . 6|2 Samuel 8:6, 14]]). He defeats Philistines, Moabites, Arameans, Edomites, and Ammonites, establishing Israel's dominance in the region.

Solomon's reign is characterized by peace: "And Judah and Israel lived in safety, from Dan even to Beersheba, every man under his vine and under his fig tree, all the days of Solomon" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 4#1 Kings 4 . 25|1 Kings 4:25]]). This peace is the fruit of David's victories—the king has defeated the enemies and established secure borders.

The commission to defeat enemies ultimately points to Christ's victory over the true enemies: sin, death, and Satan. [[Readable Bible/51 - Colossians/Colossians 2#Colossians 2 . 15|Colossians 2:15]] declares that Christ "disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him." The cross is the ultimate battlefield where the Son of David wins cosmic victory.

### Judicial Leadership: Establishing Courts and Hearing Appeals

The king serves as the supreme judge, the court of final appeal. When the two prostitutes cannot resolve their dispute, they come to Solomon ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 3#1 Kings 3 . 16|1 Kings 3:16-28]]). His wise judgment establishes his reputation as a just king.

Jehoshaphat, one of Judah's better kings, organizes the judicial system: "He appointed judges in the land in all the fortified cities of Judah, city by city, and said to the judges, 'Consider what you do, for you judge not for man but for the LORD. He is with you in giving judgment. Now then, let the fear of the LORD be upon you. Be careful what you do, for there is no injustice with the LORD our God, or partiality or taking bribes'" ([[Readable Bible/14 - 2 Chronicles/2 Chronicles 19#2 Chronicles 19 . 5|2 Chronicles 19:5-7]]).

The king's commission includes ensuring that justice flows throughout the land, that judges are appointed who fear God and administer righteousness, and that the oppressed have recourse to fair judgment.

Jesus fulfills this commission as the ultimate Judge. The Father "has given all judgment to the Son" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 5#John 5 . 22|John 5:22]]), and He will judge the living and the dead ([[Readable Bible/55 - 2 Timothy/2 Timothy 4#2 Timothy 4 . 1|2 Timothy 4:1]]). His judgments are perfectly righteous, based on perfect knowledge, and render perfect justice.

### Priestly Leadership: Offering Sacrifices and Blessing the People

Though not priests in the Levitical sense, Israel's kings exercise certain priestly functions. David wears a linen ephod (priestly garment) when bringing the ark to Jerusalem ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 6#2 Samuel 6 . 14|2 Samuel 6:14]]) and offers sacrifices ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 6#2 Samuel 6 . 17|2 Samuel 6:17-18]]). Solomon offers sacrifices at the temple dedication ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 8#1 Kings 8 . 62|1 Kings 8:62-64]]) and blesses the people ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 8#1 Kings 8 . 14|1 Kings 8:14, 55]]).

Psalm 110 explicitly designates the Davidic king as priest: "The LORD has sworn and will not change his mind, 'You are a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek'" ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 110#Psalm 110 . 4|Psalm 110:4]]). This mysterious statement indicates that the Davidic king exercises a priesthood distinct from the Levitical order, patterned after Melchizedek, the priest-king of Salem who blessed Abraham ([[Readable Bible/01 - Genesis/Genesis 14#Genesis 14 . 18|Genesis 14:18-20]]).

The Book of Hebrews extensively develops this theme, showing that Jesus is the ultimate priest-king: "Now the point in what we are saying is this: we have such a high priest, one who is seated at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in heaven" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 8#Hebrews 8 . 1|Hebrews 8:1]]). Jesus is simultaneously King (seated on the throne) and Priest (offering sacrifice), fulfilling the integrated commission that Israel's kings could only partially embody.

### Economic Leadership: Administering Resources

The king is responsible for the economic wellbeing of the nation. He collects taxes, organizes labor, manages building projects, establishes trade agreements, and ensures prosperity.

Solomon's administrative genius is detailed in 1 Kings 4. He divides the kingdom into twelve districts, each responsible for provisioning the royal household one month per year ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 4#1 Kings 4 . 7|1 Kings 4:7]]). He establishes extensive trade networks, bringing in wealth from distant lands ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 10#1 Kings 10 . 14|1 Kings 10:14-29]]).

However, this also reveals the danger of the king's economic commission. Solomon's building projects and royal luxury require heavy taxation and forced labor ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 5#1 Kings 5 . 13|1 Kings 5:13-16]]), leading to the people's complaint under Rehoboam: "Your father made our yoke heavy. Now therefore lighten the hard service of your father and his heavy yoke on us, and we will serve you" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 12#1 Kings 12 . 4|1 Kings 12:4]]).

The commission includes ensuring prosperity, but not at the expense of oppressing the people. The king is to enrich the nation, not exploit it. Jesus, the true King, brings abundance without oppression: "I came that they may have life and have it abundantly" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 10#John 10 . 10|John 10:10]]).

### Spiritual Leadership: Maintaining Covenant Faithfulness

Supremely, the king is commissioned to lead the nation in covenant faithfulness. He sets the spiritual tone. When the king is faithful, the nation tends toward obedience; when the king is apostate, the nation follows into idolatry.

This pattern is evident throughout Kings and Chronicles. The summary statements consistently link the king's spiritual state to the nation's: "And he did what was evil in the sight of the LORD" is followed by national apostasy; "And he did what was right in the eyes of the LORD" is followed by reform and covenant renewal.

Hezekiah's reformation demonstrates this commission. He cleanses the temple, restores proper worship, celebrates Passover, and invites even the remnant of the Northern Kingdom to participate ([[Readable Bible/14 - 2 Chronicles/2 Chronicles 30#2 Chronicles 30 . 1|2 Chronicles 30:1-27]]). The people respond: "And Hezekiah and all the people rejoiced because God had provided for the people, for the thing came about suddenly" ([[Readable Bible/14 - 2 Chronicles/2 Chronicles 29#2 Chronicles 29 . 36|2 Chronicles 29:36]]).

Josiah similarly leads comprehensive reform, and all the people join in renewing the covenant ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 23#2 Kings 23 . 3|2 Kings 23:3]]). The king's spiritual leadership is the decisive factor in national faithfulness.

Jesus fulfills this commission perfectly. He is the pioneer and perfecter of faith ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 12#Hebrews 12 . 2|Hebrews 12:2]]), the one who leads His people in perfect obedience, establishes a new covenant in His blood, and creates a people holy and blameless before God.

## The Commission to Guard and Expand the Temple-Kingdom

### The King as Guardian of Sacred Space

Beale emphasizes that the king's commission includes guarding the temple-sanctuary, just as Adam was commissioned to guard the garden-sanctuary ([[Readable Bible/01 - Genesis/Genesis 2#Genesis 2 . 15|Genesis 2:15]]—the Hebrew shamar can mean "guard" or "keep"). The king ensures that worship remains pure, that idolatry does not infiltrate the sanctuary, and that God's presence is honored.

David's concern for the ark and his desire to build a house for God's name ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 7#2 Samuel 7 . 2|2 Samuel 7:2]]) reflect this commission. Solomon's building and dedication of the temple are the fulfillment of this guarding role—he creates the permanent structure for God's dwelling.

The godly kings who reform Judah consistently focus on the temple. Asa removes abominable idols and renews the altar ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 15#1 Kings 15 . 12|1 Kings 15:12-13]]). Hezekiah cleanses the temple and restores proper worship ([[Readable Bible/14 - 2 Chronicles/2 Chronicles 29#2 Chronicles 29 . 3|2 Chronicles 29:3-36]]). Josiah repairs the temple and discovers the lost Book of the Law ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 22#2 Kings 22 . 3|2 Kings 22:3-13]]). Each recognizes that guarding the sanctuary is central to his royal commission.

Conversely, wicked kings defile or neglect the temple. Manasseh builds altars to false gods in the temple courts ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 21#2 Kings 21 . 4|2 Kings 21:4-5]]), erecting idolatrous altars in the very place consecrated to Yahweh's name. This is the ultimate failure of the guarding commission—the king himself introduces the serpent into the garden.

Jesus fulfills the guarding commission dramatically. He cleanses the temple, driving out money-changers and merchants: "It is written, 'My house shall be called a house of prayer,' but you make it a den of robbers" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 21#Matthew 21 . 13|Matthew 21:13]]). He is zealous for His Father's house ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 2#John 2 . 17|John 2:17]]), guarding its sanctity. Ultimately, as the true temple Himself, He guards His people from all defilement, presenting them holy and blameless.

### The King as Expander of God's Kingdom

The commission also includes expanding the boundaries of God's kingdom. This has both geographical and spiritual dimensions.

Geographically, David conquers surrounding nations and extends Israel's borders to their widest extent, approximating the boundaries promised to Abraham ([[Readable Bible/01 - Genesis/Genesis 15#Genesis 15 . 18|Genesis 15:18]]). This is the physical expansion of the kingdom, pushing back chaos and establishing God's rule over more territory.

Spiritually, the king is commissioned to bring the nations into submission to Yahweh. This is the ultimate expansion—not merely political dominance but spiritual transformation, the nations coming to worship Israel's God.

Psalm 72 envisions this: "May all kings fall down before him, all nations serve him!... May people be blessed in him, all nations call him blessed!" ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 72#Psalm 72 . 11|Psalm 72:11, 17]]). The Davidic king is the means by which God's blessing reaches all nations, fulfilling the Abrahamic promise.

Solomon's reign provides a foretaste. The Queen of Sheba comes and blesses Yahweh ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 10#1 Kings 10 . 9|1 Kings 10:9]]). People from all nations come to hear Solomon's wisdom ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 4#1 Kings 4 . 34|1 Kings 4:34]]). The temple becomes a place of prayer for foreigners ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 8#1 Kings 8 . 41|1 Kings 8:41-43]]). This is kingdom expansion in its truest sense—the nations being drawn to God's presence.

Jesus fulfills this commission through the gospel. His kingdom expands not through military conquest but through proclamation. The Great Commission sends disciples to "all nations" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 28#Matthew 28 . 19|Matthew 28:19]]). Acts chronicles this expansion: Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, and to the ends of the earth ([[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 1#Acts 1 . 8|Acts 1:8]]). Revelation portrays the consummated kingdom as including "a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages" ([[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 7#Revelation 7 . 9|Revelation 7:9]]).

The king's commission to expand God's kingdom reaches its fulfillment in Christ's eternal reign over a redeemed humanity from all nations.

## The Prophetic Commission: Kings and Prophets in Tension

### The King Under the Prophetic Word

A crucial aspect of the kingly commission is submission to the prophetic word. The king does not have autonomous authority but rules under God's revealed will, which comes through the prophets.

This is established in Deuteronomy 17 where the king must have a copy of the law "approved by the Levitical priests" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 17#Deuteronomy 17 . 18|Deuteronomy 17:18]]), placing him under the authority of covenant mediators.

Throughout the monarchic period, prophets confront kings with God's word:

- Samuel confronts Saul for offering unauthorized sacrifice ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 13#1 Samuel 13 . 13|1 Samuel 13:13-14]]) and for incomplete obedience regarding Amalek ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 22|1 Samuel 15:22-23]])
- Nathan confronts David for adultery and murder ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 7|2 Samuel 12:7-12]])
- Ahijah prophesies the kingdom's division due to Solomon's apostasy ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 11#1 Kings 11 . 29|1 Kings 11:29-39]])
- Elijah confronts Ahab for Baal worship and for seizing Naboth's vineyard ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 21#1 Kings 21 . 17|1 Kings 21:17-24]])
- Isaiah counsels Hezekiah during the Assyrian crisis ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 19#2 Kings 19 . 6|2 Kings 19:6-7]])
- Jeremiah warns the kings of Judah about coming judgment ([[Readable Bible/24 - Jeremiah/Jeremiah 22#Jeremiah 22 . 1|Jeremiah 22:1-23:8]])

The king's commission includes listening to the prophetic word and submitting to it. When kings heed the prophets, blessing follows; when they reject or persecute the prophets, judgment comes.

This creates a system of checks on royal power. The king cannot simply do as he pleases. He is accountable to God's word mediated through the prophets. This is unique in the ancient Near East, where kings were often considered above criticism or even divine themselves.

Jesus perfectly fulfills this aspect of the commission. He is Himself the ultimate Prophet (Deuteronomy 18:15-19; Acts 3:22-23), yet He also perfectly submits to the Father's word. He does nothing on His own authority but only what He sees the Father doing ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 5#John 5 . 19|John 5:19]]). He is simultaneously the Speaker of God's word and the Perfect Hearer and Obeyer of it.

### The Prophetic Hope for the Ideal King

Because Israel's kings consistently fail their commission, the prophets develop an eschatological hope for the future ideal King who will perfectly fulfill all righteousness.

Isaiah prophesies: "For to us a child is born, to us a son is given; and the government shall be upon his shoulder, and his name shall be called Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace. Of the increase of his government and of peace there will be no end, on the throne of David and over his kingdom, to establish it and to uphold it with justice and with righteousness from this time forth and forevermore" ([[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 9#Isaiah 9 . 6|Isaiah 9:6-7]]).

This King will have divine attributes (Mighty God, Everlasting Father), rule eternally (no end), and establish justice and righteousness perfectly. He will fulfill the commission that David, Solomon, and all their successors failed to complete.

Isaiah 11 describes Him as the Branch from Jesse's stump, empowered by the Spirit, judging with perfect righteousness, bringing peace to creation, and gathering the dispersed of Israel ([[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 11#Isaiah 11 . 1|Isaiah 11:1-16]]).

Jeremiah promises: "Behold, the days are coming, declares the LORD, when I will raise up for David a righteous Branch, and he shall reign as king and deal wisely, and shall execute justice and righteousness in the land. In his days Judah will be saved, and Israel will dwell securely. And this is the name by which he will be called: 'The LORD is our righteousness'" ([[Readable Bible/24 - Jeremiah/Jeremiah 23#Jeremiah 23 . 5|Jeremiah 23:5-6]]).

Ezekiel prophesies the reunification of Israel and Judah under one shepherd: "My servant David shall be king over them, and they shall all have one shepherd. They shall walk in my rules and be careful to obey my statutes" ([[Readable Bible/26 - Ezekiel/Ezekiel 37#Ezekiel 37 . 24|Ezekiel 37:24]]).

These prophecies articulate the full commission that the coming King will fulfill: He will be wise, righteous, just, peaceful, unifying, obedient, and eternal. He will succeed where all previous kings failed. He is Jesus Christ, the Son of David and Son of God, who perfectly executes every aspect of the royal commission.

## Theological Summary: The Commission Fulfilled in Christ

The commission given to Israel's kings defines the Adamic calling in its fullest Old Testament expression. The king is to be:

- **God's adopted son**, enjoying intimate relationship with the Father
- **Shepherd of God's people**, caring for them with sacrificial love
- **Executor of justice and righteousness**, defending the oppressed and judging the wicked
- **Builder and guardian of the temple-sanctuary**, ensuring pure worship and God's presence
- **Student and obeyer of God's word**, meditating on it continually and walking in perfect obedience
- **Military leader**, defeating God's enemies and establishing peace
- **Expander of God's kingdom**, bringing the nations under God's righteous rule
- **Humble servant**, not exalting himself above his brothers but remaining one with the people

No king of Israel or Judah perfectly fulfilled this commission. Even David, the man after God's heart, committed adultery and murder. Even Solomon, the wisest of men, turned to idolatry. The monarchy's history is the story of commission given, commission failed, and the growing hope for the commission fulfilled.

Beale concludes: "The kingly commission, though never perfectly fulfilled in the Old Testament, establishes the pattern for Christ's work. Jesus is the true Son of God, the good Shepherd, the righteous Judge, the true Temple, the perfect Obeyer of God's word, the Conqueror of sin and death, the King whose kingdom extends to all nations, and the humble Servant who washes His disciples' feet. What Israel's kings were commissioned to do but failed to accomplish, Jesus completes perfectly."

Kline adds: "The commission reveals the eschatological shape of God's kingdom. The Davidic covenant promises an eternal throne, eternal kingdom, eternal reign—but history shows that no temporal king can fulfill this. The commission itself is eschatological, pointing beyond history to the eternal Son who will reign from the throne of David forever, perfectly executing justice and righteousness in the new creation."

The commission to Israel's kings is ultimately the commission of the Messiah, Jesus Christ, who alone can say, "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 28#Matthew 28 . 18|Matthew 28:18]]), and who will reign until He has put all enemies under His feet ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 15#1 Corinthians 15 . 25|1 Corinthians 15:25]]), when He will deliver the kingdom to God the Father, that God may be all in all ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 15#1 Corinthians 15 . 28|1 Corinthians 15:28]]).
