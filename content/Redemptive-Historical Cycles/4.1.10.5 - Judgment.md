# 4.1.10.5 - Judgment - Death of Moses and Transition to Joshua

[[Redemptive-Historical Cycles/4.1.10 - Intrusion - Wilderness Wanderings II|← Back to 4.1.10]]

---

## Overview

The judgment phase of the second wilderness period brings devastating closure to the Mosaic era while simultaneously opening the door to the conquest under Joshua. Three major judgments dominate this phase: the plague at Baal of Peor that killed 24,000 ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 25#Numbers 25 . 9|Numbers 25:9]]), the war of vengeance against Midian ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 31#Numbers 31 . 1|Numbers 31:1-18]]), and the death of Moses himself on Mount Nebo ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 34#Deuteronomy 34 . 1|Deuteronomy 34:1-8]]). These judgments are not capricious but covenantal—they execute the sanctions sworn at Sinai for violations of the exclusive worship and moral purity required by the covenant. Yet even in judgment, grace appears: the plague is stopped through Phinehas's intervention, the war against Midian purges the source of seduction, and Moses' death paves the way for Joshua to lead a new generation into the land.

The cumulative effect of these judgments is both sobering and instructive. The first generation died in the wilderness because of unbelief ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 14#Numbers 14 . 29|Numbers 14:29-35]]). Now 24,000 from the second generation die because of idolatry and sexual immorality. Even Moses, the greatest prophet and mediator Israel ever knew ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 34#Deuteronomy 34 . 10|Deuteronomy 34:10]]), dies outside the Promised Land because of his failure at Meribah ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 20#Numbers 20 . 12|Numbers 20:12]]). The message is inescapable: covenant violation brings death, regardless of one's status or previous faithfulness. Yet the survival of a remnant (the second generation minus the 24,000) and the commissioning of Joshua demonstrate that God's purposes will not be thwarted by human failure. Judgment falls, but redemption continues.

## The Plague at Baal of Peor

The immediate judgment for Israel's apostasy at Baal of Peor was a plague that consumed 24,000 people ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 25#Numbers 25 . 9|Numbers 25:9]]). The text does not describe the symptoms or mechanism of the plague, focusing instead on its theological significance: this was divine wrath executing covenant justice. The plague's supernatural character is evident in its sudden onset (concurrent with the sin), its indiscriminate scope (24,000 dead), and its abrupt cessation when Phinehas executed Zimri and Cozbi ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 25#Numbers 25 . 8|Numbers 25:8]]). This was not natural disease following normal epidemiological patterns but direct divine intervention—the intrusion of eschatological judgment into historical time.

The number 24,000 represents roughly 4% of the adult male population (the second census counted 601,730 men twenty years and older, [[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 26#Numbers 26 . 51|Numbers 26:51]]). If women and children are included, the percentage drops further. This means the majority of Israel survived the judgment, though not because they were innocent. The plague's cessation after Phinehas's act indicates that substitutionary execution (of Zimri and Cozbi) satisfied divine justice sufficiently to spare the rest, though 24,000 had already fallen. The judgment was both severe (24,000 dead) and merciful (hundreds of thousands spared who likely participated in or tolerated the apostasy).

Paul references this incident in his warning to the Corinthian church: "We must not indulge in sexual immorality as some of them did, and twenty-three thousand fell in a single day" ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 10#1 Corinthians 10 . 8|1 Corinthians 10:8]]). The apparent discrepancy in numbers (23,000 vs. 24,000) may reflect that 23,000 died "in a single day" while the total death toll reached 24,000, or Paul may be rounding for rhetorical effect. Regardless, Paul's point is clear: God's judgment on sexual immorality and idolatry is not merely Old Testament history but ongoing divine principle. The Corinthians, facing similar temptations in a pagan city, must learn from Israel's example lest they experience similar judgment.

## War Against Midian

The LORD commands Moses to execute further judgment: "Avenge the people of Israel on the Midianites. Afterward you shall be gathered to your people" ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 31#Numbers 31 . 2|Numbers 31:2]]). This command connects Moses' impending death to the completion of judgment against those who seduced Israel into apostasy. The Midianites, though related to Israel through Abraham's son Midian ([[Readable Bible/01 - Genesis/Genesis 25#Genesis 25 . 2|Genesis 25:2]]), had acted as enemies by implementing Balaam's strategy of seduction ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 31#Numbers 31 . 16|Numbers 31:16]]). The command for vengeance is not personal vendetta but covenant justice—those who attack God's people through spiritual seduction must face judgment.

Israel musters 12,000 warriors (1,000 from each tribe) under the leadership of Phinehas, who carries "the vessels of the sanctuary and the trumpets for the alarm" ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 31#Numbers 31 . 6|Numbers 31:6]]). The campaign is framed as holy war—the trumpets signaled that this was Yahweh's battle, not merely human conquest. The result is total victory: "They killed every male... They also killed Balaam the son of Beor with the sword" ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 31#Numbers 31 . 7|Numbers 31:7-8]]). The inclusion of Balaam among the slain is significant—the prophet who could not curse Israel but counseled her seduction dies under the very judgment he sought to bring upon Israel. His death exemplifies the principle he himself articulated: "Blessed are those who bless you, and cursed are those who curse you" ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 24#Numbers 24 . 9|Numbers 24:9]]).

When the army returns with captive women and children, Moses expresses anger: "Have you let all the women live? Behold, these, on Balaam's advice, caused the people of Israel to act treacherously against the LORD in the incident of Peor, and so the plague came among the congregation of the LORD. Now therefore, kill every male among the little ones, and kill every woman who has known man by lying with him. But all the young girls who have not known man by lying with him keep alive for yourselves" ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 31#Numbers 31 . 15|Numbers 31:15-18]]). This command is deeply troubling to modern readers, appearing to sanction genocide and sexual slavery. Several factors provide theological context without minimizing the severity:

First, the judgment targets those specifically guilty of or complicit in the seduction. The adult males participated in the military/political leadership that enabled the seduction. The women "who have known man" (sexually mature women) were the instruments of seduction at Baal of Peor. Sparing them would reintroduce the very corrupting influence that brought plague upon Israel. The young girls, not yet complicit, could be integrated into Israel through adoption and marriage (similar to the provision for female captives in [[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 21#Deuteronomy 21 . 10|Deuteronomy 21:10-14]]).

Second, this judgment functions as holy war against demonic worship. Midianite culture, like broader Canaanite culture, practiced child sacrifice, ritual prostitution, and other abominations ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 12#Deuteronomy 12 . 31|Deuteronomy 12:31]]). The command to destroy them mirrors the later command regarding Canaanite nations: "You shall not leave alive anything that breathes, but you shall devote them to complete destruction... that they may not teach you to do according to all their abominable practices that they have done for their gods" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 20#Deuteronomy 20 . 16|Deuteronomy 20:16-18]]). The judgment is not ethnic cleansing but spiritual quarantine—removing the contagion of idolatry that nearly destroyed Israel.

Third, this judgment previews the final judgment where all unrepentant rebels against God will face complete destruction. The limited scope (Midianite region, not the entire world) and provision for mercy (sparing young girls) distinguish this temporal judgment from eschatological judgment. Yet the principle remains: those who oppose God's people and promote covenant violation will ultimately be destroyed. The severity of the judgment underscores the seriousness of idolatry and the holiness required for covenant relationship with God.

## Moses Views the Land from Nebo

The climactic judgment of this period is Moses' death. After forty years of leading Israel through wilderness, Moses is permitted to see but not enter the Promised Land. The LORD commands: "Go up into this mountain of Abarim, Mount Nebo, which is in the land of Moab, opposite Jericho, and view the land of Canaan, which I am giving to the people of Israel for a possession. And die on the mountain which you go up, and be gathered to your people, as Aaron your brother died in Mount Hor and was gathered to his people, because you broke faith with me in the midst of the people of Israel at the waters of Meribah-kadesh, in the wilderness of Zin, and because you did not treat me as holy in the midst of the people of Israel" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 32#Deuteronomy 32 . 49|Deuteronomy 32:49-51]]).

Moses' exclusion from Canaan results from his failure at Meribah where he struck the rock twice in anger rather than speaking to it as commanded ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 20#Numbers 20 . 8|Numbers 20:8-12]]). The judgment seems disproportionate to modern sensibilities—after forty years of faithful (though imperfect) service, one moment of anger disqualifies Moses from the reward he spent his entire adult life pursuing. But the issue was not mere procedural error; Moses "did not treat [God] as holy in the midst of the people of Israel." As mediator between God and the nation, Moses was to display God's character—patient, gracious, sufficient to provide without violence or human striving. By striking the rock in anger and claiming credit ("shall we bring water for you?"), Moses obscured God's glory at a crucial moment.

The parallel with Aaron's death is deliberate—both brothers die before entering Canaan, both because of covenant violation at Meribah, both seeing their office transferred to the next generation (Aaron's priesthood to Eleazar, Moses' leadership to Joshua). The pattern establishes a sobering principle: proximity to God and privileged service do not exempt one from judgment for covenant violation. Indeed, "to whom much is given, much will be required" ([[Readable Bible/42 - Luke/Luke 12#Luke 12 . 48|Luke 12:48]]). Moses' greater responsibility as mediator meant his failure carried greater consequence.

Yet even this judgment contains grace. Moses is not struck dead in shame but permitted a final series of profound acts: delivering three sermons that constitute Deuteronomy, writing a song that would serve as perpetual witness ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 31#Deuteronomy 31 . 19|Deuteronomy 31:19-22]]), blessing the twelve tribes ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 33#Deuteronomy 33 . 1|Deuteronomy 33:1-29]]), and viewing the Promised Land from Mount Nebo. The LORD shows Moses "all the land, Gilead as far as Dan, all Naphtali, the land of Ephraim and Manasseh, all the land of Judah as far as the western sea, the Negeb, and the Plain, that is, the Valley of Jericho the city of palm trees, as far as Zoar" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 34#Deuteronomy 34 . 1|Deuteronomy 34:1-3]]). Moses sees with supernatural clarity the entire extent of the land promised to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. He dies with faith, knowing God will fulfill His covenant promise even though Moses himself will not participate in the fulfillment.

## Moses' Death and Burial

"So Moses the servant of the LORD died there in the land of Moab, according to the word of the LORD, and he buried him in the valley in the land of Moab opposite Beth-peor; but no one knows the place of his burial to this day" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 34#Deuteronomy 34 . 5|Deuteronomy 34:5-6]]). The title "servant of the LORD" is the highest honor—Moses served faithfully despite his failure. The phrase "according to the word of the LORD" suggests Moses died at God's appointed time and manner, not from natural disease or accident. The statement "he buried him" uses third-person singular, which could refer to the LORD Himself burying Moses (as Jewish tradition interpreted it) or to an unspecified person acting under divine direction.

The secrecy of Moses' burial place prevented Israel from making it a shrine or pilgrimage site, which would have risked idolatry. Later, Jude references a dispute between Michael the archangel and the devil over Moses' body ([[Readable Bible/65 - Jude/Jude 1#Jude 1 . 9|Jude 1:9]]), suggesting that Satan sought to use Moses' body for some malign purpose (perhaps tempting Israel to worship Moses or creating division over his burial). The obscurity of the burial site protected Israel from this temptation and ensured that glory would go to God, not to God's servant.

The narrative concludes with Moses' epitaph: "Moses was 120 years old when he died. His eye was undimmed, and his vigor unabated" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 34#Deuteronomy 34 . 7|Deuteronomy 34:7]]). Despite his age, Moses retained full physical and mental capacity—his death was not the result of physical decline but divine decree. The people mourned for thirty days ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 34#Deuteronomy 34 . 8|Deuteronomy 34:8]]), the same period as for Aaron ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 20#Numbers 20 . 29|Numbers 20:29]]), signifying Israel's recognition of their loss. Yet the mourning period ends, and the narrative shifts immediately to Joshua: "And Joshua the son of Nun was full of the spirit of wisdom, for Moses had laid his hands on him. So the people of Israel obeyed him and did as the LORD had commanded Moses" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 34#Deuteronomy 34 . 9|Deuteronomy 34:9]]).

## Transition to Joshua

The judgment of Moses' death is simultaneously the grace of Joshua's elevation. Where Moses failed at the rock, Joshua will succeed in bringing Israel into rest. The commissioning described earlier ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 27#Numbers 27 . 18|Numbers 27:18-23]]; [[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 31#Deuteronomy 31 . 7|Deuteronomy 31:7-8]]) now takes effect. Joshua, described as "full of the spirit of wisdom" because "Moses had laid his hands on him" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 34#Deuteronomy 34 . 9|Deuteronomy 34:9]]), represents the new generation's hope. Unlike the first generation that refused to enter the land, and unlike Moses who was barred from entry, Joshua will lead God's people into their inheritance.

The LORD's direct commission to Joshua after Moses' death is foundational: "Moses my servant is dead. Now therefore arise, go over this Jordan, you and all this people, into the land that I am giving to them, to the people of Israel. Every place that the sole of your foot will tread upon I have given to you, just as I promised to Moses" ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 1#Joshua 1 . 2|Joshua 1:2-3]]). The promise is comprehensive: territorial boundaries from wilderness to Lebanon, from the Euphrates to the Mediterranean ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 1#Joshua 1 . 4|Joshua 1:4]]); divine presence ("I will be with you. I will not leave you or forsake you," [[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 1#Joshua 1 . 5|Joshua 1:5]]); and guaranteed success ("No man shall be able to stand before you all the days of your life," [[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 1#Joshua 1 . 5|Joshua 1:5]]).

But the promise comes with responsibility: "Only be strong and very courageous, being careful to do according to all the law that Moses my servant commanded you. Do not turn from it to the right hand or to the left, that you may have good success wherever you go. This Book of the Law shall not depart from your mouth, but you shall meditate on it day and night, so that you may be careful to do according to all that is written in it. For then you will make your way prosperous, and then you will have good success" ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 1#Joshua 1 . 7|Joshua 1:7-8]]). Success in the conquest depends not on military strategy or numerical superiority but on covenant faithfulness—meditating on and obeying the Law Moses delivered in Deuteronomy. This establishes the theological framework for the entire book of Joshua: Israel's victories result from obedience, and their defeats result from covenant violation (as the Achan incident will demonstrate, [[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 7#Joshua 7 . 1|Joshua 7:1-26]]).

## The Uniqueness of Moses

Deuteronomy concludes with an unparalleled tribute: "And there has not arisen a prophet since in Israel like Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face, none like him for all the signs and the wonders that the LORD sent him to do in the land of Egypt, to Pharaoh and to all his servants and to all his land, and for all the mighty power and all the great deeds of terror that Moses did in the sight of all Israel" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 34#Deuteronomy 34 . 10|Deuteronomy 34:10-12]]). Three elements distinguish Moses: intimate relationship with God ("face to face"), miraculous power (signs in Egypt), and public demonstration of divine authority (mighty deeds before all Israel). No subsequent prophet—not Joshua, Samuel, Elijah, or Isaiah—matched Moses in all three dimensions.

Yet this very uniqueness points forward to the need for something greater. Moses knew God "face to face," but even he could not see God's face and live ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 33#Exodus 33 . 20|Exodus 33:20]]); he saw God's back ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 33#Exodus 33 . 23|Exodus 33:23]]) and spoke with Him directly ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 12#Numbers 12 . 8|Numbers 12:8]]), but the relationship was mediated. Moses performed signs and wonders but could not transform hearts. Moses led Israel to the border of the Promised Land but could not bring them into ultimate rest. The very greatness of Moses and the tragedy of his death outside Canaan both point to the need for a greater Prophet, a greater Mediator, a greater Leader who could accomplish what Moses could not.

## Theological Analysis: Kline's Intrusion and Beale's Adamic Failure

Meredith Kline's theology of intrusion illuminates the judgments in this section as eschatological realities breaking into historical time. The plague at Baal of Peor was not natural disease but supernatural execution—a preview of final judgment against idolatry and sexual immorality. The war against Midian was holy war, implementing the ban (*herem*) that would characterize the conquest of Canaan and ultimately previews the final destruction of all God's enemies. Moses' death, though peaceful, represents the judgment that "the wages of sin is death" ([[Readable Bible/45 - Romans/Romans 6#Romans 6 . 23|Romans 6:23]])—even the greatest saint dies under the curse of the fall.

Kline's covenant structure helps explain why Moses was excluded from Canaan. The covenant at Sinai established clear sanctions: obedience brings blessing, disobedience brings curse ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 28#Deuteronomy 28 . 1|Deuteronomy 28]]). Moses, as covenant mediator, was held to the highest standard. His failure to "uphold [God] as holy" ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 20#Numbers 20 . 12|Numbers 20:12]]) violated the fundamental requirement of the mediatorial role—representing God's character accurately. The judgment was not arbitrary but covenantal—Moses received the sanction appropriate to his covenant violation. Yet even this judgment serves redemptive purposes, demonstrating that the Mosaic covenant, though glorious, was inadequate to bring people into ultimate rest.

G.K. Beale's Adamic typology provides profound insight into Moses' exclusion from Canaan. Adam was placed in the garden-temple with the commission to expand its boundaries by faithful obedience. Adam failed, bringing death upon himself and his descendants. Moses, functioning as new Adam leading a new humanity (Israel) toward a new Eden (Canaan), also failed at the crucial moment (striking the rock, the water-source representing life). Like Adam, Moses is excluded from the paradise he was meant to enter and extend. The pattern establishes that no fallen human—not even Moses—can fulfill the Adamic commission. A true and better Adam is needed.

Beale's temple theology illuminates why the sin at Baal of Peor warranted such severe judgment. The camp of Israel, centered on the tabernacle, represented Eden restored. Sexual immorality and idolatry defiled sacred space, violating the holiness required for God's presence to dwell among His people. The plague and the war against Midian function as temple-cleansing actions, purging defilement so that God's presence can remain. The principle continues in the New Testament: "Do you not know that you are God's temple and that God's Spirit dwells in you? If anyone destroys God's temple, God will destroy him. For God's temple is holy, and you are that temple" ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 3#1 Corinthians 3 . 16|1 Corinthians 3:16-17]]).

## Christological Fulfillment

Moses' death outside the Promised Land and Joshua's leadership into Canaan form a typological pattern pointing to Christ. Moses represents the Law—glorious, authoritative, necessary, but ultimately unable to bring people into rest because of human sinfulness. "For if a law had been given that could give life, then righteousness would indeed be by the law" ([[Readable Bible/48 - Galatians/Galatians 3#Galatians 3 . 21|Galatians 3:21]]). The Law can diagnose sin, command obedience, and pronounce judgment, but it cannot transform the heart or grant the righteousness it requires. Moses, embodying the Law, brings Israel to the threshold but cannot lead them across.

Joshua (Hebrew *Yehoshua*, identical to Jesus in Greek *Iesous*) represents grace and fulfillment. Only Joshua/Jesus can bring God's people into rest. The book of Hebrews makes this explicit: "For if Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken of another day later on. So then, there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God, for whoever has entered God's rest has also rested from his works as God did from his" ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 4#Hebrews 4 . 8|Hebrews 4:8-10]]). Joshua's conquest of Canaan was real and significant, but it was typological—pointing to the greater conquest of sin, death, and Satan accomplished by Jesus, and the greater rest of new creation resurrection life.

The judgment on Moses—viewing but not entering the land—finds its ultimate reversal in Christ who not only enters the true Promised Land (the new creation) but brings His people with Him. Where Moses died and was buried in an unknown grave, Jesus died and rose from the dead, His tomb empty and His location known—seated at the right hand of God ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 1#Hebrews 1 . 3|Hebrews 1:3]]). The "servant of the LORD" who died outside the land prefigures the ultimate Servant who "bore the sin of many" and "makes many to be accounted righteous" ([[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 53#Isaiah 53 . 11|Isaiah 53:11-12]]).

The plague at Baal of Peor, stopped through Phinehas's zealous execution of judgment, prefigures Christ's bearing of the plague of divine wrath at the cross. Where 24,000 died at Peor despite Phinehas's intervention, Christ's death prevents any of His elect from perishing eternally: "For God has not destined us for wrath, but to obtain salvation through our Lord Jesus Christ, who died for us" ([[Readable Bible/52 - 1 Thessalonians/1 Thessalonians 5#1 Thessalonians 5 . 9|1 Thessalonians 5:9-10]]). The wrath poured out on the 24,000 typifies the wrath that Jesus bore for the countless multitude who trust in Him.

The war against Midian, executing vengeance on those who caused Israel to sin, anticipates the final judgment when Christ returns to judge all who oppose His people and promote rebellion against God. "The Lord Jesus [will be] revealed from heaven with his mighty angels in flaming fire, inflicting vengeance on those who do not know God and on those who do not obey the gospel of our Lord Jesus" ([[Readable Bible/53 - 2 Thessalonians/2 Thessalonians 1#2 Thessalonians 1 . 7|2 Thessalonians 1:7-8]]). The limited scope of the Midianite judgment gives way to universal judgment where all unrepentant rebels face eternal destruction.

Finally, Moses' unique status—the greatest prophet until Christ—establishes the pattern that Christ fulfills and transcends. Moses spoke with God face to face; Jesus is God face to face ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 1#John 1 . 1|John 1:1, 14]]). Moses performed signs and wonders in Egypt; Jesus performed greater signs, ultimately conquering death itself ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 20#John 20 . 30|John 20:30-31]]). Moses faithfully served in God's house; Jesus is the Son over God's house ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 3#Hebrews 3 . 5|Hebrews 3:5-6]]). The uniqueness and limitations of Moses both honor his role and point beyond him to the One of whom Moses himself wrote ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 5#John 5 . 46|John 5:46]]).
