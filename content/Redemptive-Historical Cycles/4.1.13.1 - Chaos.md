# 4.1.13.1 - Chaos (Kings - United and Divided Monarchy)

## Overview: The Failure of Human Kingship

The monarchy period reveals the deepest chaos yet in Israel's history—not the external chaos of enemies or the internal chaos of anarchic judges, but the chaos that emerges when God's appointed representatives themselves become agents of corruption. The request for a king "like all the nations" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 8#1 Samuel 8 . 5|1 Samuel 8:5]]) signals Israel's fundamental rejection of Yahweh's unique kingship, initiating a tragic trajectory from Saul's promising beginning to the smoking ruins of Jerusalem in 586 BC.

This chaos unfolds in escalating stages: the corruption of judgeship under Eli's sons and Samuel's sons, the failure of Saul as first king, the partial success but ultimate failures of David, the glory and apostasy of Solomon, the permanent division of the kingdom, the unrelenting wickedness of the Northern Kingdom, the mixed record of the Southern Kingdom, and finally the catastrophic exile of both kingdoms. Each stage demonstrates that human kings, even those from David's chosen line, cannot reverse the Adamic curse—they embody and magnify it.

## The Corruption of Judgeship: Setting the Stage for Kingship

### Eli's Failed House and the Loss of the Ark

The chaos begins not with the monarchy itself but with the collapse of faithful leadership in the preceding generation. Eli, the aged priest-judge, cannot control his sons Hophni and Phinehas, who are described with devastating simplicity: "Now the sons of Eli were worthless men. They did not know the LORD" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 2#1 Samuel 2 . 12|1 Samuel 2:12]]). Their sins are not private failures but public desecrations of the sacrificial system itself—they treat "the offering of the LORD with contempt" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 2#1 Samuel 2 . 17|1 Samuel 2:17]]), commit sexual immorality "at the entrance of the tent of meeting" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 2#1 Samuel 2 . 22|1 Samuel 2:22]]), and corrupt the very means of approaching God.

Beale observes that Eli's sons function as anti-priests, defiling the tabernacle that should function as Israel's Eden. Instead of guarding the holy space and mediating God's presence to the people, they pollute it with sin and turn worship into an occasion for theft and lust. The tabernacle, meant to be the place where heaven touches earth, becomes a site of scandal.

The divine judgment comes through a "man of God" who announces the end of Eli's house: "Behold, the days are coming when I will cut off your strength and the strength of your father's house, so that there will not be an old man in your house" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 2#1 Samuel 2 . 31|1 Samuel 2:31]]). The sign of this judgment's certainty? "Both of them, Hophni and Phinehas, shall die on the same day" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 2#1 Samuel 2 . 34|1 Samuel 2:34]]).

That day arrives when Israel, desperate in battle against the Philistines, brings the ark of the covenant from Shiloh to the battlefield, treating it like a magical talisman rather than the sign of God's holy presence ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 4#1 Samuel 4 . 3|1 Samuel 4:3-4]]). The result is catastrophic: Israel is routed, 30,000 foot soldiers die, the ark is captured, and Hophni and Phinehas are killed ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 4#1 Samuel 4 . 10|1 Samuel 4:10-11]]). When Eli hears the news, he falls backward from his seat, breaks his neck, and dies ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 4#1 Samuel 4 . 18|1 Samuel 4:18]]). His daughter-in-law, dying in childbirth, names her son Ichabod—"The glory has departed from Israel" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 4#1 Samuel 4 . 21|1 Samuel 4:21]]).

Kline notes that the capture of the ark represents the withdrawal of the upper register's presence from Israel. The ark, the supreme symbol of Yahweh's throne presence, is now in enemy hands. Yet even in judgment, God's sovereignty prevails—the Philistines cannot keep the ark, as Dagon falls before it and plagues strike their cities ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 5#1 Samuel 5 . 1|1 Samuel 5:1-12]]). The ark returns to Israel, but the damage to the priesthood and the people's confidence in covenant leadership is done.

### Samuel's Sons and the Demand for a King

The chaos pattern repeats in the next generation. Samuel, the faithful prophet-judge who restored Israel spiritually and militarily, makes the same mistake as Eli—he appoints his sons as judges, but "his sons did not walk in his ways but turned aside after gain. They took bribes and perverted justice" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 8#1 Samuel 8 . 3|1 Samuel 8:3]]). The text is clear: "Joel and Abijah" served as judges in Beersheba, but they were corrupt ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 8#1 Samuel 8 . 2|1 Samuel 8:2]]).

This failure of succession becomes the occasion for Israel's fateful request: "Now appoint for us a king to judge us like all the nations" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 8#1 Samuel 8 . 5|1 Samuel 8:5]]). The request grieves Samuel, but Yahweh's response reveals the deeper issue: "They have not rejected you, but they have rejected me from being king over them" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 8#1 Samuel 8 . 7|1 Samuel 8:7]]). The desire for a king "like all the nations" represents a fundamental covenant betrayal—Israel wants to be like Egypt, Canaan, Philistia, nations under human rule and pagan gods, rather than the unique theocratic people ruled directly by Yahweh through His chosen mediators.

Samuel delivers Yahweh's warning about what "the ways of the king" will be: conscription of sons and daughters, taxation of fields and flocks, seizure of property, enslavement of the people ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 8#1 Samuel 8 . 11|1 Samuel 8:11-17]]). The climax is chilling: "And in that day you will cry out because of your king, whom you have chosen for yourselves, but the LORD will not answer you in that day" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 8#1 Samuel 8 . 18|1 Samuel 8:18]]).

But the people refuse to listen: "No! But there shall be a king over us, that we also may be like all the nations, and that our king may judge us and go out before us and fight our battles" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 8#1 Samuel 8 . 19|1 Samuel 8:19-20]]). The repetition of "like all the nations" seals their rejection. They want human kingship, military glory, visible power—the very things that characterize fallen humanity's kingdoms. They will get exactly what they ask for, with all its tragic consequences.

Beale sees here a recapitulation of Adam's fall—Israel desires to be like the nations (as Adam desired to be like God, knowing good and evil independently), rejecting their unique calling and the special presence of their divine King. Kline adds that this rejection of theocratic rule represents a premature grasping at eschatological kingship without the spiritual maturity or covenant faithfulness required. Israel wants the form of kingdom without the substance of righteousness.

## Saul: The Tragic First King

### The Promising Beginning

Saul's selection as king reveals the ambiguity of the monarchy from the start. He is chosen by divine lot, confirmed by prophetic anointing, and appears to possess impressive physical qualities: "There was not a man among the people of Israel more handsome than he. From his shoulders upward he was taller than any of the people" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 9#1 Samuel 9 . 2|1 Samuel 9:2]]). He is from the tribe of Benjamin, the smallest tribe ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 9#1 Samuel 9 . 21|1 Samuel 9:21]]), suggesting that God can work through the unlikely and humble.

Samuel anoints him privately ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 10#1 Samuel 10 . 1|1 Samuel 10:1]]), the Spirit of God rushes upon him ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 10#1 Samuel 10 . 6|1 Samuel 10:6]]), and he prophesies among the prophets—leading to the proverbial question, "Is Saul also among the prophets?" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 10#1 Samuel 10 . 11|1 Samuel 10:11]]). When publicly selected by lot at Mizpah, Saul hides among the baggage, showing either humility or insecurity ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 10#1 Samuel 10 . 22|1 Samuel 10:22]]).

His first major act as king is a military success—he defeats the Ammonites who threatened Jabesh-gilead, cutting a yoke of oxen into pieces and sending them throughout Israel to summon troops ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 11#1 Samuel 11 . 7|1 Samuel 11:7]]). The victory is decisive, and the kingdom is "renewed" at Gilgal ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 11#1 Samuel 11 . 14|1 Samuel 11:14]]).

But ominous signs appear even in this early phase. Samuel's farewell address emphasizes conditional covenant blessings: "If you will fear the LORD and serve him and obey his voice and not rebel against the commandment of the LORD, and if both you and the king who reigns over you will follow the LORD your God, it will be well. But if you will not obey the voice of the LORD, but rebel against the commandment of the LORD, then the hand of the LORD will be against you and your king" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 12#1 Samuel 12 . 14|1 Samuel 12:14-15]]).

### The Fatal Disobedience at Gilgal

The first major crisis comes quickly. Facing a Philistine threat at Gilgal, Saul waits seven days for Samuel to arrive and offer sacrifice, as instructed ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 10#1 Samuel 10 . 8|1 Samuel 10:8]]). But as the people begin to scatter in fear, Saul takes matters into his own hands: "So Saul said, 'Bring the burnt offering here to me, and the peace offerings.' And he offered the burnt offering" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 13#1 Samuel 13 . 9|1 Samuel 13:9]]).

Samuel arrives just as Saul finishes, and the confrontation is immediate and devastating: "You have done foolishly. You have not kept the command of the LORD your God, with which he commanded you. For then the LORD would have established your kingdom over Israel forever. But now your kingdom shall not continue" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 13#1 Samuel 13 . 13|1 Samuel 13:13-14]]). The next verse delivers the death sentence to Saul's dynastic hopes: "The LORD has sought out a man after his own heart, and the LORD has commanded him to be prince over his people, because you have not kept what the LORD commanded you" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 13#1 Samuel 13 . 14|1 Samuel 13:14]]).

The offense seems minor—Saul simply performed a religious duty that was delayed. But the issue is fundamental: covenant authority. Saul, the king, has usurped the priestly function that belonged to Samuel, the prophet-priest. He has prioritized pragmatic concerns (keeping the troops from scattering) over explicit divine command (wait for Samuel). He has acted independently rather than in dependent obedience. In this, he reveals that he will be a king "like the nations"—self-directed, pragmatic, autonomous—rather than a true theocratic king who rules under God's word.

Kline notes that Saul's failure here is a failure to recognize the distinction between royal and priestly offices in the theocracy. The king does not have absolute authority; he is himself under the prophetic word. By offering sacrifice, Saul attempts to merge offices that God has separated, asserting autonomy that no covenant king possesses.

### The Rejection at Amalek

The second and final rejection comes in 1 Samuel 15, in the campaign against the Amalekites. God's command through Samuel is explicit and comprehensive: "Now go and strike Amalek and devote to destruction all that they have. Do not spare them, but kill both man and woman, child and infant, ox and sheep, camel and donkey" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 3|1 Samuel 15:3]]). This is ḥerem warfare, the total consecration to God of a nation under judgment for its opposition to God's people (Exodus 17:8-16; Deuteronomy 25:17-19).

Saul defeats Amalek militarily, but he and the people "spared Agag and the best of the sheep and of the oxen and of the fattened calves and the lambs, and all that was good, and would not utterly destroy them. All that was despised and worthless they devoted to destruction" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 9|1 Samuel 15:9]]). Saul keeps Agag, the Amalekite king, alive—perhaps as a trophy, perhaps as a political prisoner—and permits the people to keep the best livestock, ostensibly "to sacrifice to the LORD your God" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 15|1 Samuel 15:15]]).

When Samuel confronts him, Saul's response reveals his self-deception: "I have performed the commandment of the LORD" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 13|1 Samuel 15:13]]). But Samuel's ironic question cuts through the facade: "What then is this bleating of the sheep in my ears and the lowing of the oxen that I hear?" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 14|1 Samuel 15:14]]).

Saul attempts to shift blame to "the people" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 15|1 Samuel 15:15, 21]]), echoing Adam's blame-shifting to Eve in Genesis 3. But Samuel will not allow it. He delivers one of Scripture's most memorable indictments: "Has the LORD as great delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices, as in obeying the voice of the LORD? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to listen than the fat of rams. For rebellion is as the sin of divination, and presumption is as iniquity and idolatry. Because you have rejected the word of the LORD, he has also rejected you from being king" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 22|1 Samuel 15:22-23]]).

The equation is stunning: Saul's disobedience is equivalent to divination, his presumption equivalent to idolatry. Why? Because both involve autonomous self-direction, consulting one's own will or other spiritual powers rather than submitting to Yahweh's revealed word. Saul has made himself the final authority, determining which commands to obey and which to modify. He is therefore not fit to represent the divine King.

Saul finally confesses, "I have sinned, for I have transgressed the commandment of the LORD and your words, because I feared the people and obeyed their voice" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 24|1 Samuel 15:24]]), but the confession is too late and too shallow—he immediately asks Samuel to honor him before the elders ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 30|1 Samuel 15:30]]), revealing that his concern is for public reputation, not true repentance.

Samuel executes Agag himself ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 33|1 Samuel 15:33]]), and the chapter closes with devastating finality: "And the LORD regretted that he had made Saul king over Israel" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 35|1 Samuel 15:35]]).

### Descent into Madness and Suicide

The rest of Saul's reign is a tragic descent into jealousy, paranoia, violence, and spiritual darkness. When the Spirit of the Lord departs from Saul, "a harmful spirit from the LORD tormented him" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 16#1 Samuel 16 . 14|1 Samuel 16:14]]). David, the shepherd boy anointed as Saul's replacement, becomes Saul's court musician, but Saul's jealousy erupts when the women sing, "Saul has struck down his thousands, and David his ten thousands" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 18#1 Samuel 18 . 7|1 Samuel 18:7]]). Saul twice hurls his spear at David ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 18#1 Samuel 18 . 11|1 Samuel 18:11]]; [[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 19#1 Samuel 19 . 10|19:10]]), pursues him relentlessly, and murders the priests of Nob for aiding David ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 22#1 Samuel 22 . 18|1 Samuel 22:18-19]]).

At his lowest point, Saul consults a medium at Endor, violating his own earlier edict against such practices ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 28#1 Samuel 28 . 7|1 Samuel 28:7-25]]). Samuel's spirit announces Saul's doom: "Tomorrow you and your sons shall be with me" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 28#1 Samuel 28 . 19|1 Samuel 28:19]]).

The prophecy comes to pass on Mount Gilboa. The Philistines rout Israel, kill Saul's sons including Jonathan, and wound Saul with arrows. Rather than be captured and abused, Saul falls on his own sword ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 31#1 Samuel 31 . 4|1 Samuel 31:4]]). The Philistines find his body, cut off his head, strip his armor, and fasten his body to the wall of Beth-shan ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 31#1 Samuel 31 . 9|1 Samuel 31:9-10]]). The first king of Israel dies a suicide, his dynasty extinct, his nation defeated, his body desecrated.

Beale observes that Saul's trajectory perfectly mirrors Adam's fall: initial blessing, direct command from God, partial obedience mixed with self-will, blame-shifting, progressive alienation from God, and finally death. Saul is the corporate Adam of Israel's monarchy, revealing that simply having a king "like the nations" will not reverse the curse—it will only embody it at the highest level.

## David: The Man After God's Heart—And His Chaos

### Civil War and Violence

Even David, "a man after [God's] own heart" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 13#1 Samuel 13 . 14|1 Samuel 13:14]]), is surrounded by and sometimes generates chaos. After Saul's death, David is anointed king over Judah in Hebron ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 2#2 Samuel 2 . 4|2 Samuel 2:4]]), but the northern tribes rally around Saul's son Ish-bosheth ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 2#2 Samuel 2 . 8|2 Samuel 2:8-9]]). The result is civil war: "There was a long war between the house of Saul and the house of David. And David grew stronger and stronger, while the house of Saul became weaker and weaker" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 3#2 Samuel 3 . 1|2 Samuel 3:1]]).

The conflict is marked by treachery and murder. Abner, Saul's general, defects to David but is then murdered by Joab in revenge for Abner's earlier killing of Joab's brother Asahel ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 3#2 Samuel 3 . 27|2 Samuel 3:27]]). Ish-bosheth is assassinated by his own commanders, who bring his head to David expecting a reward ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 4#2 Samuel 4 . 7|2 Samuel 4:7-8]]). David executes the assassins for killing "a righteous man in his own house on his bed" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 4#2 Samuel 4 . 11|2 Samuel 4:11]]), but the damage is done—the path to unified kingship is paved with blood.

### David's Adultery and Murder

David's greatest personal chaos comes at the height of his power. "In the spring of the year, the time when kings go out to battle," David remains in Jerusalem instead of leading his army ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 1|2 Samuel 11:1]]). From his rooftop, he sees Bathsheba bathing, sends for her, commits adultery with her, and she becomes pregnant ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 2|2 Samuel 11:2-5]]).

David's response to this sin generates a cascade of further evil. He summons Uriah, Bathsheba's husband, from the battlefield, hoping Uriah will sleep with his wife and provide cover for the pregnancy ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 6|2 Samuel 11:6-8]]). But Uriah, a Hittite and model of military discipline, refuses: "The ark and Israel and Judah dwell in booths, and my lord Joab and the servants of my lord are camping in the open field. Shall I then go to my house, to eat and to drink and to lie with my wife? As you live, and as your soul lives, I will not do this thing" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 11|2 Samuel 11:11]]). Uriah's integrity contrasts sharply with David's corruption.

Desperate, David sends Uriah back to battle carrying his own death warrant: "Set Uriah in the forefront of the hardest fighting, and then draw back from him, that he may be struck down, and die" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 15|2 Samuel 11:15]]). Joab arranges the murder ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 17|2 Samuel 11:17]]), Uriah dies, and David takes Bathsheba as his wife ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 27|2 Samuel 11:27]]).

The chapter concludes with a single, devastating sentence: "But the thing that David had done displeased the LORD" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 27|2 Samuel 11:27]]).

Nathan the prophet confronts David with a parable about a rich man who steals a poor man's only lamb ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 1|2 Samuel 12:1-4]]). David's anger burns against the rich man—"As the LORD lives, the man who has done this deserves to die" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 5|2 Samuel 12:5-6]])—and Nathan springs the trap: "You are the man!" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 7|2 Samuel 12:7]]).

Nathan recounts God's blessings to David and pronounces judgment: "Now therefore the sword shall never depart from your house, because you have despised me and have taken the wife of Uriah the Hittite to be your wife" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 10|2 Samuel 12:10]]). The punishment fits the crime: "Behold, I will raise up evil against you out of your own house. And I will take your wives before your eyes and give them to your neighbor, and he shall lie with your wives in the sight of this sun. For you did it secretly, but I will do this thing before all Israel and before the sun" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 11|2 Samuel 12:11-12]]).

David confesses—"I have sinned against the LORD" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 13|2 Samuel 12:13]])—and Nathan announces that David will not die, but "the child who is born to you shall die" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 14|2 Samuel 12:14]]). The child becomes ill, David fasts and prays, but the child dies on the seventh day ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 18|2 Samuel 12:18]]).

Beale notes that David, the Adamic king, has failed in the garden-temple of his kingdom. He has taken what was not his (like Adam taking the forbidden fruit), he has committed murder (like Cain), and he has violated covenant law. The "sword" that will never depart from his house represents the entrance of death into the royal line, the curse of Genesis 3 manifesting in the Davidic dynasty.

### The Sword in David's House: Amnon, Tamar, Absalom

Nathan's prophecy unfolds with terrible precision. David's son Amnon becomes infatuated with his half-sister Tamar, rapes her, and then hates her ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 13#2 Samuel 13 . 14|2 Samuel 13:14-15]]). David is angry but does nothing ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 13#2 Samuel 13 . 21|2 Samuel 13:21]]). Two years later, Absalom, Tamar's full brother, murders Amnon in revenge ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 13#2 Samuel 13 . 28|2 Samuel 13:28-29]]).

Absalom flees to Geshur for three years ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 13#2 Samuel 13 . 38|2 Samuel 13:38]]), returns through Joab's manipulation ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 14#2 Samuel 14 . 1|2 Samuel 14:1-24]]), but remains alienated from David for two more years ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 14#2 Samuel 14 . 28|2 Samuel 14:28]]). Finally reconciled superficially, Absalom begins to undermine his father's authority, "steal the hearts of the men of Israel" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 15#2 Samuel 15 . 6|2 Samuel 15:6]]), and eventually launches an open rebellion.

David must flee Jerusalem, barefoot and weeping ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 15#2 Samuel 15 . 30|2 Samuel 15:30]]), while Absalom enters the city and publicly sleeps with David's concubines "in the sight of all Israel" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 16#2 Samuel 16 . 22|2 Samuel 16:22]]), fulfilling Nathan's prophecy exactly.

The rebellion ends when Absalom's forces are defeated and Absalom himself is killed by Joab, despite David's orders to "deal gently for my sake with the young man Absalom" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 18#2 Samuel 18 . 5|2 Samuel 18:5]]). Absalom's head is caught in an oak tree, and Joab drives three javelins through his heart ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 18#2 Samuel 18 . 14|2 Samuel 18:14]]).

David's grief is profound and public: "O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! Would I had died instead of you, O Absalom, my son, my son!" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 18#2 Samuel 18 . 33|2 Samuel 18:33]]). But the chaos in his house is not finished—the narrative immediately continues with further rebellion and conflict.

### David's Census and the Plague

Late in David's reign, he commits another sin that brings catastrophic judgment. "Again the anger of the LORD was kindled against Israel, and he incited David against them, saying, 'Go, number Israel and Judah'" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 1|2 Samuel 24:1]]). The parallel account in 1 Chronicles attributes the incitement to Satan ([[Readable Bible/13 - 1 Chronicles/1 Chronicles 21#1 Chronicles 21 . 1|1 Chronicles 21:1]]), suggesting both divine permission and satanic agency.

David commands Joab to conduct a census. Joab objects—"Why does my lord the king delight in this thing?" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 3|2 Samuel 24:3]])—but David insists. The census takes nine months and twenty days, yielding 800,000 fighting men in Israel and 500,000 in Judah ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 9|2 Samuel 24:9]]).

Immediately, "David's heart struck him after he had numbered the people" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 10|2 Samuel 24:10]]), and he confesses: "I have sinned greatly in what I have done. But now, O LORD, please take away the iniquity of your servant, for I have done very foolishly" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 10|2 Samuel 24:10]]).

The prophet Gad offers David three choices of punishment: three years of famine, three months of fleeing before enemies, or three days of plague ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 13|2 Samuel 24:13]]). David chooses the plague, saying, "Let us fall into the hand of the LORD, for his mercy is great; but let me not fall into the hand of man" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 14|2 Samuel 24:14]]).

The plague strikes, and 70,000 men die ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 15|2 Samuel 24:15]]). When the destroying angel reaches Jerusalem, the LORD relents, and the angel stops at the threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 16|2 Samuel 24:16]]). David purchases the threshing floor and offers sacrifices, and "the plague was averted from Israel" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 25|2 Samuel 24:25]]).

What was David's sin? The census itself was not inherently wrong—Moses had conducted censuses by divine command. The sin appears to be David's motive: pride in military strength, reliance on human resources rather than divine power, or perhaps a violation of census regulations requiring atonement money ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 30#Exodus 30 . 12|Exodus 30:12]]). Whatever the specific offense, the result is clear: even the best of Israel's kings brings death and judgment through his sin.

Kline observes that the census represents David's attempt to quantify and control Israel's strength in a way that usurps God's sovereignty. It is a grasp at autonomous power, a reliance on numbers rather than covenant faithfulness. The threshing floor where the plague stops will become the site of Solomon's temple ([[Readable Bible/14 - 2 Chronicles/2 Chronicles 3#2 Chronicles 3 . 1|2 Chronicles 3:1]]), suggesting that even out of this judgment, God will bring redemptive purpose.

## Solomon: Wisdom and Apostasy

### The Glory That Exceeded All Kings

Solomon begins with unprecedented divine blessing. When God appears to him at Gibeon and offers, "Ask what I shall give you" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 3#1 Kings 3 . 5|1 Kings 3:5]]), Solomon requests "an understanding mind to govern your people, that I may discern between good and evil" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 3#1 Kings 3 . 9|1 Kings 3:9]]). God is pleased and grants not only wisdom but also riches and honor beyond any king ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 3#1 Kings 3 . 12|1 Kings 3:12-13]]).

Solomon's wisdom becomes legendary. He speaks 3,000 proverbs and 1,005 songs ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 4#1 Kings 4 . 32|1 Kings 4:32]]), discourses on botany and zoology ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 4#1 Kings 4 . 33|1 Kings 4:33]]), and people from all nations come to hear him ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 4#1 Kings 4 . 34|1 Kings 4:34]]). His kingdom is vast and prosperous: "Judah and Israel were as many as the sand by the sea. They ate and drank and were happy" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 4#1 Kings 4 . 20|1 Kings 4:20]]).

The centerpiece of Solomon's reign is the temple. Seven years in construction ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 6#1 Kings 6 . 38|1 Kings 6:38]]), it is a magnificent structure of stone and cedar, overlaid with gold, featuring the Holy of Holies as a perfect cube (20 cubits in each dimension) housing the ark of the covenant ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 6#1 Kings 6 . 20|1 Kings 6:20]]). When the priests bring the ark into the temple, "the cloud filled the house of the LORD, so that the priests could not stand to minister because of the cloud, for the glory of the LORD filled the house of the LORD" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 8#1 Kings 8 . 10|1 Kings 8:10-11]]).

Solomon's dedicatory prayer is magnificent, acknowledging God's transcendence—"But will God indeed dwell on the earth? Behold, heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you; how much less this house that I have built!" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 8#1 Kings 8 . 27|1 Kings 8:27]])—while asking God to make the temple a place where prayers are heard. He specifically prays for foreigners who come to pray toward this house ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 8#1 Kings 8 . 41|1 Kings 8:41-43]]), envisioning a global witness.

God's response is both promise and warning: "I have consecrated this house that you have built, by putting my name there forever. My eyes and my heart will be there for all time" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 9#1 Kings 9 . 3|1 Kings 9:3]]), but "if you turn aside from following me, you or your children, and do not keep my commandments and my statutes that I have set before you, but go and serve other gods and worship them, then I will cut off Israel from the land that I have given them, and the house that I have consecrated for my name I will cast out of my sight" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 9#1 Kings 9 . 6|1 Kings 9:6-7]]).

Beale sees Solomon's temple as the high point of Israel's attempt to restore Eden. The temple is the garden sanctuary, the place of God's dwelling with humanity, the center from which blessing should extend to all nations. The Queen of Sheba's visit ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 10#1 Kings 10 . 1|1 Kings 10:1-13]]) demonstrates that the nations are indeed coming to Jerusalem's light. Solomon appears to be the Adamic king par excellence, exercising dominion in wisdom and building the house where God dwells.

### The Fatal Apostasy

But Solomon violates the precise warnings given to Israel's kings in Deuteronomy 17. The law commanded that the king "must not acquire many horses for himself" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 17#Deuteronomy 17 . 16|Deuteronomy 17:16]]), yet Solomon accumulates 40,000 stalls of horses for his chariots ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 4#1 Kings 4 . 26|1 Kings 4:26]]). The law commanded that he "shall not acquire many wives for himself, lest his heart turn away" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 17#Deuteronomy 17 . 17|Deuteronomy 17:17]]), yet Solomon has 700 wives and 300 concubines ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 11#1 Kings 11 . 3|1 Kings 11:3]]). The law commanded that he "not acquire for himself excessive silver and gold" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 17#Deuteronomy 17 . 17|Deuteronomy 17:17]]), yet Solomon's wealth is beyond measure ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 10#1 Kings 10 . 14|1 Kings 10:14-25]]).

The consequences are devastating: "For when Solomon was old his wives turned away his heart after other gods, and his heart was not wholly true to the LORD his God, as was the heart of David his father. For Solomon went after Ashtoreth the goddess of the Sidonians, and after Milcom the abomination of the Ammonites" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 11#1 Kings 11 . 4|1 Kings 11:4-5]]). He builds high places for Chemosh the god of Moab and Molech the god of Ammon on the mountain east of Jerusalem ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 11#1 Kings 11 . 7|1 Kings 11:7]]), and "so he did for all his foreign wives, who made offerings and sacrificed to their gods" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 11#1 Kings 11 . 8|1 Kings 11:8]]).

The verdict is damning: "And the LORD was angry with Solomon, because his heart had turned away from the LORD, the God of Israel, who had appeared to him twice and had commanded him concerning this thing, that he should not go after other gods. But he did not keep what the LORD commanded" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 11#1 Kings 11 . 9|1 Kings 11:9-10]]).

God pronounces judgment: "Since this has been your practice and you have not kept my covenant and my statutes that I have commanded you, I will surely tear the kingdom from you and will give it to your servant. Yet for the sake of David your father I will not do it in your days, but I will tear it out of the hand of your son. However, I will not tear away all the kingdom, but I will give one tribe to your son, for the sake of David my servant and for the sake of Jerusalem that I have chosen" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 11#1 Kings 11 . 11|1 Kings 11:11-13]]).

Kline notes the theological significance: Solomon, at the pinnacle of theocratic kingship, the builder of the temple where heaven and earth meet, commits the ultimate covenant betrayal—idolatry. He who should have led the nations to worship Yahweh instead imports the gods of the nations into the holy mountain. The temple still stands, the sacrifices continue, but the king's heart has departed. The kingdom that seemed to fulfill the Abrahamic promise—blessing to the nations through Abraham's seed—is about to fracture.

Beale adds that Solomon's apostasy represents the Adamic failure at its worst. Having been given dominion, wisdom, and the very presence of God in the temple-Eden, Solomon chooses the forbidden fruit of foreign gods. Like Adam, he grasps at knowledge and experience beyond God's boundaries. The result will be exile from the land, just as Adam was exiled from Eden.

## The Divided Kingdom: Permanent Chaos

### Rehoboam's Folly and the Division

Solomon's son Rehoboam faces the assembled tribes at Shechem. The people, led by Jeroboam, ask for relief from Solomon's heavy labor and taxation: "Your father made our yoke heavy. Now therefore lighten the hard service of your father and his heavy yoke on us, and we will serve you" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 12#1 Kings 12 . 4|1 Kings 12:4]]).

The older counselors advise Rehoboam: "If you will be a servant to this people today and serve them, and speak good words to them when you answer them, then they will be your servants forever" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 12#1 Kings 12 . 7|1 Kings 12:7]]). But Rehoboam rejects their counsel and follows the advice of his young peers: "My father made your yoke heavy, but I will add to your yoke. My father disciplined you with whips, but I will discipline you with scorpions" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 12#1 Kings 12 . 14|1 Kings 12:14]]).

The response is immediate and catastrophic: "What portion do we have in David? We have no inheritance in the son of Jesse. To your tents, O Israel! Look now to your own house, David" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 12#1 Kings 12 . 16|1 Kings 12:16]]). Ten tribes defect to Jeroboam, leaving only Judah and Benjamin loyal to Rehoboam ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 12#1 Kings 12 . 20|1 Kings 12:20-21]]).

The narrator's comment is crucial: "So it was the fulfillment of the word of the LORD, which he spoke by Ahijah the Shilonite to Jeroboam the son of Nebat" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 12#1 Kings 12 . 15|1 Kings 12:15]]). The division is both human folly and divine judgment. Rehoboam's arrogance triggers the split, but God has ordained it as punishment for Solomon's idolatry.

### Jeroboam's Golden Calves

Jeroboam, now king of the northern ten tribes, faces a problem: if his people go to Jerusalem to worship, "the heart of this people will turn again to their lord, to Rehoboam king of Judah, and they will kill me and return to Rehoboam king of Judah" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 12#1 Kings 12 . 27|1 Kings 12:27]]). His solution is to create an alternative worship system.

He makes two golden calves and sets them up in Bethel and Dan, saying, "You have gone up to Jerusalem long enough. Behold your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 12#1 Kings 12 . 28|1 Kings 12:28]]). The language deliberately echoes Aaron's sin at Sinai (Exodus 32:4), linking Jeroboam's apostasy to the archetypal wilderness rebellion. He also makes houses on high places, appoints non-Levitical priests, and institutes a rival festival on the fifteenth day of the eighth month—a month he "devised from his own heart" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 12#1 Kings 12 . 33|1 Kings 12:33]]).

A man of God from Judah confronts Jeroboam at the altar in Bethel, prophesying that a future king named Josiah will sacrifice the idolatrous priests on this very altar ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 13#1 Kings 13 . 2|1 Kings 13:2]])—a prophecy fulfilled over 300 years later ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 23#2 Kings 23 . 15|2 Kings 23:15-16]]). When Jeroboam stretches out his hand to seize the prophet, his hand withers ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 13#1 Kings 13 . 4|1 Kings 13:4]]), and the altar splits apart ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 13#1 Kings 13 . 5|1 Kings 13:5]]).

Despite this dramatic sign, Jeroboam does not repent: "After this thing Jeroboam did not turn from his evil way, but made priests for the high places again from among all the people" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 13#1 Kings 13 . 33|1 Kings 13:33]]). The narrator's assessment is final: "And this thing became sin to the house of Jeroboam, so as to cut it off and to destroy it from the face of the earth" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 13#1 Kings 13 . 34|1 Kings 13:34]]).

"The sin of Jeroboam" becomes the refrain by which every subsequent northern king is judged. The Northern Kingdom never experiences covenant faithfulness or legitimate worship. Its entire existence, from beginning to end, is characterized by systemic apostasy.

## Northern Kingdom: Unrelenting Chaos

The Northern Kingdom's history is a litany of wickedness. Of its nineteen kings across nine dynasties, not one receives a positive evaluation. The formula recurs with depressing regularity: "He did what was evil in the sight of the LORD and walked in the way of Jeroboam and in his sin which he made Israel to sin."

### The Omri Dynasty and Baal Worship

The nadir comes with the Omri dynasty. Omri himself "did more evil than all who were before him" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 16#1 Kings 16 . 25|1 Kings 16:25]]), but his son Ahab exceeds even this: "And as if it had been a light thing for him to walk in the sins of Jeroboam the son of Nebat, he took for his wife Jezebel the daughter of Ethbaal king of the Sidonians, and went and served Baal and worshiped him" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 16#1 Kings 16 . 31|1 Kings 16:31]]).

Ahab builds a house for Baal in Samaria, makes an Asherah, and "did more to provoke the LORD, the God of Israel, to anger than all the kings of Israel who were before him" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 16#1 Kings 16 . 33|1 Kings 16:33]]). Jezebel actively persecutes the prophets of Yahweh, cutting them off ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 18#1 Kings 18 . 4|1 Kings 18:4]]), while supporting 450 prophets of Baal and 400 prophets of Asherah at her table ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 18#1 Kings 18 . 19|1 Kings 18:19]]).

The contest on Mount Carmel between Elijah and the prophets of Baal ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 18#1 Kings 18 . 20|1 Kings 18:20-40]]) demonstrates Yahweh's supremacy, but Ahab and Jezebel do not repent. When Ahab covets Naboth's vineyard and Naboth refuses to sell his ancestral inheritance, Jezebel arranges false testimony to have Naboth executed for blasphemy, and Ahab seizes the vineyard ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 21#1 Kings 21 . 1|1 Kings 21:1-16]]).

Elijah pronounces devastating judgment: "In the place where dogs licked up the blood of Naboth shall dogs lick your own blood" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 21#1 Kings 21 . 19|1 Kings 21:19]]), and concerning Jezebel: "The dogs shall eat Jezebel within the walls of Jezreel" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 21#1 Kings 21 . 23|1 Kings 21:23]]). Both prophecies are fulfilled exactly ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 9#2 Kings 9 . 33|2 Kings 9:33-37]]; [[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 22#1 Kings 22 . 38|1 Kings 22:38]]).

### The Fall of Samaria

The Northern Kingdom's end comes in 722 BC when the Assyrians conquer Samaria after a three-year siege ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 17#2 Kings 17 . 5|2 Kings 17:5-6]]). The narrator provides a comprehensive theological explanation: "And this occurred because the people of Israel had sinned against the LORD their God, who had brought them up out of the land of Egypt from under the hand of Pharaoh king of Egypt, and had feared other gods and walked in the customs of the nations whom the LORD drove out before the people of Israel, and in the customs that the kings of Israel had practiced" ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 17#2 Kings 17 . 7|2 Kings 17:7-8]]).

The indictment is exhaustive: they built high places, set up pillars and Asherim, burned incense to other gods, served idols, made metal images, worshiped the host of heaven, served Baal, burned their sons and daughters as offerings, practiced divination and omens, and sold themselves to do evil ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 17#2 Kings 17 . 16|2 Kings 17:16-17]]).

The conclusion is stark: "Therefore the LORD was very angry with Israel and removed them out of his sight. None was left but the tribe of Judah only" ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 17#2 Kings 17 . 18|2 Kings 17:18]]). The ten northern tribes are deported and scattered, never to return as a unified entity. The kingdom established by Jeroboam's apostasy ends in complete destruction.

Beale sees the fall of the Northern Kingdom as a partial fulfillment of Adamic exile. The Northern tribes, having rejected God's temple and instituted false worship, are expelled from the land just as Adam was expelled from Eden. Their chaos—political, religious, moral—leads inevitably to judgment and expulsion.

## Southern Kingdom: Incomplete Chaos with Glimmers of Hope

The Southern Kingdom of Judah presents a mixed picture. While it includes godly kings who bring reform, the overall trajectory is downward, punctuated by periods of revival that prove temporary.

### Occasional Good Kings

Asa "did what was right in the eyes of the LORD, as David his father had done. He put away the male cult prostitutes out of the land and removed all the idols that his fathers had made" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 15#1 Kings 15 . 11|1 Kings 15:11-12]]). His son Jehoshaphat "walked in all the way of Asa his father. He did not turn aside from it, doing what was right in the sight of the LORD" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 22#1 Kings 22 . 43|1 Kings 22:43]]).

Hezekiah "did what was right in the eyes of the LORD, according to all that David his father had done. He removed the high places and broke the pillars and cut down the Asherah. And he broke in pieces the bronze serpent that Moses had made, for until those days the people of Israel had made offerings to it" ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 18#2 Kings 18 . 3|2 Kings 18:3-4]]). When Assyria threatens Jerusalem, Hezekiah prays, and God delivers the city miraculously, striking down 185,000 Assyrians in one night ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 19#2 Kings 19 . 35|2 Kings 19:35]]).

Josiah receives the highest praise: "Before him there was no king like him, who turned to the LORD with all his heart and with all his soul and with all his might, according to all the Law of Moses, nor did any like him arise after him" ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 23#2 Kings 23 . 25|2 Kings 23:25]]). When the Book of the Law is discovered during temple repairs ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 22#2 Kings 22 . 8|2 Kings 22:8]]), Josiah tears his clothes in repentance ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 22#2 Kings 22 . 11|2 Kings 22:11]]) and institutes comprehensive reforms: he destroys the high places, removes idolatrous priests, defiles Topheth where children were burned to Molech, and even fulfills the ancient prophecy by sacrificing the priests of the high places on the altar at Bethel ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 23#2 Kings 23 . 20|2 Kings 23:20]]).

### Predominantly Evil Kings

But these godly kings are exceptions. The majority of Judah's rulers do evil. Rehoboam, Solomon's son, oversees widespread idolatry: "For they also built for themselves high places and pillars and Asherim on every high hill and under every green tree, and there were also male cult prostitutes in the land. They did according to all the abominations of the nations that the LORD drove out before the people of Israel" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 14#1 Kings 14 . 23|1 Kings 14:23-24]]).

Manasseh, Hezekiah's son, undoes his father's reforms and commits abominations exceeding even the Canaanites: "He rebuilt the high places that Hezekiah his father had destroyed, and he erected altars for Baal and made an Asherah, as Ahab king of Israel had done, and worshiped all the host of heaven and served them" ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 21#2 Kings 21 . 3|2 Kings 21:3]]). He builds altars in the house of the Lord itself ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 21#2 Kings 21 . 4|2 Kings 21:4]]), burns his son as an offering ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 21#2 Kings 21 . 6|2 Kings 21:6]]), practices divination and omens, and "shed very much innocent blood, till he had filled Jerusalem from one end to another" ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 21#2 Kings 21 . 16|2 Kings 21:16]]).

The verdict on Manasseh is that his sin is unforgivable at the national level: "Still the LORD did not turn from the burning of his great wrath, by which his anger was kindled against Judah, because of all the provocations with which Manasseh had provoked him" ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 23#2 Kings 23 . 26|2 Kings 23:26]]). Even Josiah's reforms cannot avert the coming judgment.

### The Fall of Jerusalem

The end comes in stages. Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon besieges Jerusalem multiple times. In 597 BC, he deports King Jehoiachin along with the royal family, officials, and skilled craftsmen—10,000 people ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 24#2 Kings 24 . 14|2 Kings 24:14]]). He installs Zedekiah as puppet king, but Zedekiah rebels ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 24#2 Kings 24 . 20|2 Kings 24:20]]).

In 586 BC, Nebuchadnezzar returns and lays siege to Jerusalem for eighteen months. Famine becomes so severe that people resort to cannibalism (Lamentations 4:10). Finally, the Babylonians break through the walls. Zedekiah flees but is captured. The Babylonians kill his sons before his eyes, then gouge out his eyes and take him in chains to Babylon ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 25#2 Kings 25 . 7|2 Kings 25:7]]).

The temple of Solomon, the glory of Israel, is burned to the ground: "And he burned the house of the LORD and the king's house and all the houses of Jerusalem; every great house he burned down" ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 25#2 Kings 25 . 9|2 Kings 25:9]]). The bronze pillars, the stands, and the bronze sea are broken up and carried to Babylon ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 25#2 Kings 25 . 13|2 Kings 25:13]]). The chief priests and leading citizens are executed at Riblah ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 25#2 Kings 25 . 21|2 Kings 25:21]]).

The people are deported, and "Judah was taken into exile out of its land" ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 25#2 Kings 25 . 21|2 Kings 25:21]]). The Davidic throne stands empty. The ark of the covenant has disappeared, never to be recovered. The glory that filled Solomon's temple has departed. The corporate Adam of Israel's monarchy has failed utterly, and the people face the curse of exile.

Yet the narrative ends with a tiny flicker of hope: "And in the thirty-seventh year of the exile of Jehoiachin king of Judah, in the twelfth month, on the twenty-seventh day of the month, Evil-merodach king of Babylon, in the year that he began to reign, graciously freed Jehoiachin king of Judah from prison. And he spoke kindly to him and gave him a seat above the seats of the kings who were with him in Babylon. So Jehoiachin put off his prison garments. And every day of his life he dined regularly at the king's table" ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 25#2 Kings 25 . 27|2 Kings 25:27-29]]).

Jehoiachin, the Davidic king, is still alive. The line of David continues, even in exile. The promise to David—"Your throne shall be established forever" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 7#2 Samuel 7 . 16|2 Samuel 7:16]])—has not been completely nullified. The chaos of the monarchy is complete, but the stage is set for a greater David, a true Son who will reign forever.

## Theological Summary: Corporate Adam's Failure and the Need for the True King

The chaos of the monarchy period reveals the depth of humanity's fallenness and the impossibility of human kingship solving the Adamic problem. Saul, David, Solomon, and all their successors prove unable to be the true corporate Adam who restores Eden and extends God's rule to the nations.

Beale summarizes: "The kings of Israel and Judah were supposed to function as corporate Adams, exercising dominion in righteousness, guarding the sanctuary, and expanding God's reign. But they failed repeatedly and catastrophically. The very best of them—David and Solomon—committed grievous sins. The worst of them—Ahab, Manasseh—led the nation into abominations worse than the Canaanites. The overall trajectory is chaos compounding chaos, culminating in the destruction of the temple and exile from the land, recapitulating Adam's expulsion from Eden."

Kline adds: "The monarchy was meant to be an intrusion of eschatological kingship into history, a foretaste of the consummated kingdom. But human kings could not bear the weight of this calling. They grasped at autonomy, multiplied horses and wives, imported foreign gods, and brought judgment rather than blessing. The divided kingdom shows that human rule, even when divinely instituted, fragments under the weight of sin. Only a king who is fully God and fully man, who perfectly obeys in dependent sonship, can establish the eternal throne and reunite the scattered tribes."

The chaos of the monarchy drives the reader forward to the prophecies of a coming King: the Branch from Jesse's stump ([[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 11#Isaiah 11 . 1|Isaiah 11:1]]), the child who will be called Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace, of whose kingdom there will be no end ([[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 9#Isaiah 9 . 6|Isaiah 9:6-7]]), the one who will restore the fortunes of Judah and Israel and reunite them under one shepherd ([[Readable Bible/26 - Ezekiel/Ezekiel 37#Ezekiel 37 . 24|Ezekiel 37:24]]).

The chaos of human kingship is not the end of the story. It is the necessary demonstration that no son of Adam can save Adam's race. The true King must come from outside the chaos, yet be fully identified with those trapped in it—the eternal Son becoming the son of David, the Lord becoming the suffering servant, the King whose throne is established not by military conquest but by atoning death and victorious resurrection.
