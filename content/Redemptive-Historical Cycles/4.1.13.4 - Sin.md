# 4.1.13.4 - Sin (Kings - United and Divided Monarchy)

## Overview: The Failure of the Corporate Adam

The sin of Israel's kings is not merely personal moral failure but covenant betrayal at the highest level. The king, as corporate representative, embodies the nation's relationship with God. When the king sins, the entire nation is implicated. When the king leads into idolatry, the people follow. The monarchy period demonstrates that even the best human kings—divinely chosen, Spirit-anointed, covenant-bound—recapitulate Adam's fall rather than reverse it.

The pattern is tragic and consistent: initial blessing and divine favor, then gradual or sudden turning from covenant faithfulness, followed by judgment. Saul begins well but ends in disobedience, jealousy, and occult practices. David, the man after God's heart, commits adultery and murder. Solomon, the wisest of men, turns to idolatry through his foreign wives. The Northern Kingdom, from its inception, is founded on false worship and never produces a single godly king. The Southern Kingdom oscillates between apostasy and reform, but the overall trajectory is downward toward exile.

Beale emphasizes that the kings function as corporate Adams. Like Adam, they are placed in a position of authority and blessing, given explicit commands, and warned of consequences for disobedience. Like Adam, they choose autonomy over obedience, grasp at what is forbidden, and bring death rather than life. The monarchy's sin reveals that simply having a king "after God's heart" is insufficient—what is needed is a King who is both fully God and fully human, who can perfectly obey where Adam and all his descendants failed.

Kline adds that the kings' sin demonstrates the impossibility of establishing the eschatological kingdom through fallen human agency. The intrusion of God's kingdom into history requires a sinless mediator, one who can bear the weight of covenant representation without succumbing to sin. The monarchy's failure creates the space for the gospel: the announcement that God Himself has come in Christ to be the true King, the second Adam who succeeds where the first Adam and all subsequent Adams failed.

## Saul: The Sin of Incomplete Obedience and Self-Will

### The Sin at Gilgal: Usurping Priestly Authority

Saul's first recorded sin occurs at Gilgal, facing a Philistine threat. Samuel had instructed him: "Go down before me to Gilgal. And behold, I am coming down to you to offer burnt offerings and to sacrifice peace offerings. Seven days you shall wait, until I come to you and show you what you shall do" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 10#1 Samuel 10 . 8|1 Samuel 10:8]]).

Saul waits the seven days, but as the people begin to scatter in fear, he takes matters into his own hands: "So Saul said, 'Bring the burnt offering here to me, and the peace offerings.' And he offered the burnt offering" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 13#1 Samuel 13 . 9|1 Samuel 13:9]]).

The sin here is not the act of sacrifice itself but the usurpation of authority. Saul, the king, performs the function reserved for Samuel, the prophet-priest. He acts independently of God's explicit command, prioritizing pragmatic military concerns (keeping the troops from scattering) over covenant obedience (waiting for Samuel).

When Samuel arrives, his rebuke is devastating: "You have done foolishly. You have not kept the command of the LORD your God, with which he commanded you. For then the LORD would have established your kingdom over Israel forever. But now your kingdom shall not continue. The LORD has sought out a man after his own heart, and the LORD has commanded him to be prince over his people, because you have not kept what the LORD commanded you" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 13#1 Samuel 13 . 13|1 Samuel 13:13-14]]).

The theological issue is fundamental: covenant authority and dependent obedience. Saul has demonstrated that he will be a king who acts on his own initiative, who modifies or ignores divine commands when they seem inconvenient, who prioritizes results over obedience. This is Adamic sin—the assertion of autonomy, the determination to decide for oneself what is good and necessary, rather than trusting and obeying God's word.

Beale notes that Saul's sin parallels Adam's. Both received explicit divine commands. Both faced circumstances that seemed to call for independent action (Adam: "you will be like God, knowing good and evil"; Saul: "the people are scattering, the enemy is gathering, Samuel hasn't come"). Both chose to act on their own judgment rather than trust God's word. Both discovered that autonomy leads to death—Adam dies and is expelled from Eden; Saul's kingdom dies and will be taken from him.

### The Sin at Amalek: Partial Obedience and Self-Deception

Saul's second and final rejection comes in the Amalek campaign. God's command through Samuel is unambiguous: "Now go and strike Amalek and devote to destruction all that they have. Do not spare them, but kill both man and woman, child and infant, ox and sheep, camel and donkey" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 3|1 Samuel 15:3]]).

This is ḥerem warfare—the total devotion to God of everything under judgment. It is God's prerogative to command this, and it is Israel's obligation to obey completely. The command is clear, comprehensive, and non-negotiable.

But Saul obeys selectively: "Saul and the people spared Agag and the best of the sheep and of the oxen and of the fattened calves and the lambs, and all that was good, and would not utterly destroy them. All that was despised and worthless they devoted to destruction" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 9|1 Samuel 15:9]]).

When Samuel confronts him, Saul's response reveals profound self-deception: "I have performed the commandment of the LORD" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 13|1 Samuel 15:13]]). Samuel's ironic question cuts through the lie: "What then is this bleating of the sheep in my ears and the lowing of the oxen that I hear?" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 14|1 Samuel 15:14]]).

Saul attempts to justify himself: "They have brought them from the Amalekites, for the people spared the best of the sheep and of the oxen to sacrifice to the LORD your God, and the rest we have devoted to destruction" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 15|1 Samuel 15:15]]). Notice the blame-shifting ("the people spared") and the rationalization ("to sacrifice to the LORD").

Samuel will not accept it: "Has the LORD as great delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices, as in obeying the voice of the LORD? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to listen than the fat of rams. For rebellion is as the sin of divination, and presumption is as iniquity and idolatry. Because you have rejected the word of the LORD, he has also rejected you from being king" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 22|1 Samuel 15:22-23]]).

This is one of Scripture's most important theological statements. Saul's sin is not merely disobeying a command but "rejecting the word of the LORD." His partial obedience is equivalent to divination (consulting spirits rather than God) and idolatry (worshiping false gods). Why? Because both involve autonomous self-direction, placing one's own will or other authorities above God's explicit word.

Saul finally confesses, "I have sinned, for I have transgressed the commandment of the LORD and your words, because I feared the people and obeyed their voice" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 24|1 Samuel 15:24]]). But even this confession is shallow—he immediately asks Samuel to honor him before the elders ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 15#1 Samuel 15 . 30|1 Samuel 15:30]]), revealing that his concern is public reputation, not true repentance.

The sin of Amalek reveals several dimensions of royal disobedience:

1. **Incomplete obedience**: Saul obeyed most of the command but not all. He destroyed what was "despised and worthless" but kept what was "good." This selective obedience is, in God's eyes, disobedience.

2. **Self-justification**: Saul convinces himself he has obeyed. He rationalizes keeping the livestock as preparation for sacrifice. He reinterprets God's command to fit his actions rather than conforming his actions to God's command.

3. **Blame-shifting**: Like Adam ("The woman you gave me"), Saul blames "the people." He refuses to take full responsibility as the covenant head.

4. **Fear of man rather than fear of God**: "I feared the people and obeyed their voice." Saul prioritizes human opinion and approval over divine command.

5. **Religious performance substituted for obedience**: Saul proposes sacrifice as compensation for disobedience, but God rejects this. Worship without obedience is worthless.

Kline observes that Saul's sin demonstrates the impossibility of establishing the theocratic kingdom on the foundation of human obedience. Saul, though chosen and anointed by God, cannot sustain dependent covenant faithfulness. He repeatedly asserts autonomy, modifies divine commands to suit his judgment, and ultimately rejects the word of the Lord. This rejection is not merely moral failure but covenant apostasy—the king who should mediate God's rule instead rebels against it.

### The Descent into Jealousy, Violence, and Occultism

After his rejection, Saul's sin escalates. When David defeats Goliath and the women sing, "Saul has struck down his thousands, and David his ten thousands" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 18#1 Samuel 18 . 7|1 Samuel 18:7]]), Saul becomes consumed with jealousy: "And Saul eyed David from that day on" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 18#1 Samuel 18 . 9|1 Samuel 18:9]]).

Jealousy leads to attempted murder. Twice Saul hurls his spear at David while David plays music for him ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 18#1 Samuel 18 . 11|1 Samuel 18:11]]; [[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 19#1 Samuel 19 . 10|19:10]]). When David flees, Saul pursues him relentlessly, devoting vast resources to hunting down the man God has chosen as his successor.

The nadir of Saul's sin comes when he murders the priests of Nob for helping David. Ahimelech the priest innocently gave David bread and Goliath's sword, not knowing David was fleeing from Saul ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 21#1 Samuel 21 . 1|1 Samuel 21:1-9]]). When Saul learns of this, he commands his guards: "Turn and kill the priests of the LORD, because their hand also is with David, and they knew that he fled and did not disclose it to me" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 22#1 Samuel 22 . 17|1 Samuel 22:17]]).

The guards refuse—"But the servants of the king would not put out their hand to strike the priests of the LORD" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 22#1 Samuel 22 . 17|1 Samuel 22:17]]). So Saul commands Doeg the Edomite, who kills 85 priests and then goes to Nob and kills "both men and women, children and infants, oxen, donkeys, and sheep" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 22#1 Samuel 22 . 18|1 Samuel 22:18-19]]).

This is horrific sin. Saul, the anointed king, murders God's priests and destroys a priestly city. He treats God's servants as enemies and commits the kind of total destruction (ḥerem) that he failed to carry out against Amalek. The irony is bitter—he preserved Amalek's livestock for sacrifice but slaughters the priests who offer sacrifices.

Finally, in desperation before his last battle, Saul consults a medium at Endor ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 28#1 Samuel 28 . 7|1 Samuel 28:7-25]]), directly violating God's law: "Do not turn to mediums or necromancers; do not seek them out, and so make yourselves unclean by them: I am the LORD your God" ([[Readable Bible/03 - Leviticus/Leviticus 19#Leviticus 19 . 31|Leviticus 19:31]]). Ironically, Saul himself had earlier "put the mediums and the necromancers out of the land" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 28#1 Samuel 28 . 3|1 Samuel 28:3]]), yet now he seeks one out.

The medium conjures Samuel's spirit (whether genuinely or as a demonic deception is debated), and Samuel announces Saul's doom: "The LORD has torn the kingdom out of your hand and given it to your neighbor, David... Moreover, the LORD will give Israel also with you into the hand of the Philistines, and tomorrow you and your sons shall be with me" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 28#1 Samuel 28 . 17|1 Samuel 28:17, 19]]).

The next day, Saul dies by suicide, falling on his own sword after being wounded in battle ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 31#1 Samuel 31 . 4|1 Samuel 31:4]]). His body is desecrated by the Philistines ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 31#1 Samuel 31 . 9|1 Samuel 31:9-10]]). The first king of Israel ends in complete failure—disobedient, jealous, murderous, occultic, suicidal, defeated, desecrated.

Beale summarizes: "Saul's trajectory from anointed king to rejected rebel to suicidal corpse demonstrates the downward spiral of sin. What begins as incomplete obedience escalates into violent jealousy, priestly murder, occult consultation, and self-destruction. Saul embodies corporate Adam's fall—from blessing and calling to curse and death."

## David: The Sin of Adultery, Murder, and Pride

### The Bathsheba Incident: Adultery and Murder

David, "a man after [God's] own heart" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 13#1 Samuel 13 . 14|1 Samuel 13:14]]), commits grievous sin at the height of his power. The narrative begins ominously: "In the spring of the year, the time when kings go out to battle, David sent Joab, and his servants with him, and all Israel... But David remained at Jerusalem" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 1|2 Samuel 11:1]]).

David is not where he should be. Kings go out to battle in spring; David stays home. This spatial dislocation signals moral dislocation.

Late one afternoon, David walks on his roof and sees "a woman bathing; and the woman was very beautiful" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 2|2 Samuel 11:2]]). He inquires about her and is told, "Is not this Bathsheba, the daughter of Eliam, the wife of Uriah the Hittite?" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 3|2 Samuel 11:3]]). The information is clear: she is married. This should end the matter.

But David sends messengers to take her, "and she came to him, and he lay with her" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 4|2 Samuel 11:4]]). The verb "take" is ominous—it suggests taking possession, acquiring what belongs to another. The woman becomes pregnant and sends word to David: "I am pregnant" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 5|2 Samuel 11:5]]).

David's response is not repentance but cover-up. He summons Uriah from the battlefield, ostensibly to receive news of the war, but actually hoping Uriah will sleep with his wife, thus providing cover for the pregnancy ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 6|2 Samuel 11:6-8]]). David explicitly tells him, "Go down to your house and wash your feet" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 8|2 Samuel 11:8]])—a euphemism for marital intimacy.

But Uriah, a Hittite convert to Yahwism, demonstrates greater covenant faithfulness than David. He refuses to go home: "The ark and Israel and Judah dwell in booths, and my lord Joab and the servants of my lord are camping in the open field. Shall I then go to my house, to eat and to drink and to lie with my wife? As you live, and as your soul lives, I will not do this thing" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 11|2 Samuel 11:11]]).

Uriah's integrity contrasts starkly with David's corruption. The Gentile soldier displays self-denial and solidarity with his comrades and God's ark, while the Israelite king indulges in adultery. Uriah will not enjoy domestic pleasure while his brothers are in the field and the ark is in a tent. David, who should be with the ark and the army, stays in Jerusalem for sexual pleasure.

Desperate, David tries again, getting Uriah drunk, but even intoxicated, Uriah maintains his integrity: "in the evening he went out to lie on his couch with the servants of his lord, but he did not go down to his house" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 13|2 Samuel 11:13]]).

David now escalates from adultery to murder. He sends Uriah back to battle carrying his own death warrant: "Set Uriah in the forefront of the hardest fighting, and then draw back from him, that he may be struck down, and die" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 15|2 Samuel 11:15]]).

Joab arranges the murder. Uriah is placed at the city wall where the fighting is fiercest, and when the Ammonites attack, Joab's men withdraw, leaving Uriah exposed. "Uriah the Hittite died also" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 17|2 Samuel 11:17]]). When Bathsheba hears, she mourns her husband, and "when the mourning was over, David sent and brought her to his house, and she became his wife and bore him a son" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 27|2 Samuel 11:27]]).

The narrative seems to close with David's sin successfully concealed. But the final sentence delivers the theological verdict: "But the thing that David had done displeased the LORD" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 27|2 Samuel 11:27]]).

### Nathan's Confrontation and David's Confession

God sends Nathan the prophet with a parable about a rich man who steals a poor man's only lamb to feed a guest, rather than taking from his own abundant flocks ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 1|2 Samuel 12:1-4]]).

David's anger burns: "As the LORD lives, the man who has done this deserves to die, and he shall restore the lamb fourfold, because he did this thing, and because he had no pity" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 5|2 Samuel 12:5-6]]). David pronounces judgment on himself, unwittingly declaring his own guilt.

Nathan springs the trap: "You are the man!" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 7|2 Samuel 12:7]]). He recounts God's blessings to David and delivers God's indictment: "Why have you despised the word of the LORD, to do what is evil in his sight? You have struck down Uriah the Hittite with the sword and have taken his wife to be your wife and have killed him with the sword of the Ammonites" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 9|2 Samuel 12:9]]).

The judgment is multi-faceted:

1. **The sword will never depart from David's house** ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 10|2 Samuel 12:10]])—violence will plague the royal family.

2. **Evil will rise from within David's own house** ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 11|2 Samuel 12:11]])—family members will rebel and betray.

3. **David's wives will be given to his neighbor** who will lie with them publicly ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 11|2 Samuel 12:11-12]])—David acted in secret; his punishment will be in the open.

4. **The child born from adultery will die** ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 14|2 Samuel 12:14]])—death enters David's house through his sin.

David's response contrasts with Saul's. He does not blame-shift or rationalize. He simply confesses: "I have sinned against the LORD" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 13|2 Samuel 12:13]]). Nathan announces that God has forgiven the sin—"you shall not die"—but the consequences remain: "the child who is born to you shall die" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 13|2 Samuel 12:13-14]]).

The child becomes ill, and David fasts and prays, lying all night on the ground ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 16|2 Samuel 12:16-17]]). On the seventh day, the child dies ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 18|2 Samuel 12:18]]). David rises, washes, worships, and eats, explaining: "While the child was still alive, I fasted and wept, for I said, 'Who knows whether the LORD will be gracious to me, that the child may live?' But now he is dead. Why should I fast? Can I bring him back again? I shall go to him, but he will not return to me" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 12#2 Samuel 12 . 22|2 Samuel 12:22-23]]).

### The Nature of David's Sin

David's sin with Bathsheba involves multiple violations:

**1. Coveting**: The tenth commandment prohibits coveting a neighbor's wife ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 20#Exodus 20 . 17|Exodus 20:17]]). David sees Bathsheba and desires what belongs to another.

**2. Adultery**: The seventh commandment is directly violated ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 20#Exodus 20 . 14|Exodus 20:14]]). David commits sexual immorality with a married woman.

**3. Theft**: David "takes" Bathsheba, essentially stealing Uriah's wife. Nathan's parable explicitly frames it as theft: the rich man took the poor man's lamb.

**4. Murder**: David arranges Uriah's death, violating the sixth commandment ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 20#Exodus 20 . 13|Exodus 20:13]]). Though David doesn't personally wield the sword, he is the architect of the murder.

**5. Deception**: David attempts to deceive Uriah and the nation, pretending the child is Uriah's. He abuses his royal authority to cover up sin.

**6. Abuse of power**: David uses his position as king to summon Bathsheba, command Joab, and manipulate circumstances. He turns the apparatus of state into an instrument of personal sin.

Psalm 51, David's penitential prayer, reveals his understanding of the sin's depth: "Against you, you only, have I sinned and done what is evil in your sight" ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 51#Psalm 51 . 4|Psalm 51:4]]). Though David sinned against Bathsheba, Uriah, Joab, and the nation, he recognizes that ultimately all sin is against God. He sinned against Bathsheba by taking her, against Uriah by murdering him, but supremely he sinned against God by violating His commandments and despising His word.

David also acknowledges his inherited sinful nature: "Behold, I was brought forth in iniquity, and in sin did my mother conceive me" ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 51#Psalm 51 . 5|Psalm 51:5]]). This is not blaming his mother but recognizing that he is a son of Adam, born into a sinful condition, with a heart inclined toward evil from birth. His adultery and murder flow from a corrupted nature.

Beale observes that David, the Adamic king, has failed in the temple-garden of his kingdom. He takes what is forbidden (like Adam taking the fruit), he covers his nakedness with deception (like Adam hiding in the garden), and he brings death into his house (as Adam brought death into the world). David's sin is paradigmatic corporate-Adam sin—the representative head acts, and the consequences cascade through those he represents.

### The Census: Pride and Self-Reliance

Later in his reign, David commits another significant sin. "Again the anger of the LORD was kindled against Israel, and he incited David against them, saying, 'Go, number Israel and Judah'" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 1|2 Samuel 24:1]]). The parallel account attributes the incitement to Satan ([[Readable Bible/13 - 1 Chronicles/1 Chronicles 21#1 Chronicles 21 . 1|1 Chronicles 21:1]]), suggesting both divine sovereignty (God permits) and satanic agency (Satan tempts).

David commands Joab to conduct a census. Joab objects: "May the LORD your God add to the people a hundred times as many as they are, while the eyes of my lord the king still see it, but why does my lord the king delight in this thing?" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 3|2 Samuel 24:3]]). Even Joab, not known for spiritual sensitivity, recognizes something wrong in David's command.

But David insists, and the census proceeds, taking nine months and twenty days. The results: 800,000 fighting men in Israel, 500,000 in Judah ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 9|2 Samuel 24:9]]).

Immediately, "David's heart struck him after he had numbered the people" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 10|2 Samuel 24:10]]). He confesses: "I have sinned greatly in what I have done. But now, O LORD, please take away the iniquity of your servant, for I have done very foolishly" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 10|2 Samuel 24:10]]).

What was David's sin? Several possibilities:

1. **Pride in military strength**: The census quantifies Israel's power, and David may have been congratulating himself on the size of his army, taking credit for what God had built.

2. **Self-reliance**: Numbering troops suggests planning based on human resources rather than trusting in God's deliverance.

3. **Failure to collect atonement money**: [[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 30#Exodus 30 . 12|Exodus 30:12]] requires that when a census is taken, each person must give a ransom "that there be no plague among them when you number them." David may have violated this regulation.

4. **Usurping divine prerogative**: God numbers His people ([[Readable Bible/01 - Genesis/Genesis 15#Genesis 15 . 5|Genesis 15:5]]; 22:17); for David to do so without divine command may constitute presumption.

Whatever the specific offense, God offers David three punishments: three years of famine, three months fleeing from enemies, or three days of plague ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 13|2 Samuel 24:13]]). David chooses the plague: "Let us fall into the hand of the LORD, for his mercy is great; but let me not fall into the hand of man" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 14|2 Samuel 24:14]]).

The plague strikes, and 70,000 men die ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 15|2 Samuel 24:15]]). When the destroying angel reaches Jerusalem, God relents ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 16|2 Samuel 24:16]]). David sees the angel and intercedes: "Behold, I have sinned, and I have done wickedly. But these sheep, what have they done? Please let your hand be against me and against my father's house" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 17|2 Samuel 24:17]]).

David purchases the threshing floor of Araunah, builds an altar, and offers sacrifices. "And the LORD responded to the plea for the land, and the plague was averted from Israel" ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 24#2 Samuel 24 . 25|2 Samuel 24:25]]).

Kline observes that David's census represents a grasp at autonomous control—the attempt to measure and manage Israel's strength independent of covenant reliance on God. It is a subtle but real form of pride, the Adamic sin of wanting to "be like God," determining for oneself what is good and how to achieve security. The massive death toll (70,000) demonstrates the devastating consequences of corporate Adam's sin—when the representative head sins, the people he represents suffer.

## Solomon: The Sin of Apostasy Through Foreign Marriages

### The Gradual Drift into Compromise

Solomon's sin is particularly tragic because it involves the slow erosion of covenant faithfulness despite unprecedented blessing. God gives Solomon wisdom, wealth, honor, and peace. Solomon builds the magnificent temple where God's glory dwells. Nations come to Jerusalem to hear his wisdom. Everything suggests a reign of perfect righteousness.

But the seeds of destruction are planted early. Solomon "made a marriage alliance with Pharaoh king of Egypt. He took Pharaoh's daughter and brought her into the city of David" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 3#1 Kings 3 . 1|1 Kings 3:1]]). This is the first warning sign—marriage to an Egyptian princess reverses the Exodus, seeking alliance with the house of bondage.

The narrator adds, "Solomon loved the LORD, walking in the statutes of David his father, only he sacrificed and made offerings at the high places" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 3#1 Kings 3 . 3|1 Kings 3:3]]). The "only" is ominous—Solomon is faithful except for this one area. But worship at high places, while not yet explicitly forbidden, represents a compromise, the failure to centralize worship at the tabernacle.

Solomon also violates the Deuteronomic prohibitions systematically:

**1. Multiplying horses**: "And Solomon had 40,000 stalls of horses for his chariots, and 12,000 horsemen" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 4#1 Kings 4 . 26|1 Kings 4:26]]). Deuteronomy 17:16 explicitly forbids this, yet Solomon accumulates a massive chariot force.

**2. Horses from Egypt**: "And Solomon's import of horses was from Egypt and Kue, and the king's traders received them from Kue at a price" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 10#1 Kings 10 . 28|1 Kings 10:28]]). Not only does Solomon multiply horses, but he gets them from Egypt, the very thing Deuteronomy prohibits: "Only he must not... cause the people to return to Egypt in order to acquire many horses" ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 17#Deuteronomy 17 . 16|Deuteronomy 17:16]]).

**3. Accumulating gold**: Solomon's gold revenue is 666 talents per year ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 10#1 Kings 10 . 14|1 Kings 10:14]]), and he makes silver and gold "as common in Jerusalem as stone" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 10#1 Kings 10 . 27|1 Kings 10:27]]). Deuteronomy 17:17 prohibits acquiring "excessive silver and gold."

**4. Multiplying wives**: "Now King Solomon loved many foreign women, along with the daughter of Pharaoh: Moabite, Ammonite, Edomite, Sidonian, and Hittite women, from the nations concerning which the LORD had said to the people of Israel, 'You shall not enter into marriage with them, neither shall they with you, for surely they will turn away your heart after their gods.' Solomon clung to these in love" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 11#1 Kings 11 . 1|1 Kings 11:1-2]]).

This is the most devastating violation. Solomon has 700 wives and 300 concubines ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 11#1 Kings 11 . 3|1 Kings 11:3]])—a staggering number that represents political alliances sealed by marriage, accumulation of royal prestige, and unbridled indulgence.

### The Inevitable Apostasy

The narrator's assessment is blunt: "For when Solomon was old his wives turned away his heart after other gods, and his heart was not wholly true to the LORD his God, as was the heart of David his father" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 11#1 Kings 11 . 4|1 Kings 11:4]]). The very thing God warned would happen through multiple wives has happened—Solomon's heart is divided.

The specific gods Solomon worships are named:

- **Ashtoreth**, goddess of the Sidonians—a fertility deity associated with sexual immorality
- **Milcom**, the abomination of the Ammonites—associated with child sacrifice
- **Chemosh**, the god of Moab
- **Molech**, the god of the Ammonites

Solomon not only tolerates these gods but builds "high places" for them: "Then Solomon built a high place for Chemosh the abomination of Moab, and for Molech the abomination of the Ammonites, on the mountain east of Jerusalem" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 11#1 Kings 11 . 7|1 Kings 11:7]]).

This is staggering. Solomon, who built the temple for Yahweh's name, now builds temples for pagan gods. The high places are on the mountain east of Jerusalem—visible from the temple mount, a constant affront to the house of God. "And so he did for all his foreign wives, who made offerings and sacrificed to their gods" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 11#1 Kings 11 . 8|1 Kings 11:8]]).

The verdict is devastating: "And the LORD was angry with Solomon, because his heart had turned away from the LORD, the God of Israel, who had appeared to him twice and had commanded him concerning this thing, that he should not go after other gods. But he did not keep what the LORD commanded" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 11#1 Kings 11 . 9|1 Kings 11:9-10]]).

Note the emphasis: God appeared to Solomon twice—at Gibeon granting wisdom ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 3#1 Kings 3 . 5|1 Kings 3:5-15]]) and after the temple dedication warning against apostasy ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 9#1 Kings 9 . 2|1 Kings 9:2-9]]). Solomon received direct divine revelation, explicit warning, unprecedented blessing. Yet he turned away.

### The Nature of Solomon's Sin

Solomon's sin reveals several dimensions:

**1. Gradual compromise**: Solomon doesn't plunge into idolatry immediately. He begins with one Egyptian wife, accumulates more wives over time, tolerates their gods, eventually participates in their worship, and finally builds temples for false gods. Sin is progressive—small compromises lead to great apostasy.

**2. Rationalization**: Solomon likely justified his actions. Political alliances require royal marriages. Wisdom means understanding all religions. Tolerance means allowing wives to worship as they choose. But these rationalizations lead to covenant betrayal.

**3. Dividing the heart**: Solomon's fundamental sin is a divided heart. He loves God but also loves foreign women and their gods. He builds the temple for Yahweh but also temples for Chemosh and Molech. He wants to serve God and enjoy the pleasures of the nations. Jesus's words apply: "No one can serve two masters" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 6#Matthew 6 . 24|Matthew 6:24]]).

**4. Violating explicit command**: God specifically commanded Israel not to intermarry with the surrounding nations ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 34#Exodus 34 . 16|Exodus 34:16]]; [[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 7#Deuteronomy 7 . 3|Deuteronomy 7:3-4]]). Solomon knew this but chose disobedience.

**5. Abusing blessing**: Solomon's wealth, wisdom, and international influence—all gifts from God—become instruments of sin. He uses his wisdom to accumulate wives, his wealth to build pagan shrines, his influence to spread idolatry.

Beale emphasizes that Solomon represents the Adamic failure at its zenith. Having been given the garden-temple (Solomon's temple is the fullest realization of Eden-sanctuary in the Old Testament), having been granted wisdom exceeding Adam's original knowledge, having been placed in a position to bring blessing to all nations, Solomon chooses the forbidden fruit of foreign gods. Like Adam, he is not content with God's provision but grasps at what is prohibited. Like Adam, he brings death (the kingdom will be torn from his son) rather than life.

Kline adds that Solomon's sin demonstrates the impossibility of sustaining the eschatological kingdom through human obedience. Even the wisest man, building the most glorious temple, receiving the clearest revelation, cannot maintain covenant faithfulness. The intrusion of God's kingdom into history through Solomon's reign collapses under the weight of sin. Only a king who is both divine (and thus unable to sin) and human (and thus able to represent humanity) can establish the eternal kingdom.

## The Northern Kingdom: The Sin of Institutional Apostasy

### Jeroboam's Foundational Apostasy

When the kingdom divides, Jeroboam becomes king of the northern ten tribes. Immediately, he commits the sin that will define the Northern Kingdom's entire existence. Fearing that if his people go to Jerusalem to worship, they will return allegiance to Rehoboam ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 12#1 Kings 12 . 27|1 Kings 12:27]]), Jeroboam creates an alternative worship system.

He makes two golden calves and places them in Bethel and Dan, saying, "You have gone up to Jerusalem long enough. Behold your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 12#1 Kings 12 . 28|1 Kings 12:28]]).

The language deliberately echoes Aaron's sin at Sinai ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 32#Exodus 32 . 4|Exodus 32:4]]), linking Jeroboam's apostasy to the archetypal wilderness rebellion. The calves may have been intended as pedestals for Yahweh (similar to the cherubim in the temple) or as representations of divine power, but they violate the second commandment's prohibition of graven images ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 20#Exodus 20 . 4|Exodus 20:4-5]]).

Jeroboam compounds the sin by appointing non-Levitical priests ("from among all the people" [[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 12#1 Kings 12 . 31|1 Kings 12:31]]) and instituting a rival festival on the fifteenth day of the eighth month, "a month that he had devised from his own heart" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 12#1 Kings 12 . 33|1 Kings 12:33]]).

The phrase "from his own heart" is damning. Jeroboam has created a religious system based on human invention rather than divine revelation, political expediency rather than covenant faithfulness. He has established false worship at the very foundation of the Northern Kingdom.

A man of God from Judah confronts Jeroboam, prophesying that a future king named Josiah will defile the altar by burning the bones of its priests on it ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 13#1 Kings 13 . 2|1 Kings 13:2]])—a prophecy fulfilled over 300 years later ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 23#2 Kings 23 . 15|2 Kings 23:15-16]]). When Jeroboam stretches out his hand to seize the prophet, his hand withers ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 13#1 Kings 13 . 4|1 Kings 13:4]]), and the altar splits apart ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 13#1 Kings 13 . 5|1 Kings 13:5]]).

Despite this dramatic sign, "Jeroboam did not turn from his evil way, but made priests for the high places again from among all the people" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 13#1 Kings 13 . 33|1 Kings 13:33]]). The narrator's conclusion is final: "And this thing became sin to the house of Jeroboam, so as to cut it off and to destroy it from the face of the earth" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 13#1 Kings 13 . 34|1 Kings 13:34]]).

"The sin of Jeroboam" becomes the standard by which all subsequent northern kings are judged. The phrase recurs with monotonous regularity: "He did what was evil in the sight of the LORD and walked in the way of Jeroboam and in his sin which he made Israel to sin."

### The Omri Dynasty: Escalating Evil

The northern kings progressively outdo each other in wickedness. Omri "did more evil than all who were before him" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 16#1 Kings 16 . 25|1 Kings 16:25]]), but his son Ahab surpasses even this.

"And as if it had been a light thing for him to walk in the sins of Jeroboam the son of Nebat, he took for his wife Jezebel the daughter of Ethbaal king of the Sidonians, and went and served Baal and worshiped him. He erected an altar for Baal in the house of Baal, which he built in Samaria. And Ahab made an Asherah. Ahab did more to provoke the LORD, the God of Israel, to anger than all the kings of Israel who were before him" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 16#1 Kings 16 . 31|1 Kings 16:31-33]]).

Ahab's sin is not merely continuing Jeroboam's calf worship but adding Baal worship—the worship of a foreign god. He builds a temple for Baal in the capital city. His wife Jezebel actively persecutes Yahweh's prophets, cutting them off ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 18#1 Kings 18 . 4|1 Kings 18:4]]), while supporting 450 prophets of Baal and 400 prophets of Asherah ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 18#1 Kings 18 . 19|1 Kings 18:19]]).

When Ahab covets Naboth's vineyard, and Naboth refuses to sell his ancestral inheritance, Jezebel arranges false testimony accusing Naboth of cursing God and the king. Naboth is stoned to death, and Ahab seizes the vineyard ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 21#1 Kings 21 . 1|1 Kings 21:1-16]]).

Elijah pronounces judgment: "Have you killed and also taken possession?... In the place where dogs licked up the blood of Naboth shall dogs lick your own blood" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 21#1 Kings 21 . 19|1 Kings 21:19]]). Concerning Jezebel: "The dogs shall eat Jezebel within the walls of Jezreel" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 21#1 Kings 21 . 23|1 Kings 21:23]]).

The narrator's summary is comprehensive: "There was none who sold himself to do what was evil in the sight of the LORD like Ahab, whom Jezebel his wife incited. He acted very abominably in going after idols, as the Amorites had done, whom the LORD cast out before the people of Israel" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 21#1 Kings 21 . 25|1 Kings 21:25-26]]).

Ahab has become worse than the Canaanites whom Israel was supposed to destroy. The covenant people under covenant kingship have descended below the level of the pagan nations.

### The Northern Kingdom's Total Apostasy

The pattern continues through every northern king. Not one receives a positive evaluation. The litany is depressing:

- Nadab: "He did what was evil in the sight of the LORD and walked in the way of his father, and in his sin which he made Israel to sin" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 15#1 Kings 15 . 26|1 Kings 15:26]])
- Baasha: Same verdict ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 15#1 Kings 15 . 34|1 Kings 15:34]])
- Zimri, Omri, Ahab, Ahaziah, Jehoram, Jehu, Jehoahaz, Jehoash, Jeroboam II, Zechariah, Shallum, Menahem, Pekahiah, Pekah, Hoshea: All "did evil in the sight of the LORD"

Even Jehu, who executes judgment on Ahab's house and destroys Baal worship ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 10#2 Kings 10 . 28|2 Kings 10:28]]), maintains the golden calves: "But Jehu did not turn aside from the sins of Jeroboam the son of Nebat, which he made Israel to sin—that is, the golden calves that were in Bethel and in Dan" ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 10#2 Kings 10 . 29|2 Kings 10:29]]).

The Northern Kingdom's sin is systemic. The false worship instituted by Jeroboam becomes permanent. Every king maintains the calves, most add further idolatry, and none leads the nation back to covenant faithfulness.

When Samaria finally falls to Assyria in 722 BC, the theological explanation is comprehensive ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 17#2 Kings 17 . 7|2 Kings 17:7-23]]):

- They feared other gods and walked in the customs of the nations
- They built high places and set up pillars and Asherim
- They burned incense and served idols
- They made metal images and worshiped the host of heaven
- They burned their sons and daughters as offerings
- They used divination and omens
- They sold themselves to do evil

The conclusion: "Therefore the LORD was very angry with Israel and removed them out of his sight. None was left but the tribe of Judah only" ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 17#2 Kings 17 . 18|2 Kings 17:18]]).

Beale notes that the Northern Kingdom embodies corporate-Adam failure at the national level. Jeroboam's foundational apostasy is an Adamic choice—rejecting God's revealed worship for a system "devised from his own heart." Every subsequent king recapitulates this choice, hardening the pattern of rebellion. The kingdom born in apostasy dies in apostasy, never experiencing covenant faithfulness.

## The Southern Kingdom: Oscillating Between Sin and Repentance

### The Majority Pattern: Evil Kings

While the Southern Kingdom includes occasional godly reformers, the majority of Judah's kings "did evil in the sight of the LORD." Rehoboam, Solomon's son, oversees widespread idolatry: "For they also built for themselves high places and pillars and Asherim on every high hill and under every green tree, and there were also male cult prostitutes in the land. They did according to all the abominations of the nations that the LORD drove out before the people of Israel" ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 14#1 Kings 14 . 23|1 Kings 14:23-24]]).

Manasseh represents the nadir. His reign is characterized by comprehensive apostasy:

"He rebuilt the high places that Hezekiah his father had destroyed, and he erected altars for Baal and made an Asherah, as Ahab king of Israel had done, and worshiped all the host of heaven and served them. And he built altars in the house of the LORD, of which the LORD had said, 'In Jerusalem will I put my name.' And he built altars for all the host of heaven in the two courts of the house of the LORD. And he burned his son as an offering and used fortune-telling and omens and dealt with mediums and with necromancers. He did much evil in the sight of the LORD, provoking him to anger" ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 21#2 Kings 21 . 3|2 Kings 21:3-6]]).

Manasseh doesn't just tolerate idolatry; he brings it into the temple itself, erecting altars to false gods in the very courts consecrated to Yahweh's name. He sacrifices his own son—child sacrifice to Molech, the most horrific abomination. He practices divination, consulted mediums, and dealt with necromancers—all explicitly forbidden in the law.

The narrator adds: "Moreover, Manasseh shed very much innocent blood, till he had filled Jerusalem from one end to another, besides the sin that he made Judah to sin so that they did what was evil in the sight of the LORD" ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 21#2 Kings 21 . 16|2 Kings 21:16]]).

The verdict: "Because Manasseh king of Judah has committed these abominations and has done things more evil than all that the Amorites did, who were before him, and has made Judah also to sin with his idols, therefore thus says the LORD, the God of Israel: Behold, I am bringing upon Jerusalem and Judah such disaster that the ears of everyone who hears of it will tingle" ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 21#2 Kings 21 . 11|2 Kings 21:11-12]]).

Manasseh's sin is so great that it seals Judah's fate. Even Josiah's later reforms cannot avert the coming judgment: "Still the LORD did not turn from the burning of his great wrath, by which his anger was kindled against Judah, because of all the provocations with which Manasseh had provoked him" ([[Readable Bible/12 - 2 Kings/2 Kings 23#2 Kings 23 . 26|2 Kings 23:26]]).

### The Last Kings: Rejecting Jeremiah's Warnings

The final kings of Judah consistently reject the prophetic word through Jeremiah. Jehoiakim burns Jeremiah's scroll containing God's words ([[Readable Bible/24 - Jeremiah/Jeremiah 36#Jeremiah 36 . 23|Jeremiah 36:23]]), demonstrating contempt for divine revelation. Zedekiah, though he secretly consults Jeremiah, lacks the courage to obey his counsel and instead listens to his officials who advocate resistance against Babylon ([[Readable Bible/24 - Jeremiah/Jeremiah 38#Jeremiah 38 . 14|Jeremiah 38:14-28]]).

The pattern established in Deuteronomy 17—the king must read and obey God's word—is comprehensively violated. The kings reject the prophetic word, refuse to humble themselves, persist in idolatry, and lead the nation into exile.

## Theological Summary: Corporate Adam's Universal Failure

The sin of Israel's kings demonstrates that no son of Adam can fulfill the Adamic calling. Saul, chosen and anointed, fails through incomplete obedience and self-will. David, the man after God's heart, commits adultery and murder. Solomon, the wisest man, turns to idolatry. The Northern Kingdom institutionalizes apostasy from its foundation. The Southern Kingdom oscillates between reform and rebellion but trends inexorably toward exile.

Beale concludes: "Every king—even the best—recapitulates Adam's fall. They are given the garden-temple (the land and sanctuary), explicit commands (the law), and the promise of blessing for obedience. Yet they choose autonomy over dependence, forbidden fruit over covenant faithfulness, and bring death rather than life. The monarchy's failure demonstrates the universal human condition: we are all sons of Adam, inheriting his sinful nature and repeating his rebellion. No human king can save Adam's race because every human king is himself a fallen son of Adam."

Kline adds: "The kings' sin reveals the impossibility of establishing the eternal kingdom through fallen human agency. The Davidic covenant promises an eternal throne, but no temporal king can fulfill this because all are sinners. The very commission that defines kingship—perfect obedience, righteous judgment, temple-guarding, kingdom-expansion—becomes the standard by which all kings are condemned. This creates the necessity of the gospel: God Himself must become King, taking on human nature while retaining divine perfection, to be the sinless second Adam who can establish the eternal kingdom."

The sin of the monarchy is not the failure of God's plan but the necessary demonstration that redemption cannot come through human effort. It prepares for the announcement of the gospel: "The LORD has sought out a man after his own heart" ([[Readable Bible/09 - 1 Samuel/1 Samuel 13#1 Samuel 13 . 14|1 Samuel 13:14]])—and that man is not merely David but David's greater Son, Jesus Christ, who perfectly fulfills every aspect of the kingly commission and establishes the kingdom that will never end.
