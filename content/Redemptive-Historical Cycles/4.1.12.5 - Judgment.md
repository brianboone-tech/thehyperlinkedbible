# 4.1.12.5 - Judgment: The Judges Period and Ruth - Covenant Curses and Divine Discipline

## Introduction: The LORD's Anger Burned

The book of Judges is not only a record of Israel's sin but also a demonstration of God's covenant faithfulness in judgment. Throughout the period, Yahweh responds to Israel's apostasy with measured discipline, giving them into the hand of foreign oppressors. Yet even in judgment, God's grace is evident—He hears their cries, raises deliverers, and provides temporary relief.

The judgment theme in Judges and Ruth includes:
1. **Foreign oppression**: Six cycles of oppression by Mesopotamia, Moab, Canaan, Midian, Ammon, and Philistia
2. **Divine anger**: Yahweh's burning wrath in response to covenant violation
3. **Covenant curses fulfilled**: The warnings of Deuteronomy 28 come to pass
4. **Civil war**: Israel turns on itself, nearly exterminating Benjamin
5. **Death of judges**: Mortality reveals the inadequacy of human deliverers
6. **Grace amid judgment**: God repeatedly delivers despite Israel's unfaithfulness
7. **Ruth's story**: Personal judgment (exile, death) followed by redemption and restoration

G.K. Beale's corporate Adam framework helps explain why judgment is necessary: Israel, as the new humanity created through the Exodus, is called to be holy image-bearers of Yahweh. When they violate this calling through idolatry, God must act to preserve His holiness and the integrity of the covenant. The judgment is not arbitrary wrath but covenant discipline designed to restore Israel to faithfulness.

Meredith Kline's intrusion eschatology explains the tension of judgment in Judges: the oppressors represent the present evil age intruding into Israel's experience, demonstrating that the final Sabbath-rest has not yet arrived. The judges provide temporary intrusions of eschatological deliverance, but the persistence of oppression reveals that the ultimate judgment on evil and the final deliverance of God's people await the coming of the Messianic Judge-King.

The book of Ruth shows that individual judgment (Elimelech's family dying in Moab) can be reversed through kinsman-redemption, anticipating the greater redemption that will come through Jesus Christ, who bears the judgment we deserve and gives us the righteousness we lack.

## The Cyclical Pattern: Yahweh's Anger and Oppression

The theological structure of judgment in Judges is established in [[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 2#Judges 2 . 14|Judges 2:14-15]]:

> **"So the anger of the LORD was kindled against Israel, and he gave them over to plunderers, who plundered them. And he sold them into the hand of their surrounding enemies, so that they could no longer withstand their enemies. Whenever they marched out, the hand of the LORD was against them for harm, as the LORD had warned, and as the LORD had sworn to them. And they were in terrible distress."**

This passage establishes several key truths about judgment in the Judges period:

### 1. The Anger of the LORD

**"The anger of the LORD was kindled against Israel"**—this is covenant wrath. God's anger is not capricious or arbitrary; it is the righteous response of a holy God to covenant violation. Israel has abandoned Yahweh for Baals and Ashtaroth ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 2#Judges 2 . 11|Judges 2:11-13]]), and His jealousy (covenant exclusivity) is provoked.

The phrase **"anger of the LORD"** (Hebrew *ap YHWH*) appears throughout the Old Testament as the divine response to idolatry and rebellion:
- At the golden calf: **"The anger of the LORD burned hot"** ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 32#Exodus 32 . 10|Exodus 32:10]])
- In the wilderness: **"The anger of the LORD burned against Moses"** ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 4#Exodus 4 . 14|Exodus 4:14]])
- At Kadesh-barnea: **"The LORD's anger was kindled"** ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 32#Numbers 32 . 13|Numbers 32:13]])

God's anger is not an emotion out of control but a deliberate judicial response to sin. It is the outworking of His holiness in the face of covenant treachery.

Beale observes that God's anger echoes His response to Adam's sin in Eden. When Adam violated the one command, God pronounced judgment: exile from the garden, toil in work, pain in childbirth, and eventual death ([[Readable Bible/01 - Genesis/Genesis 3#Genesis 3 . 16|Genesis 3:16-19]]). Similarly, when Israel violates the covenant, God pronounces judgment: oppression, plundering, and distress. The pattern is consistent—sin against the Holy One brings judgment.

### 2. Given Over to Plunderers

**"He gave them over to plunderers, who plundered them"**—this is judicial abandonment. God removes His protective presence and allows enemies to oppress Israel. The language of "giving over" appears throughout Scripture as a form of divine judgment:
- Romans 1: God **"gave them up"** to impurity, dishonorable passions, and a debased mind ([[Readable Bible/45 - Romans/Romans 1#Romans 1 . 24|Romans 1:24, 26, 28]])
- Hosea: **"Ephraim is joined to idols; let him alone"** ([[Readable Bible/28 - Hosea/Hosea 4#Hosea 4 . 17|Hosea 4:17]])

When God gives people over, He withdraws restraining grace and allows the natural consequences of sin to take their course. Israel wanted to be like the nations, serving their gods—so God allows them to experience the oppression that comes from rejecting His protection.

### 3. Sold into the Hand of Enemies

**"He sold them into the hand of their surrounding enemies"**—this is covenant curse language. [[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 28#Deuteronomy 28 . 68|Deuteronomy 28:68]] warns: **"The LORD will bring you back in ships to Egypt... and there you shall offer yourselves for sale to your enemies as male and female slaves, but there will be no buyer."** The threat of being sold into slavery is the reversal of the Exodus: Israel, whom Yahweh redeemed from slavery, will become slaves again if they violate the covenant.

The verb "sold" (Hebrew *makar*) emphasizes that this is judicial transaction. God is not passively allowing Israel's defeat—He is actively handing them over to oppressors as covenant discipline.

### 4. The Hand of the LORD Was Against Them

**"Whenever they marched out, the hand of the LORD was against them for harm"**—this is the reversal of God's presence. Throughout the conquest, **"the hand of the LORD"** was with Israel for victory ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 4#Joshua 4 . 24|Joshua 4:24]]). Now, that same hand is against them for harm.

The phrase **"as the LORD had warned, and as the LORD had sworn to them"** emphasizes that this judgment is not a surprise. It is the outworking of covenant curses explicitly detailed in Deuteronomy 28-30. God warned what would happen if Israel abandoned Him, and now those warnings are fulfilled.

Kline's intrusion theology explains the tension: Israel is in the land (conquest achieved) but not at rest (oppression continues). This reveals that possession of the land does not automatically guarantee Sabbath-rest. Only obedience to the covenant brings rest; disobedience brings oppression. The judges provide temporary intrusions of rest, but the final Sabbath-rest requires the ultimate Deliverer who will establish righteousness forever.

## The Six Cycles of Oppression and Deliverance

The book of Judges records six major cycles of oppression and deliverance, each demonstrating the pattern of covenant judgment and divine grace:

### Cycle 1: Mesopotamia (8 Years) - Othniel

**Judgment**: **"The people of Israel did what was evil in the sight of the LORD. They forgot the LORD their God and served the Baals and the Asheroth. Therefore the anger of the LORD was kindled against Israel, and he sold them into the hand of Cushan-rishathaim king of Mesopotamia. And the people of Israel served Cushan-rishathaim eight years."** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 3#Judges 3 . 7|Judges 3:7-8]])

**Deliverance**: **"But when the people of Israel cried out to the LORD, the LORD raised up a deliverer for the people of Israel, who saved them, Othniel the son of Kenaz, Caleb's younger brother."** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 3#Judges 3 . 9|Judges 3:9]])

**Rest**: **"So the land had rest forty years."** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 3#Judges 3 . 11|Judges 3:11]])

The first cycle establishes the pattern: sin → judgment (oppression) → cry for help → deliverance → rest. The oppression lasts 8 years, the rest lasts 40 years (one generation). But when Othniel dies, the cycle begins again.

Beale notes that the 8 years of oppression is relatively short compared to later cycles, suggesting that early in the Judges period, Israel's sin is less entrenched and God's discipline is less severe. However, as the cycles progress, the oppression lengthens, revealing the escalating seriousness of Israel's apostasy.

### Cycle 2: Moab (18 Years) - Ehud

**Judgment**: **"And the people of Israel again did what was evil in the sight of the LORD, and the LORD strengthened Eglon the king of Moab against Israel, because they had done what was evil in the sight of the LORD. He gathered to himself the Ammonites and the Amalekites, and went and defeated Israel. And they took possession of the city of palms. And the people of Israel served Eglon the king of Moab eighteen years."** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 3#Judges 3 . 12|Judges 3:12-14]])

**Deliverance**: **"Then the people of Israel cried out to the LORD, and the LORD raised up for them a deliverer, Ehud, the son of Gera, the Benjaminite, a left-handed man."** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 3#Judges 3 . 15|Judges 3:15]])

**Rest**: **"So Moab was subdued that day under the hand of Israel. And the land had rest for eighty years."** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 3#Judges 3 . 30|Judges 3:30]])

The second cycle intensifies: the oppression lasts 18 years (more than double the first cycle), indicating that Israel's sin is worsening. Yet God's grace is also greater: the land has rest for 80 years (two generations), the longest period of rest in the book.

The phrase **"the LORD strengthened Eglon the king of Moab against Israel"** emphasizes divine agency. God is not merely allowing oppression—He is actively empowering Israel's enemies as covenant discipline. This is the outworking of [[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 28#Deuteronomy 28 . 25|Deuteronomy 28:25]]: **"The LORD will cause you to be defeated before your enemies. You shall go out one way against them and flee seven ways before them."**

### Cycle 3: Canaan (20 Years) - Deborah and Barak

**Judgment**: **"And the people of Israel again did what was evil in the sight of the LORD after Ehud died. And the LORD sold them into the hand of Jabin king of Canaan, who reigned in Hazor. The commander of his army was Sisera, who lived in Harosheth-hagoyim. Then the people of Israel cried out to the LORD for help, for he had 900 chariots of iron and he oppressed the people of Israel cruelly for twenty years."** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 4#Judges 4 . 1|Judges 4:1-3]])

**Deliverance**: Deborah and Barak defeat Sisera; Jael kills Sisera ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 4#Judges 4 . 14|Judges 4:14-24]])

**Rest**: **"And the land had rest for forty years."** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 5#Judges 5 . 31|Judges 5:31]])

The third cycle continues the escalation: the oppression lasts 20 years, and the oppression is described as **"cruel"** (Hebrew *chazaq*—strong, violent, harsh). Jabin's 900 chariots of iron represent overwhelming military superiority. Israel is powerless against such technology.

Yet God intervenes. The LORD routs Sisera and his chariots ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 4#Judges 4 . 15|Judges 4:15]]), and Jael, a Kenite woman, kills Sisera with a tent peg ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 4#Judges 4 . 21|Judges 4:21]]). The deliverance is entirely God's doing—Israel's weakness highlights God's power.

Kline observes that the iron chariots represent the present evil age's military might, which seems invincible from a human perspective. But God's intrusion into history defeats the seemingly undefeatable. This prefigures the ultimate victory of Christ over the powers of darkness ([[Readable Bible/51 - Colossians/Colossians 2#Colossians 2 . 15|Colossians 2:15]]).

### Cycle 4: Midian (7 Years) - Gideon

**Judgment**: **"The people of Israel did what was evil in the sight of the LORD, and the LORD gave them into the hand of Midian seven years. And the hand of Midian overpowered Israel, and because of Midian the people of Israel made for themselves the dens that are in the mountains and the caves and the strongholds. For whenever the Israelites planted crops, the Midianites and the Amalekites and the people of the East would come up against them. They would encamp against them and devour the produce of the land, as far as Gaza, and leave no sustenance in Israel and no sheep or ox or donkey. For they would come up with their livestock and their tents; they would come like locusts in number—both they and their camels could not be counted—so that they laid waste the land as they came in. And Israel was brought very low because of Midian."** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 6#Judges 6 . 1|Judges 6:1-6]])

**Deliverance**: Gideon, empowered by the Spirit, defeats the Midianites with 300 men ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 7#Judges 7 . 7|Judges 7:7-25]])

**Rest**: **"So Midian was subdued before the people of Israel, and they raised their heads no more. And the land had rest forty years in the days of Gideon."** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 8#Judges 8 . 28|Judges 8:28]])

The fourth cycle is characterized by total devastation. The Midianites, Amalekites, and peoples of the East invade like locusts, devouring crops and livestock, leaving **"no sustenance in Israel"** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 6#Judges 6 . 4|Judges 6:4]]). This is the fulfillment of Deuteronomy 28:33: **"A nation that you have not known shall eat up the fruit of your ground and of all your labors, and you shall be only oppressed and crushed continually."**

Israel is **"brought very low"**—reduced to hiding in caves and strongholds. The judgment is not merely military defeat but economic and social collapse. Israel cannot survive if their crops are constantly destroyed.

Yet God delivers them through Gideon, reducing Israel's army from 32,000 to 300 to ensure that the glory goes to God alone ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 7#Judges 7 . 2|Judges 7:2]]). The victory is miraculous, demonstrating that deliverance is by grace, not by human strength.

Beale notes that the Midianite oppression echoes the plagues on Egypt: just as locusts devoured Egypt's crops ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 10#Exodus 10 . 15|Exodus 10:15]]), so the Midianites devour Israel's crops. The corporate Adam (Israel) is now experiencing the judgment that once fell on their enemies. This reversal reveals the seriousness of covenant violation.

### Cycle 5: Ammon (18 Years) - Jephthah

**Judgment**: **"The people of Israel again did what was evil in the sight of the LORD and served the Baals and the Ashtaroth, the gods of Syria, the gods of Sidon, the gods of Moab, the gods of the Ammonites, and the gods of the Philistines. And they forsook the LORD and did not serve him. So the anger of the LORD was kindled against Israel, and he sold them into the hand of the Philistines and into the hand of the Ammonites, and they crushed and oppressed the people of Israel that year. For eighteen years they oppressed all the people of Israel who were beyond the Jordan in the land of the Amorites, which is in Gilead."** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 10#Judges 10 . 6|Judges 10:6-8]])

**Deliverance**: Jephthah defeats the Ammonites ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 11#Judges 11 . 32|Judges 11:32-33]])

**Rest**: No explicit "rest" is recorded, suggesting the deliverance is incomplete or short-lived.

The fifth cycle marks a significant escalation in sin: Israel now serves **seven different foreign pantheons**—Baals, Ashtaroth, gods of Syria, Sidon, Moab, Ammon, and Philistia. This is wholesale apostasy. The text emphasizes: **"And they forsook the LORD and did not serve him"** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 10#Judges 10 . 6|Judges 10:6]])—total abandonment of Yahweh.

God's response is correspondingly severe: He sells them into the hand of both the Philistines and the Ammonites, a dual oppression lasting 18 years. The verb **"crushed"** (Hebrew *rats*) suggests violent, brutal oppression.

Remarkably, when Israel cries out, God initially refuses to deliver them:

> **"And the LORD said to the people of Israel, 'Did I not save you from the Egyptians and from the Amorites, from the Ammonites and from the Philistines? The Sidonians also, and the Amalekites and the Maonites oppressed you, and you cried out to me, and I saved you out of their hand. Yet you have forsaken me and served other gods; therefore I will save you no more. Go and cry out to the gods whom you have chosen; let them save you in the time of your distress.'"** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 10#Judges 10 . 11|Judges 10:11-14]])

This is a stunning pronouncement: **"I will save you no more."** God is not obligated to deliver a persistently rebellious people. He has delivered them repeatedly, and they have repeatedly returned to idolatry. There is a limit to presuming on divine grace.

Yet when Israel puts away their foreign gods and serves Yahweh ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 10#Judges 10 . 16|Judges 10:16]]), the text says: **"And he became impatient over the misery of Israel"** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 10#Judges 10 . 16|Judges 10:16]], ESV margin: "his soul was shortened over"). God's compassion overcomes His anger, and He raises Jephthah to deliver them.

Kline notes that this cycle reveals the tension between divine justice and divine mercy. God has every right to abandon Israel permanently, yet He cannot bear to see His people suffer. This tension is only resolved at the cross, where justice and mercy meet: God's wrath is poured out on His own Son, satisfying justice, so that mercy can be extended to rebels.

### Cycle 6: Philistines (40 Years) - Samson

**Judgment**: **"And the people of Israel again did what was evil in the sight of the LORD, so the LORD gave them into the hand of the Philistines for forty years."** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 13#Judges 13 . 1|Judges 13:1]])

**Deliverance**: Samson **"began to save Israel from the hand of the Philistines"** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 13#Judges 13 . 5|Judges 13:5]]), but the deliverance is incomplete.

**Rest**: No rest is recorded; the Philistine threat continues into the time of Samuel and David.

The sixth cycle is the longest oppression: 40 years (an entire generation). The Philistines are the most persistent enemy in the Judges period, and their oppression extends into the early monarchy. Samson provides some relief but does not fully defeat them.

The judgment is severe: an entire generation grows up under Philistine domination. This is the consequence of persistent, unrepentant idolatry. The escalation from 8 years (cycle 1) to 40 years (cycle 6) reveals the progressive hardening of Israel's heart and the increasing severity of God's discipline.

Beale observes that by the end of Judges, the cycle has broken down. There is no clear deliverance, no rest, no restoration. The book ends in chaos (Judges 17-21), suggesting that the cyclical pattern of judges cannot solve Israel's fundamental problem. A permanent solution—a king who will reign in righteousness forever—is needed.

## Covenant Curses Fulfilled: Deuteronomy 28 in Action

The judgments in Judges are not arbitrary—they are the outworking of covenant curses explicitly detailed in Deuteronomy 28-30.

### Deuteronomy 28:15-68: The Warnings

Moses warned Israel: **"But if you will not obey the voice of the LORD your God or be careful to do all his commandments and his statutes that I command you today, then all these curses shall come upon you and overtake you"** ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 28#Deuteronomy 28 . 15|Deuteronomy 28:15]]).

The curses include:
- **Military defeat**: **"The LORD will cause you to be defeated before your enemies"** ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 28#Deuteronomy 28 . 25|Deuteronomy 28:25]]) → Fulfilled in all six cycles
- **Economic devastation**: **"You shall betroth a wife, but another man shall ravish her. You shall build a house, but you shall not dwell in it. You shall plant a vineyard, but you shall not enjoy its fruit"** ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 28#Deuteronomy 28 . 30|Deuteronomy 28:30]]) → Fulfilled in Midianite raids ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 6#Judges 6 . 3|Judges 6:3-4]])
- **Oppression**: **"You shall be only oppressed and robbed continually"** ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 28#Deuteronomy 28 . 29|Deuteronomy 28:29]]) → Fulfilled throughout Judges
- **Foreign rule**: **"The sojourner who is among you shall rise higher and higher above you, and you shall come down lower and lower"** ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 28#Deuteronomy 28 . 43|Deuteronomy 28:43]]) → Fulfilled as Canaanites dominate Israel
- **Servitude**: **"You shall serve your enemies whom the LORD will send against you, in hunger and thirst, in nakedness, and lacking everything"** ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 28#Deuteronomy 28 . 48|Deuteronomy 28:48]]) → Fulfilled repeatedly

The key warning is in [[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 28#Deuteronomy 28 . 47|Deuteronomy 28:47-48]]: **"Because you did not serve the LORD your God with joyfulness and gladness of heart, because of the abundance of all things, therefore you shall serve your enemies whom the LORD will send against you."**

Israel refused to serve Yahweh in freedom and abundance, so God causes them to serve foreign oppressors in bondage and poverty. The punishment fits the crime.

### Deuteronomy 30:1-3: Hope Beyond Judgment

Yet Moses also promises restoration: **"And when all these things come upon you, the blessing and the curse, which I have set before you, and you call them to mind among all the nations where the LORD your God has driven you, and return to the LORD your God, you and your children, and obey his voice in all that I command you today, with all your heart and with all your soul, then the LORD your God will restore your fortunes and have mercy on you"** ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 30#Deuteronomy 30 . 1|Deuteronomy 30:1-3]]).

This promise is partially fulfilled in each Judges cycle: Israel cries out, God delivers. But the full restoration—where Israel permanently obeys with all their heart and soul—awaits the new covenant ([[Readable Bible/24 - Jeremiah/Jeremiah 31#Jeremiah 31 . 31|Jeremiah 31:31-34]]) and the coming of the Messiah.

Kline's intrusion theology explains the tension: the judges provide temporary restorations that prefigure the ultimate restoration. But because the judges cannot transform hearts or establish eternal righteousness, the restoration is always temporary. The final restoration requires the eschatological King who will write the Law on hearts and pour out the Spirit.

## Civil War: Israel Judging Itself

The most tragic judgment in the book of Judges is not foreign oppression but civil war—Israel turning on itself.

### The Gibeah Atrocity

After the rape and murder of the Levite's concubine in Gibeah ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 19#Judges 19 . 25|Judges 19:25-28]]), the tribes of Israel assemble and demand that Benjamin hand over the guilty men ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 20#Judges 20 . 12|Judges 20:12-13]]). Benjamin refuses, and civil war erupts.

### The War's Toll

The casualties are staggering:
- **First battle**: Israel loses 22,000 men ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 20#Judges 20 . 21|Judges 20:21]])
- **Second battle**: Israel loses 18,000 men ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 20#Judges 20 . 25|Judges 20:25]])
- **Third battle**: Benjamin loses 25,000 men ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 20#Judges 20 . 35|Judges 20:35]])
- **Total**: At least 65,000 Israelites killed in fratricidal warfare

Benjamin is nearly exterminated—only 600 men survive ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 20#Judges 20 . 47|Judges 20:47]]). The judgment is self-inflicted: Israel is destroying itself.

### The Tragic "Solution"

After the war, Israel realizes they have nearly destroyed one of their own tribes. Because they swore not to give their daughters to Benjamin ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 21#Judges 21 . 1|Judges 21:1]]), they devise two horrific solutions:
1. **Massacre Jabesh-gilead**: Kill all men and married women, take 400 virgins for Benjamin ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 21#Judges 21 . 10|Judges 21:10-12]])
2. **Abduct virgins from Shiloh**: Have Benjaminites kidnap women during a festival ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 21#Judges 21 . 20|Judges 21:20-23]])

The result: **more violence, more sin, more judgment**. Israel's attempt to solve the problem created by sin only leads to further sin. The cycle is vicious and seemingly unbreakable.

The book ends with the chilling refrain: **"In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did what was right in his own eyes"** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 21#Judges 21 . 25|Judges 21:25]]). The diagnosis is clear: without righteous leadership, Israel descends into chaos. The solution is implicit: Israel needs a king.

Beale notes that this civil war reveals the ultimate consequences of corporate Adam failure. Just as humanity descended into violence before the Flood ([[Readable Bible/01 - Genesis/Genesis 6#Genesis 6 . 11|Genesis 6:11-13]]), so Israel descends into fratricidal violence. The need for a new humanity, redeemed and renewed, is overwhelming.

## The Death of Judges: Mortality as Judgment

A subtle but significant form of judgment in the book is the repeated refrain: **"Then [judge] died."**

- Othniel: **"Then Othniel the son of Kenaz died"** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 3#Judges 3 . 11|Judges 3:11]])
- Ehud: **"And when Ehud died"** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 4#Judges 4 . 1|Judges 4:1]])
- Gideon: **"As soon as Gideon died"** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 8#Judges 8 . 33|Judges 8:33]])
- Samson: **"So the dead whom he killed at his death"** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 16#Judges 16 . 30|Judges 16:30]])

The mortality of the judges reveals their inadequacy. They provide temporary deliverance, but as soon as they die, Israel returns to apostasy. No human judge can establish permanent righteousness because all human judges are mortal.

This echoes the curse pronounced on Adam: **"You are dust, and to dust you shall return"** ([[Readable Bible/01 - Genesis/Genesis 3#Genesis 3 . 19|Genesis 3:19]]). Death is the ultimate judgment on sin, and even the deliverers themselves are under its power.

Kline's intrusion theology explains: the judges are intrusions of the eschatological Deliverer, but they are not the reality. They die because they are mortal. The ultimate Judge-King must either never die or die and rise again to reign forever. Only such a Deliverer can break the cycle of sin, death, and apostasy.

This anticipates [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 7#Hebrews 7 . 23|Hebrews 7:23-24]]: **"The former priests were many in number, because they were prevented by death from continuing in office, but he [Jesus] holds his priesthood permanently, because he continues forever."** Jesus is the Judge-King who does not die (or who dies and rises), establishing eternal deliverance.

## Grace Amid Judgment: God's Persistent Compassion

Despite the severity of judgment, the dominant theme of Judges is **grace amid judgment**. God repeatedly delivers Israel despite their repeated apostasy.

### The LORD Was Moved to Pity

[[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 2#Judges 2 . 18|Judges 2:18]] states: **"Whenever the LORD raised up judges for them, the LORD was with the judge, and he saved them from the hand of their enemies all the days of the judge. For the LORD was moved to pity by their groaning because of those who afflicted and oppressed them."**

The phrase **"moved to pity"** (Hebrew *nacham*) suggests deep compassion—God's heart is stirred by Israel's suffering. This is not sentimental emotion but covenant faithfulness. God remembers His promises to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 2#Exodus 2 . 24|Exodus 2:24]]), and He cannot abandon His people permanently.

### The LORD's Soul Was Shortened

In the fifth cycle, after initially refusing to deliver Israel, the text says: **"And he became impatient over the misery of Israel"** ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 10#Judges 10 . 16|Judges 10:16]], ESV margin: "his soul was shortened"). This is vivid anthropomorphic language: God cannot bear to watch His people suffer, even though they deserve judgment.

This tension between justice and mercy is resolved only at the cross. God's wrath against sin is real and must be satisfied. But His love for His people is also real and compels Him to provide a substitute. Jesus bears the judgment we deserve so that we can receive the mercy we do not deserve.

### The Pattern: Cry Out → God Raises a Deliverer

Throughout Judges, the pattern is consistent: **Israel cries out → God raises a deliverer → Israel is saved**. The cry for help is often accompanied by no evidence of genuine repentance (except in cycle 5, where they put away foreign gods - [[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 10#Judges 10 . 16|Judges 10:16]]). Yet God delivers them anyway.

This reveals that deliverance in Judges is by **grace, not merit**. Israel does not earn deliverance—they receive it as a gift. The judges do not succeed by their own strength—the Spirit empowers them. The victories are not achieved by military might—God gives the enemies into their hand.

Beale observes that this grace anticipates the gospel: salvation is by grace through faith, not by works ([[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 2#Ephesians 2 . 8|Ephesians 2:8-9]]). Just as Israel cried out in distress and God saved them, so sinners cry out to Jesus and He saves them. The pattern is consistent throughout Scripture: divine grace responding to human need.

## Ruth: Personal Judgment and Redemption

The book of Ruth provides a personal example of judgment and redemption within the broader Judges period.

### Judgment: Famine, Exile, Death

The story begins with covenant curse: **"In the days when the judges ruled there was a famine in the land"** ([[Readable Bible/08 - Ruth/Ruth 1#Ruth 1 . 1|Ruth 1:1]]). Famine is one of the covenant curses for disobedience ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 28#Deuteronomy 28 . 23|Deuteronomy 28:23-24]]).

Elimelech takes his family to Moab—a form of exile. In Moab, Elimelech dies, then his two sons die ([[Readable Bible/08 - Ruth/Ruth 1#Ruth 1 . 3|Ruth 1:3-5]]). Naomi is left barren, widowed, and destitute. She interprets this as divine judgment: **"The LORD has testified against me and the Almighty has brought calamity upon me"** ([[Readable Bible/08 - Ruth/Ruth 1#Ruth 1 . 21|Ruth 1:21]]).

### Redemption: Kinsman-Redeemer, Return, Restoration

Yet God provides redemption through Boaz, the kinsman-redeemer. Boaz redeems Naomi's land and marries Ruth ([[Readable Bible/08 - Ruth/Ruth 4#Ruth 4 . 9|Ruth 4:9-10]]). Ruth bears a son, Obed, who becomes the grandfather of King David ([[Readable Bible/08 - Ruth/Ruth 4#Ruth 4 . 17|Ruth 4:17-22]]).

Naomi, who left Bethlehem full and returned empty ([[Readable Bible/08 - Ruth/Ruth 1#Ruth 1 . 21|Ruth 1:21]]), is now full again. The women of Bethlehem celebrate: **"Blessed be the LORD, who has not left you this day without a redeemer, and may his name be renowned in Israel!"** ([[Readable Bible/08 - Ruth/Ruth 4#Ruth 4 . 14|Ruth 4:14-15]])

### Theological Significance

Ruth's story demonstrates that personal judgment (famine, death) can be reversed through kinsman-redemption. This anticipates the gospel, where Jesus the ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer bears the judgment we deserve (death) and gives us the life we do not deserve (resurrection).

Kline notes that Boaz's redemption is an intrusion of eschatological grace into Naomi and Ruth's experience. It is a foretaste of the ultimate redemption that will come through Jesus Christ, who will redeem not just land and person but all creation ([[Readable Bible/45 - Romans/Romans 8#Romans 8 . 21|Romans 8:21-23]]).

Beale observes that Ruth's inclusion in the Messianic line ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 1#Matthew 1 . 5|Matthew 1:5]]) reveals God's grace extending beyond ethnic Israel to include Gentiles. The Moabite woman, who by law should have been excluded from the assembly ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 23#Deuteronomy 23 . 3|Deuteronomy 23:3]]), becomes the great-grandmother of David and an ancestor of Jesus. This is grace triumphing over judgment.

## Theological Synthesis: Judgment Revealing the Need for the King

The judgment theme in Judges serves multiple theological functions:

### 1. Demonstrates God's Covenant Faithfulness

God's judgment is not arbitrary—it is the outworking of covenant curses explicitly detailed in Deuteronomy 28-30. God warned what would happen if Israel disobeyed, and He faithfully fulfills His warnings. This demonstrates that God is trustworthy—both His promises and His threats are reliable.

### 2. Reveals the Seriousness of Sin

The escalating severity of judgment (from 8 years to 40 years of oppression) reveals the escalating seriousness of sin. Idolatry is not a minor infraction—it is covenant adultery that provokes God's jealous wrath. The civil war and near-extermination of Benjamin reveal that unchecked sin leads to self-destruction.

### 3. Exposes Human Inability

The repeated cycle—sin, judgment, deliverance, rest, sin again—demonstrates that human judges cannot break the power of sin. The mortality of the judges, the moral compromise of the judges, and the eventual breakdown of the cycle (no rest after Samson) all reveal that a permanent solution requires divine intervention.

### 4. Displays God's Grace

Despite Israel's persistent apostasy, God repeatedly delivers them. His compassion overcomes His anger. He cannot bear to see His people suffer, even when they deserve it. This grace anticipates the cross, where God's love for sinners compels Him to send His Son to bear the judgment we deserve.

### 5. Anticipates the Coming King

The refrain "there was no king in Israel" ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 17#Judges 17 . 6|Judges 17:6]]; [[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 21#Judges 21 . 25|Judges 21:25]]) points forward to the need for a righteous king who will establish justice and righteousness. The judges provide temporary relief, but Israel needs a permanent King who will reign forever.

### Beale's Corporate Adam Framework

Beale's reading emphasizes that the judgment in Judges parallels the judgment on Adam in Genesis 3:
- **Adam sinned** → expelled from Eden, subjected to toil and death
- **Israel sinned** → oppressed by enemies, subjected to foreign rule

Just as Adam's sin brought curse on all humanity, so Israel's corporate sin brings curse on the nation. The need for a new Adam—one who will obey perfectly and reverse the curse—is overwhelming.

### Kline's Intrusion Eschatology

Kline's framework explains the tension of judgment in Judges:
- **Already**: Israel is in the land, possessing the inheritance
- **Not yet**: Israel is not at rest, constantly oppressed

The judges provide temporary intrusions of eschatological deliverance, but the final Sabbath-rest requires the ultimate Judge-King who will:
- Definitively defeat all enemies ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 15#1 Corinthians 15 . 25|1 Corinthians 15:25-26]])
- Establish eternal righteousness ([[Readable Bible/27 - Daniel/Daniel 9#Daniel 9 . 24|Daniel 9:24]])
- Bring permanent rest ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 4#Hebrews 4 . 9|Hebrews 4:9]])

## Christological Fulfillment: Jesus Bears the Judgment We Deserve

The judgment theme in Judges points forward to Jesus Christ in multiple ways:

### 1. Jesus Bears the Covenant Curses

Where Israel deserved the covenant curses of Deuteronomy 28, Jesus bears those curses in our place. **"Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us—for it is written, 'Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree'"** ([[Readable Bible/48 - Galatians/Galatians 3#Galatians 3 . 13|Galatians 3:13]]).

The oppression, defeat, and suffering that Israel experienced as covenant judgment are borne by Jesus at the cross. He experiences the full weight of divine wrath so that we can experience divine mercy.

### 2. Jesus Breaks the Cycle of Judgment

The cyclical pattern in Judges—sin, judgment, cry out, deliverance, sin again—is finally broken in Jesus. He delivers us not temporarily but eternally ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 9#Hebrews 9 . 12|Hebrews 9:12]]). His death and resurrection inaugurate the age to come, where the cycle of sin and judgment is replaced by eternal righteousness and rest.

### 3. Jesus Is the Judge Who Does Not Die

The mortality of the judges reveals their inadequacy. Jesus is the Judge who dies but rises again, conquering death and reigning forever ([[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 1#Revelation 1 . 18|Revelation 1:18]]). **"He continues forever"** ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 7#Hebrews 7 . 24|Hebrews 7:24]]), establishing eternal deliverance.

### 4. Jesus Displays Grace Amid Judgment

The pattern of God being "moved to pity" by Israel's groaning ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 2#Judges 2 . 18|Judges 2:18]]) anticipates Jesus' compassion for sinners. **"When he saw the crowds, he had compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd"** ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 9#Matthew 9 . 36|Matthew 9:36]]).

Jesus does not merely deliver from oppression—He delivers from sin, death, and Satan. And He does so by bearing the judgment Himself.

### 5. Jesus Is the Ultimate Kinsman-Redeemer

Boaz redeemed Ruth from poverty and barrenness. Jesus redeems us from sin and death. **"In him we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses"** ([[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 1#Ephesians 1 . 7|Ephesians 1:7]]).

Just as Boaz bore the cost of redemption, Jesus bears the cost—His own life. Just as Boaz raised up seed for the dead, Jesus raises us to new life through His resurrection ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 15#1 Corinthians 15 . 20|1 Corinthians 15:20-22]]).

## Conclusion: Judgment Calling for the Eternal Judge

The judgment theme in Judges is overwhelming: foreign oppression, civil war, economic devastation, moral collapse, and the mortality of the deliverers themselves. Yet even in judgment, God's grace shines through—He repeatedly delivers His people despite their persistent apostasy.

The cyclical pattern reveals that human judges cannot establish permanent righteousness. The escalating severity of judgment reveals that sin, left unchecked, leads to self-destruction. The refrain "there was no king in Israel" points to the solution: a righteous King who will reign forever.

G.K. Beale's corporate Adam framework shows that Israel's judgment parallels Adam's judgment: both fail to guard the sanctuary, both are expelled/oppressed, both need a new Adam to reverse the curse. Meredith Kline's intrusion eschatology explains that the judges provide temporary foretastes of eschatological deliverance, but the final Sabbath-rest requires the ultimate Judge-King.

Ruth's story demonstrates that personal judgment can be reversed through kinsman-redemption, anticipating the greater redemption that comes through Jesus Christ, who bears the judgment we deserve and gives us the righteousness we lack.

The judgment of Judges cries out for Christ—the Judge who bears covenant curses, the King who reigns forever, the Kinsman-Redeemer who pays the ultimate price, and the Deliverer who breaks the cycle of sin and death once and for all.
