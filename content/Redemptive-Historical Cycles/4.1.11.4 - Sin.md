# 4.1.11.4 - Sin

## The Pattern Repeats: Adam in Canaan

The conquest of Canaan begins with spectacular divine intervention and obedient faith. The Jordan parts, Jericho's walls collapse, and Israel experiences miraculous victory. But it does not take long for the dark shadow of sin to fall across the narrative. Just as Adam's commission in Eden was disrupted by rebellion, so Israel's commission in Canaan is compromised by disobedience. <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">The pattern of creation-fall repeats in redemptive history</span>, demonstrating that neither the first Adam nor the corporate Adam (Israel) can fulfill God's purposes apart from divine grace.

The sin phase in Joshua is not a minor subplot but a central theological component of the narrative. It demonstrates several crucial realities: (1) covenant blessing is conditioned on covenant faithfulness, (2) corporate solidarity means individual sin affects the whole community, (3) incomplete obedience leads to long-term spiritual disaster, and (4) even partial success under Joshua points to the need for a greater Joshua who will perfectly and finally accomplish what Israel could not.

From G.K. Beale's perspective, Israel's sin in the conquest represents the <span style="color: #16a085; font-weight: bold;">failure of the corporate Adam</span> to resist temptation and maintain covenant faithfulness. Just as the first Adam was tested in the garden and failed, so Israel is tested in the land and fails. The serpent who corrupted Eden continues his work in Canaan, tempting Israel to compromise their commission through covetousness, deception, and complacency.

From Meredith Kline's framework, Israel's sin demonstrates that even during an <span style="color: #1f618d; font-weight: bold;">intrusion of eschatological blessing</span> (entering the land, experiencing holy war victories), human depravity persists. The conquest provides salvation from external enemies but not from the internal enemy of the sinful heart. This points forward to the need for new covenant transformation that will address not merely external behavior but internal disposition.

## Achan's Sin: Taking the Devoted Things

The first and most dramatic sin in the conquest narrative occurs at Jericho, and it involves <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">violation of the *herem*</span>—the command to devote all spoils to God. Before the conquest of Jericho, Joshua had explicitly commanded: <span style="color: #1f618d; font-weight: bold;">"But you, keep yourselves from the things devoted to destruction, lest when you have devoted them you take any of the devoted things and make the camp of Israel a thing for destruction and bring trouble upon it. But all silver and gold, and every vessel of bronze and iron, are holy to the LORD; they shall go into the treasury of the LORD"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 6#Joshua 6 . 18|Joshua 6:18-19]]).

The command was clear and comprehensive. Jericho was to be utterly destroyed as <span style="color: #d68910; font-weight: bold;">the firstfruits offering of the conquest</span>. Just as Israel gave the firstfruits of their agricultural harvest to God, so the first city conquered would be entirely devoted to Yahweh. No plunder was to be taken for personal use. All valuable metals would go into the tabernacle treasury. All living things would be killed. The city would be burned as a whole burnt offering.

But <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">Achan son of Carmi violated the *herem*</span>: <span style="color: #8e44ad; font-weight: bold;">"But the people of Israel broke faith in regard to the devoted things, for Achan the son of Carmi, son of Zabdi, son of Zerah, of the tribe of Judah, took some of the devoted things. And the anger of the LORD burned against the people of Israel"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 7#Joshua 7 . 1|Joshua 7:1]]).

Several elements of this text are theologically significant. First, the text says <span style="color: #16a085; font-weight: bold;">"the people of Israel broke faith"</span>—even though only one man sinned, the entire nation is held responsible. This reflects the principle of <span style="color: #1f618d; font-weight: bold;">corporate solidarity</span> in covenant relationship. Israel is not merely a collection of individuals but a unified body. When one member sins, the whole body is defiled.

Second, the text notes that <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">"the anger of the LORD burned against the people of Israel"</span>—not merely against Achan. God's covenant anger is directed at the corporate entity, not just the individual perpetrator. This explains why Israel will experience military defeat at Ai before Achan's sin is discovered and purged.

Third, the genealogy is significant: <span style="color: #d68910; font-weight: bold;">"Achan son of Carmi, son of Zabdi, son of Zerah, of the tribe of Judah."</span> Judah was the royal tribe from which Messiah would come ([[Readable Bible/01 - Genesis/Genesis 49#Genesis 49 . 10|Genesis 49:10]]). That the first major sin in the conquest comes from Judah underscores that no tribe is exempt from the corrupting power of sin. Even the most privileged position does not guarantee faithfulness.

Later, when Achan's sin is exposed through the casting of lots, he confesses: <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">"Truly I have sinned against the LORD God of Israel, and this is what I did: when I saw among the spoil a beautiful cloak from Shinar, and 200 shekels of silver, and a bar of gold weighing 50 shekels, then I coveted them and took them. And see, they are hidden in the earth inside my tent, with the silver underneath"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 7#Joshua 7 . 20|Joshua 7:20-21]]).

Achan's confession reveals the <span style="color: #8e44ad; font-weight: bold;">anatomy of temptation and sin</span>. The progression is classic: he saw, he coveted, he took, he hid. This echoes Eve's temptation in Genesis 3: "When the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was a delight to the eyes, and that the tree was to be desired to make one wise, she took of its fruit and ate" ([[Readable Bible/01 - Genesis/Genesis 3#Genesis 3 . 6|Genesis 3:6]]). The same pattern appears in David's sin with Bathsheba ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 11#2 Samuel 11 . 2|2 Samuel 11:2-4]]) and in James' analysis of temptation ([[Readable Bible/59 - James/James 1#James 1 . 14|James 1:14-15]]).

The specific items Achan took are revealing. The <span style="color: #16a085; font-weight: bold;">cloak from Shinar</span> (Babylon) represents exotic luxury from the East—a status symbol that would elevate Achan's standing. The silver and gold represent wealth and economic security. Achan's sin was motivated by <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">covetousness</span>—the desire for material possessions beyond what God had allotted. He wanted more than God's provision; he wanted the forbidden fruit.

The hiding of the stolen items "in the earth inside my tent, with the silver underneath" shows the nature of secret sin. Achan believed he could conceal his transgression from human eyes. But <span style="color: #1f618d; font-weight: bold;">hidden sin cannot be hidden from God</span>, and it defiles the community even when unknown to others. The silver buried under Achan's tent is like the corpse under the Israelite's tent in Numbers 19—it makes the entire dwelling unclean.

Achan's sin is not merely personal but <span style="color: #d68910; font-weight: bold;">paradigmatic</span>. It demonstrates that even after miraculous deliverance (Jordan crossing) and spectacular victory (Jericho), the human heart remains prone to rebellion. It also demonstrates that covenant blessing can be forfeited through disobedience. Most significantly, it reveals the utter incompatibility of holiness and sin—God cannot bless His people while they harbor unconfessed transgression.

## The Defeat at Ai: Covenant Consequences

The immediate consequence of Achan's hidden sin is <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">military defeat at Ai</span>. Ai was a small city, insignificant compared to Jericho. The spies who surveyed it reported: <span style="color: #1f618d; font-weight: bold;">"Let not all the people go up, but let about two or three thousand men go up and attack Ai. Do not make the whole people toil up there, for they are few"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 7#Joshua 7 . 3|Joshua 7:3]]).

This assessment was militarily sound. Ai was weak; Israel was strong. A small force should easily conquer the city. But the spies' confidence reflects the beginning of <span style="color: #8e44ad; font-weight: bold;">presumption</span>—the assumption that Israel's strength, rather than God's presence, guarantees victory. They do not inquire of the LORD before attacking Ai, repeating the mistake that will later occur with the Gibeonites ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 9#Joshua 9 . 14|Joshua 9:14]]).

The result is humiliating defeat: <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">"So about three thousand men went up there from the people. And they fled before the men of Ai, and the men of Ai killed about thirty-six men of them and chased them before the gate as far as Shebarim and struck them at the descent. And the hearts of the people melted and became as water"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 7#Joshua 7 . 4|Joshua 7:4-5]]).

The defeat is both material and psychological. Materially, thirty-six Israelites are killed—not a massive number, but shocking after the easy victory at Jericho. Psychologically, <span style="color: #16a085; font-weight: bold;">"the hearts of the people melted and became as water"</span>—the very fear that had gripped the Canaanites ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 2#Joshua 2 . 11|Joshua 2:11]]) now grips Israel. The reversal is complete: Israel, who should be conquering in God's strength, is fleeing in terror.

Joshua's response to the defeat reveals both his humanity and his covenant loyalty. He tears his clothes, falls on his face before the ark, and cries out to God: <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">"Alas, O Lord GOD, why have you brought this people over the Jordan at all, to give us into the hands of the Amorites, to destroy us? Would that we had been content to dwell beyond the Jordan! O Lord, what can I say, when Israel has turned their backs before their enemies! For the Canaanites and all the inhabitants of the land will hear of it and will surround us and cut off our name from the earth. And what will you do for your great name?"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 7#Joshua 7 . 7|Joshua 7:7-9]]).

Joshua's lament echoes Israel's complaints in the wilderness ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 14#Exodus 14 . 11|Exodus 14:11-12]]; [[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 14#Numbers 14 . 2|Numbers 14:2-3]]). In his distress, he questions whether the conquest was wise, wishing they had stayed east of the Jordan. But unlike the wilderness generation, Joshua's concern is ultimately <span style="color: #1f618d; font-weight: bold;">for God's reputation</span>: "What will you do for your great name?" He recognizes that Israel's defeat reflects badly not merely on Israel but on Yahweh, whose power the Canaanites will question.

God's response is sharp and instructive: <span style="color: #d68910; font-weight: bold;">"Get up! Why have you fallen on your face? Israel has sinned; they have transgressed my covenant that I commanded them; they have taken some of the devoted things; they have stolen and lied and put them among their own belongings. Therefore the people of Israel cannot stand before their enemies. They turn their backs before their enemies, because they have become devoted for destruction. I will be with you no more, unless you destroy the devoted things from among you"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 7#Joshua 7 . 10|Joshua 7:10-12]]).

Several elements here are crucial for understanding covenant theology. First, God commands Joshua to <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">"Get up!"</span>—prayer is not the appropriate response when unconfessed sin remains in the camp. The time for intercession will come after judgment is executed. For now, action is required: the sin must be identified and purged.

Second, God describes the sin in corporate terms: <span style="color: #8e44ad; font-weight: bold;">"Israel has sinned... they have transgressed... they have taken... they have stolen and lied."</span> Although only Achan committed the actual theft, God speaks as if the entire nation is guilty. This is not rhetorical exaggeration but covenant reality. In corporate solidarity, one member's sin defiles the whole body.

Third, God explains the connection between sin and defeat: <span style="color: #16a085; font-weight: bold;">"They turn their backs before their enemies, because they have become devoted for destruction."</span> By taking devoted things, Israel has become devoted things—the *herem* has transferred from Jericho to Israel. They are now under the same judgment they were supposed to execute on the Canaanites. Sin reverses roles: the holy become profane, the conquerors become conquered.

Fourth, God issues an ultimatum: <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">"I will be with you no more, unless you destroy the devoted things from among you."</span> The promise of divine presence ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 1#Joshua 1 . 5|Joshua 1:5]]) is conditional on covenant faithfulness. God will not dwell with a defiled people. Either the sin must be removed, or God will withdraw His presence. Without His presence, Israel cannot conquer—thus the defeat at Ai.

The defeat at Ai demonstrates a fundamental principle of biblical theology: <span style="color: #1f618d; font-weight: bold;">covenant blessing is inseparable from covenant obedience</span>. God's promises are sure, but they are mediated through the covenant relationship. When that relationship is ruptured by sin, the blessings are suspended until the sin is dealt with. Israel learns through painful defeat what they should have known through Torah instruction: <span style="color: #d68910; font-weight: bold;">you cannot harbor sin and experience God's blessing simultaneously</span>.

## The Valley of Achor: Corporate Judgment

The identification and punishment of Achan is one of the most severe judgment scenes in Scripture. Through the casting of lots—a divinely guided process ([[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 16#Proverbs 16 . 33|Proverbs 16:33]])—the guilty party is narrowed down from all Israel, to the tribe of Judah, to the clan of Zerah, to the household of Zabdi, to Achan himself ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 7#Joshua 7 . 16|Joshua 7:16-18]]).

When Achan is identified, Joshua says to him: <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">"My son, give glory to the LORD God of Israel and give praise to him. And tell me now what you have done; do not hide it from me"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 7#Joshua 7 . 19|Joshua 7:19]]). The call to "give glory to God" by confession is poignant. Even in judgment, there is opportunity for repentance and truth-telling. Achan's confession, while coming too late to avoid judgment, at least acknowledges the truth.

The stolen items are retrieved from Achan's tent, confirming his guilt. Then comes the execution: <span style="color: #8e44ad; font-weight: bold;">"And Joshua and all Israel with him took Achan the son of Zerah, and the silver and the cloak and the bar of gold, and his sons and daughters and his oxen and donkeys and sheep and his tent and all that he had. And they brought them up to the Valley of Achor. And Joshua said, 'Why did you bring trouble on us? The LORD brings trouble on you today.' And all Israel stoned him with stones. They burned them with fire and stoned them with stones"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 7#Joshua 7 . 24|Joshua 7:24-25]]).

The severity of this judgment raises difficult questions. Why are <span style="color: #16a085; font-weight: bold;">Achan's family members killed along with him</span>? The text does not explicitly say they participated in the sin, though the fact that the stolen items were hidden in the tent suggests they likely knew. But even if they were innocent of direct participation, they are executed as part of the household.

Several factors help explain this severity. First, this is an <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">intrusion of eschatological judgment</span> (per Kline's framework). In the final judgment, sin will be punished comprehensively, and all who are identified with the wicked will share their fate. The judgment on Achan's household is a temporal picture of that ultimate reality.

Second, this is early in the conquest, and <span style="color: #1f618d; font-weight: bold;">a dramatic object lesson is needed</span>. The entire nation must understand the absolute seriousness of violating the *herem*. The comprehensive destruction of Achan's household makes unmistakably clear that covenant violation has severe consequences.

Third, the destruction of Achan's family and possessions completes the *herem* that was violated. Achan took devoted things for himself; now he and his household become devoted things. The <span style="color: #d68910; font-weight: bold;">contagion of sin is purged through fire and stones</span>, the same elements used in purification rituals ([[Readable Bible/04 - Numbers/Numbers 31#Numbers 31 . 23|Numbers 31:23]]).

The place is named <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">the Valley of Achor</span>—"Achor" meaning "trouble." Joshua's words explain: "Why did you bring trouble (*achar*) on us? The LORD brings trouble (*achar*) on you today." The wordplay emphasizes that Achan's sin brought trouble on Israel, and God's judgment brings trouble on Achan. Sin always brings trouble—first to the sinner, then to the community.

Yet even this place of judgment will become, in prophetic perspective, a <span style="color: #16a085; font-weight: bold;">door of hope</span>. Hosea prophesies: "And there I will give her her vineyards and make the Valley of Achor a door of hope" ([[Readable Bible/28 - Hosea/Hosea 2#Hosea 2 . 15|Hosea 2:15]]). The place where sin was judged will become the place where restoration begins. This anticipates the gospel pattern: through the judgment executed on Christ at the cross (the ultimate Valley of Achor), the door of hope opens for sinners to enter into covenant blessing.

After Achan's execution, "the LORD turned from his burning anger" ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 7#Joshua 7 . 26|Joshua 7:26]]). The covenant relationship is restored, divine presence returns, and Israel is ready to conquer Ai. The purging of sin precedes the reception of blessing—a pattern that will recur throughout Scripture.

## The Gibeonite Deception: Faithless Pragmatism

The second major sin in Joshua involves not covetousness but <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">pragmatic compromise</span>. After Israel's victories at Jericho and Ai become known, the Canaanite kings form military coalitions to resist the conquest ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 9#Joshua 9 . 1|Joshua 9:1-2]]). But the Gibeonites adopt a different strategy: <span style="color: #8e44ad; font-weight: bold;">deception</span>.

The Gibeonites were Hivites ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 9#Joshua 9 . 7|Joshua 9:7]]), one of the seven Canaanite nations marked for destruction ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 7#Deuteronomy 7 . 1|Deuteronomy 7:1]]). They knew they were under *herem*. They also knew that Israel had been commanded not to make treaties with inhabitants of the land ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 7#Deuteronomy 7 . 2|Deuteronomy 7:2]]). But they had heard that Israel could make treaties with distant nations ([[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 20#Deuteronomy 20 . 15|Deuteronomy 20:15]]). So they hatched a plan to <span style="color: #16a085; font-weight: bold;">disguise themselves as distant travelers</span>.

The Gibeonites came to Joshua at Gilgal wearing worn-out clothes and sandals, carrying dry and crumbly bread, and claiming: <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">"We have come from a distant country, so now make a covenant with us"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 9#Joshua 9 . 6|Joshua 9:6]]). They concocted an elaborate backstory: "This our bread was still warm when we took it from our houses as our food for the journey on the day we set out to come to you, but now, behold, it is dry and crumbly. These wineskins were new when we filled them, and behold, they have burst. And these garments and sandals of ours are worn out from the very long journey" ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 9#Joshua 9 . 12|Joshua 9:12-13]]).

The deception was clever and thorough. The Gibeonites understood Israelite theology well enough to appeal to Yahweh's fame: <span style="color: #1f618d; font-weight: bold;">"Your servants have come because of the name of the LORD your God. For we have heard a report of him, and all that he did in Egypt, and all that he did to the two kings of the Amorites who were beyond the Jordan"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 9#Joshua 9 . 9|Joshua 9:9-10]]). They framed their request as homage to Yahweh, making it religiously appealing.

Israel's response reveals the fatal flaw: <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">"So the men took some of their provisions, but did not ask counsel from the LORD"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 9#Joshua 9 . 14|Joshua 9:14]]). This single sentence explains the entire fiasco. The Israelite leaders examined the physical evidence (worn clothes, dry bread, cracked wineskins) and reached what seemed like a reasonable conclusion. But they <span style="color: #d68910; font-weight: bold;">failed to seek divine guidance</span>. They relied on empirical observation and human reasoning rather than consulting Yahweh through the Urim and Thummim or prophetic word.

The result: <span style="color: #8e44ad; font-weight: bold;">"And Joshua made peace with them and made a covenant with them, to let them live, and the leaders of the congregation swore to them"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 9#Joshua 9 . 15|Joshua 9:15]]). The covenant was solemnly sworn in Yahweh's name, making it binding. Three days later, Israel discovered the truth: <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">"At the end of three days after they had made a covenant with them, they heard that they were their neighbors and that they lived among them"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 9#Joshua 9 . 16|Joshua 9:16]]).

The people of Israel wanted to attack the Gibeonite cities immediately, but the leaders refused: <span style="color: #16a085; font-weight: bold;">"We have sworn to them by the LORD, the God of Israel, and now we may not touch them"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 9#Joshua 9 . 19|Joshua 9:19]]). Even though the covenant had been obtained through deception, it had been sworn in Yahweh's name and therefore remained binding. To violate the oath would be to profane God's name.

This creates a profound dilemma. Israel is <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">trapped between two divine commands</span>: the command to destroy all Canaanites, and the command to keep oaths made in God's name. By failing to seek God's counsel before making the treaty, Israel created a situation where perfect obedience became impossible. They must either break the *herem* command or break their oath—both involve covenant violation.

The leaders choose to honor the oath but modify its terms: <span style="color: #1f618d; font-weight: bold;">"Let them live, but let them be cutters of wood and drawers of water for all the congregation"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 9#Joshua 9 . 21|Joshua 9:21]]). The Gibeonites are relegated to servile status, performing menial tasks for the tabernacle. This is presented as fulfilling Noah's curse on Canaan: "a servant of servants shall he be to his brothers" ([[Readable Bible/01 - Genesis/Genesis 9#Genesis 9 . 25|Genesis 9:25]]).

The Gibeonite deception demonstrates several forms of sin. First, the Gibeonites' sin of <span style="color: #d68910; font-weight: bold;">deception</span>—they lied to preserve their lives, putting self-preservation above truth. Second, Israel's sin of <span style="color: #8e44ad; font-weight: bold;">prayerlessness</span>—they failed to seek God's counsel in a critical decision. Third, Israel's sin of <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">pragmatism</span>—they relied on empirical evidence and human judgment rather than divine revelation.

The long-term consequences prove severe. The Gibeonites remain in the land as a permanent Canaanite presence, creating ongoing temptation toward syncretism. Centuries later, King Saul will attempt to exterminate the Gibeonites in misguided zeal, bringing covenant curse on Israel ([[Readable Bible/10 - 2 Samuel/2 Samuel 21#2 Samuel 21 . 1|2 Samuel 21:1-2]]). The failure to fully obey God's command in Joshua 9 creates problems that persist for generations.

The Gibeonite incident also illustrates a tragic irony: <span style="color: #16a085; font-weight: bold;">the very pragmatism intended to avoid problems creates worse problems</span>. Israel thought they were making a wise diplomatic move, securing allies from a distant nation. Instead, they violated God's command and created a permanent compromise in the land. Human wisdom apart from divine guidance leads to disaster.

## Incomplete Conquest: The Sin of Compromise

The most pervasive sin in Joshua is not a single dramatic violation like Achan's theft or a specific failure like the Gibeonite treaty, but the <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">pattern of incomplete conquest</span>. Throughout the book, there are repeated notices that Israel failed to fully drive out the inhabitants of the land.

The pattern begins with specific geographical failures: <span style="color: #1f618d; font-weight: bold;">"But the people of Judah could not drive out the Jebusites, the inhabitants of Jerusalem, so the Jebusites dwell with the people of Judah at Jerusalem to this day"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 15#Joshua 15 . 63|Joshua 15:63]]). Similarly, Ephraim "did not drive out the Canaanites who lived in Gezer, so the Canaanites have lived in the midst of Ephraim to this day but have been made to do forced labor" ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 16#Joshua 16 . 10|Joshua 16:10]]).

Manasseh's failure is even more extensive: <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">"Yet the people of Manasseh could not take possession of those cities, but the Canaanites persisted in dwelling in that land. Now when the people of Israel grew strong, they put the Canaanites to forced labor, but did not utterly drive them out"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 17#Joshua 17 . 12|Joshua 17:12-13]]).

The phrase <span style="color: #d68910; font-weight: bold;">"to this day"</span> punctuates these notices, indicating that the incomplete conquest had lasting consequences extending to the time of the book's composition. The Canaanites who should have been destroyed remain as ongoing presence, creating persistent temptation toward idolatry.

The reason for the incomplete conquest is complex. Sometimes the text says the Israelites <span style="color: #8e44ad; font-weight: bold;">"could not"</span> drive out the inhabitants, suggesting military difficulty—particularly with nations possessing iron chariots ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 17#Joshua 17 . 16|Joshua 17:16]]). Other times it says they <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">"did not"</span> drive them out, suggesting lack of will or effort. The combination indicates both external obstacles and internal failures.

But from a theological perspective, the incomplete conquest represents <span style="color: #16a085; font-weight: bold;">failure to trust God's promise and power</span>. God had explicitly promised to drive out the nations ([[Readable Bible/02 - Exodus/Exodus 23#Exodus 23 . 28|Exodus 23:28-30]]; [[Readable Bible/05 - Deuteronomy/Deuteronomy 7#Deuteronomy 7 . 22|Deuteronomy 7:22-24]]). The military obstacles—iron chariots, fortified cities, numerous enemies—were real, but God had promised to overcome them. Israel's failure to complete the conquest reflects <span style="color: #1f618d; font-weight: bold;">lack of faith</span>, not lack of divine power.

Moreover, Israel adopted a <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">pragmatic compromise</span>: instead of destroying the Canaanites, they subjected them to forced labor. This seemed economically advantageous—free labor force to build cities and infrastructure. But it violated God's explicit command and set the stage for spiritual disaster. The very nations they spared would become "thorns in your sides" and "snares to you" ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 23#Joshua 23 . 13|Joshua 23:13]]).

Joshua himself recognizes the incomplete nature of the conquest. Near the end of his life, God tells him: <span style="color: #d68910; font-weight: bold;">"You are old and advanced in years, and there remains yet very much land to possess"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 13#Joshua 13 . 1|Joshua 13:1]]). God then lists the unconquered territories—the Philistine cities, regions of Lebanon, areas in the north and south. Despite spectacular victories, <span style="color: #8e44ad; font-weight: bold;">the conquest remains unfinished</span>.

In his farewell addresses, Joshua warns Israel about the consequences of this incomplete obedience: <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">"Know for certain that the LORD your God will no longer drive out these nations before you, but they shall be a snare and a trap for you, a whip on your sides and thorns in your eyes, until you perish from off this good land that the LORD your God has given you"</span> ([[Readable Bible/06 - Joshua/Joshua 23#Joshua 23 . 13|Joshua 23:13]]).

The prophecy proves tragically accurate. The book of Judges opens with continued attempts to complete the conquest, all ending in failure ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 1#Judges 1 . 19|Judges 1:19-36]]). The nations that remain in the land become instruments of divine testing ([[Readable Bible/07 - Judges/Judges 2#Judges 2 . 22|Judges 2:22-23]]) and eventually sources of temptation that lead Israel into apostasy. The incomplete conquest under Joshua necessitates the entire tragic cycle of Judges: oppression, crying out, deliverance, apostasy, oppression.

The incomplete conquest demonstrates that <span style="color: #16a085; font-weight: bold;">partial obedience is disobedience</span>. God did not command Israel to mostly destroy the Canaanites or to drive them out eventually or to subject them to forced labor. He commanded complete destruction. Anything less than full obedience constitutes covenant violation, even when it seems pragmatically wise or militarily necessary.

## Theological Analysis: Beale's Corporate Adam Failure

From G.K. Beale's perspective, the sin phase in Joshua represents the <span style="color: #1f618d; font-weight: bold;">failure of corporate Adam to fulfill the creation mandate</span>. Just as the first Adam succumbed to temptation in the garden, taking forbidden fruit, so Israel succumbs to temptation in the land, taking forbidden plunder (Achan) and making forbidden treaties (Gibeonites).

The parallel between Achan and Adam is particularly striking. Both saw something desirable, both coveted what God had forbidden, both took what was prohibited, both tried to hide their sin. <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">Achan is Adam in Canaan</span>—repeating the pattern of temptation, sin, and judgment that characterized the fall.

But the sin in Joshua is not merely individual but corporate. Israel as a whole fails to complete the conquest, compromising with the nations they should have destroyed. This corporate failure demonstrates that <span style="color: #d68910; font-weight: bold;">the corporate Adam is no more successful than the individual Adam</span> at maintaining covenant faithfulness. Even with Torah, tabernacle, priesthood, and divine presence, Israel cannot perfectly obey.

This failure points forward to the need for the <span style="color: #8e44ad; font-weight: bold;">ultimate Adam</span> who will succeed where all previous Adams failed. Jesus Christ will face temptation in the wilderness and overcome ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 4#Matthew 4 . 1|Matthew 4:1-11]]). He will perfectly obey Torah ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 8#John 8 . 29|John 8:29]]). He will complete the conquest by destroying sin, death, and Satan ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 2#Hebrews 2 . 14|Hebrews 2:14]]). He will purify a people for Himself, creating a new humanity that finally fulfills the Adamic commission ([[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 2#Ephesians 2 . 15|Ephesians 2:15]]).

The sin phase in Joshua thus functions typologically, demonstrating both the <span style="color: #16a085; font-weight: bold;">continuity and escalation of the Adamic pattern</span>. The pattern continues: creation, commission, sin, judgment, redemption. But the pattern also escalates, moving toward ultimate fulfillment in Christ. Each iteration of the pattern reveals more clearly the depth of human sin and the necessity of divine intervention.

## Theological Analysis: Kline's Persistence of Sin in the Intrusion

Meredith Kline's framework helps explain why sin persists even during the conquest, which represents an intrusion of eschatological blessing. The conquest is holy war—a breaking through of the age to come into the present age. Israel experiences miraculous victories, divine presence, and covenant blessing that anticipate the final kingdom. Yet <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">even during this intrusion of blessing, human depravity remains active</span>.

This demonstrates that <span style="color: #1f618d; font-weight: bold;">external circumstances do not transform the human heart</span>. Achan witnessed the Jordan crossing, participated in the conquest of Jericho, heard the explicit command against taking devoted things. Yet he still sinned. Israel experienced God's power at Jericho, yet they still failed to seek His counsel regarding the Gibeonites. The tribes received land allotments, yet they still compromised by allowing Canaanites to remain.

The persistence of sin during the intrusion underscores the need for <span style="color: #d68910; font-weight: bold;">internal transformation, not merely external blessing</span>. This points forward to the new covenant promise: "I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you. And I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh" ([[Readable Bible/26 - Ezekiel/Ezekiel 36#Ezekiel 36 . 26|Ezekiel 36:26]]). Only when God transforms the heart through regeneration can His people truly and consistently obey.

Kline's framework also illuminates why God's response to Israel's sin is so severe. During an intrusion event, when the age to come breaks into the present age, <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">the standards are heightened</span>. Sin that might be tolerated under common grace conditions cannot be tolerated during holy war. Achan's theft would be serious at any time, but during the conquest of Jericho—the firstfruits offering—it constitutes covenant treason requiring immediate and comprehensive judgment.

The incomplete conquest also fits Kline's framework. The failure to fully destroy the Canaanites represents a <span style="color: #8e44ad; font-weight: bold;">failure to fully execute the eschatological judgment</span> that the conquest represents. The intrusion is therefore partial—some judgment is executed (Jericho, Ai, southern and northern campaigns) but not complete judgment (many Canaanites remain). This partial intrusion points forward to the need for complete intrusion at Christ's return, when all enemies will be finally and fully destroyed ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 15#1 Corinthians 15 . 25|1 Corinthians 15:25-26]]).

## The Christological Solution

Every dimension of sin revealed in Joshua finds its ultimate solution in <span style="color: #16a085; font-weight: bold;">Jesus Christ</span>. Where Achan coveted and took forbidden things, Jesus resisted all temptation and remained perfectly obedient ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 4#Hebrews 4 . 15|Hebrews 4:15]]). Where Israel failed to seek God's counsel, Jesus constantly sought the Father's will ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 5#John 5 . 19|John 5:19]]). Where Israel incompletely destroyed God's enemies, Jesus will completely and finally destroy all evil ([[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 20#Revelation 20 . 14|Revelation 20:14-15]]).

Most significantly, <span style="color: #c0392b; font-weight: bold;">Jesus bore the judgment that Israel's sin deserved</span>. Achan was stoned and burned in the Valley of Achor for taking devoted things. Jesus was crucified on Golgotha for taking upon Himself the sin of the world. The judgment that fell on Achan typologically points to the judgment that fell on Christ—except that Christ was innocent, bearing the guilt of others.

Through Christ's death and resurrection, the <span style="color: #1f618d; font-weight: bold;">Valley of Achor becomes a door of hope</span> ([[Readable Bible/28 - Hosea/Hosea 2#Hosea 2 . 15|Hosea 2:15]]). The place of judgment becomes the place of restoration. Believers who were devoted to destruction because of sin are redeemed through Christ's devotion to death on their behalf. The gospel transforms the valley of trouble into the gateway of grace.

Moreover, Christ accomplishes the <span style="color: #d68910; font-weight: bold;">complete conquest</span> that Joshua could not. He destroys the works of the devil ([[Readable Bible/62 - 1 John/1 John 3#1 John 3 . 8|1 John 3:8]]), disarms the spiritual powers ([[Readable Bible/51 - Colossians/Colossians 2#Colossians 2 . 15|Colossians 2:15]]), and will ultimately make all things new ([[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 21#Revelation 21 . 5|Revelation 21:5]]). Where Joshua left much land unpossessed, Jesus will possess all creation, filling it with His glory. The incomplete conquest under Joshua points to the complete conquest under Jesus.

The sin phase in Joshua thus serves a crucial redemptive-historical purpose: <span style="color: #8e44ad; font-weight: bold;">it reveals the depth of human sin and the necessity of divine grace</span>. Israel's failure under Joshua makes clear that the law cannot save, that human effort cannot establish God's kingdom, that only Christ can accomplish what fallen humanity cannot. The way forward is not through better obedience to Torah but through faith in the one who perfectly obeyed on our behalf.
