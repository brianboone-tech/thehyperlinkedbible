# 4.1.15 - Intrusion - The Wisdom of Israel

## Overview

**Biblical Scope**: Job, [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 1#Psalm 1 . 1|Psalms]], [[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 1#Proverbs 1 . 1|Proverbs]], [[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 1#Ecclesiastes 1 . 1|Ecclesiastes]], [[Readable Bible/22 - Song of Solomon/Song of Solomon 1#Song of Solomon 1 . 1|Song of Solomon]]

The Wisdom Literature of Israel represents a unique intrusion of God's redemptive grace—revelation given not through historical narrative or prophetic lawsuit, but through **poetry, proverb, reflection, and song**. While other biblical genres focus on Israel's covenant history, the Wisdom Literature addresses universal human questions: How do we live in God's world? Why do the righteous suffer? What is the meaning of life under the sun? How should we relate to God and neighbor?

Yet this literature is far from secular philosophy. Wisdom in Israel begins with **the fear of the LORD** ([[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 1#Proverbs 1 . 7|Proverbs 1:7]]; [[Readable Bible/18 - Job/Job 28#Job 28 . 28|Job 28:28]]; [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 111#Psalm 111 . 10|Psalm 111:10]])—it is covenant wisdom rooted in knowing God. The five books that comprise Israel's wisdom corpus (Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Solomon) provide theological commentary on living faithfully in the world between Eden and New Creation, between promise and fulfillment.

**G.K. Beale's Framework**: The Wisdom Literature addresses Israel's **corporate Adamic vocation** from a different angle than law or prophecy. Where the Law prescribes covenant obedience and the Prophets prosecute covenant violation, Wisdom **trains the image-bearer** to exercise dominion rightly—to cultivate creation with skill, discernment, and reverence for God. Wisdom personified ([[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 8#Proverbs 8 . 1|Proverbs 8]]) anticipates Christ, the Wisdom of God ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 1#1 Corinthians 1 . 24|1 Corinthians 1:24, 30]]), who perfectly fulfills the Adamic commission.

**Meredith Kline's Intrusion Ethics**: Wisdom Literature navigates the **tension of the Common Grace period**—life under the curse yet sustained by God's mercy. Ecclesiastes exposes the vanity of life "under the sun" without eschatological hope. Psalms oscillate between lament (groaning under the curse) and praise (anticipating final redemption). Job questions God's justice in a world where the righteous suffer, pointing forward to the **Suffering Servant** who will resolve theodicy through substitutionary atonement.

The Wisdom Literature does not bring final resolution—it points forward to the One who is Wisdom incarnate, the righteous Sufferer, the ultimate Psalmist, the greater Solomon, and the true Bridegroom.

### The Pattern of Intrusion

The Wisdom Literature follows the fivefold pattern, demonstrating both the glory of creation wisdom and the impossibility of attaining perfect righteousness apart from grace:

**Chaos**: Humanity lives under the curse ([[Readable Bible/01 - Genesis/Genesis 3#Genesis 3 . 17|Genesis 3:17-19]])—toil, suffering, injustice, death. The righteous suffer (Job), the wicked prosper temporarily ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 73#Psalm 73 . 3|Psalm 73:3-12]]), life appears meaningless "under the sun" ([[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 1#Ecclesiastes 1 . 2|Ecclesiastes 1:2]]). Wisdom seeks to navigate chaos with skill and reverence.

**Creation**: God grants wisdom to His people—Job's integrity, David's psalms, Solomon's proverbs. Wisdom personified calls out in the streets ([[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 8#Proverbs 8 . 1|Proverbs 8:1-11]]), offering life to those who heed. The Psalms celebrate God's creative power ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 19#Psalm 19 . 1|Psalm 19:1-6]]; [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 104#Psalm 104 . 1|Psalm 104]]) and covenant faithfulness ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 103#Psalm 103 . 1|Psalm 103]]; [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 136#Psalm 136 . 1|Psalm 136]]).

**Commission**: The wise are called to **fear the LORD** ([[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 1#Proverbs 1 . 7|Proverbs 1:7]]), pursue righteousness, exercise dominion skillfully, worship God in truth, and live according to the created order. This is the Adamic commission expressed through wisdom categories.

**Sin**: Yet even the wisest fail. Job questions God's justice. David commits adultery and murder, crying out for cleansing ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 51#Psalm 51 . 1|Psalm 51]]). Solomon, wisest of all, ends in idolatry ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 11#1 Kings 11 . 4|1 Kings 11:4-8]]). The fool rejects wisdom ([[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 1#Proverbs 1 . 24|Proverbs 1:24-32]]), and even the wise cannot escape death's vanity ([[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 2#Ecclesiastes 2 . 15|Ecclesiastes 2:15-16]]).

**Judgment**: "All is vanity" ([[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 1#Ecclesiastes 1 . 2|Ecclesiastes 1:2]])—apart from God, wisdom cannot overcome death or guarantee justice. The wicked prosper temporarily, the righteous suffer, and "man goes to his eternal home" ([[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 12#Ecclesiastes 12 . 5|Ecclesiastes 12:5]]) under judgment. Wisdom points to the need for a **greater Wise Man** who will embody perfect wisdom and conquer death.

## Major Theological Themes

### 1. Job: The Righteous Sufferer and the Problem of Theodicy

The Book of Job addresses the most profound theological question: **Why do the righteous suffer?** Job is introduced as "blameless and upright, one who feared God and turned away from evil" ([[Readable Bible/18 - Job/Job 1#Job 1 . 1|Job 1:1]])—a model of covenant faithfulness. Yet God permits Satan to afflict him with catastrophic losses: wealth, children, health ([[Readable Bible/18 - Job/Job 1#Job 1 . 13|Job 1:13-2:10]]).

**The Debate**: Job's three friends (Eliphaz, Bildad, Zophar) represent traditional retribution theology—suffering is always punishment for sin ([[Readable Bible/18 - Job/Job 4#Job 4 . 7|Job 4:7-8]]; [[Readable Bible/18 - Job/Job 8#Job 8 . 3|Job 8:3-6]]). They urge Job to confess hidden sin. Yet Job maintains his integrity ([[Readable Bible/18 - Job/Job 27#Job 27 . 5|Job 27:5-6]]), demanding an audience with God to vindicate himself.

**God's Response**: When God finally speaks from the whirlwind ([[Readable Bible/18 - Job/Job 38#Job 38 . 1|Job 38-41]]), He does not explain Job's suffering. Instead, He reveals His transcendent wisdom through creation's complexity—"Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth?" ([[Readable Bible/18 - Job/Job 38#Job 38 . 4|Job 38:4]]). God's governance of creation (constellations, weather, animals) demonstrates that His wisdom infinitely exceeds human comprehension.

**Job's Submission**: Job responds in humble worship: "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you; therefore I despise myself, and repent in dust and ashes" ([[Readable Bible/18 - Job/Job 42#Job 42 . 5|Job 42:5-6]]). The answer to suffering is not explanation but **encounter with God**—trust in His inscrutable wisdom.

**Messianic Typology**: Job anticipates Christ as the **righteous Sufferer**:

- **Innocent suffering**: Job suffers though blameless; Christ suffers though sinless ([[Readable Bible/60 - 1 Peter/1 Peter 2#1 Peter 2 . 22|1 Peter 2:22]])
- **Rejected by friends**: Job's friends accuse him falsely; Christ's disciples abandon Him ([[Readable Bible/41 - Mark/Mark 14#Mark 14 . 50|Mark 14:50]])
- **Mediator longing**: Job cries for a mediator—"there is no arbiter between us" ([[Readable Bible/18 - Job/Job 9#Job 9 . 33|Job 9:33]])—Christ is that Mediator ([[Readable Bible/54 - 1 Timothy/1 Timothy 2#1 Timothy 2 . 5|1 Timothy 2:5]])
- **Resurrection hope**: "I know that my Redeemer lives... in my flesh I shall see God" ([[Readable Bible/18 - Job/Job 19#Job 19 . 25|Job 19:25-27]])—anticipating bodily resurrection through Christ

Job demonstrates that the **problem of evil** cannot be solved by human wisdom—it requires divine revelation and ultimately the cross, where God Himself bears the curse.

### 2. Psalms: The Hymnbook of the Second Temple and Messianic Pointer

The Psalms constitute Israel's **worship manual**, used in temple liturgy and personal devotion. Traditionally attributed largely to David (73 psalms bear his name), the Psalter spans Israel's history from Moses ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 90#Psalm 90 . 1|Psalm 90]]) to post-exilic return.

**Categories**:
- **Lament psalms** (e.g., [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 13#Psalm 13 . 1|Psalm 13]], [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 22#Psalm 22 . 1|Psalm 22]], [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 88#Psalm 88 . 1|Psalm 88]]): Cry out to God in distress, demonstrating honest prayer under suffering
- **Praise psalms** (e.g., [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 103#Psalm 103 . 1|Psalm 103]], [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 145#Psalm 145 . 1|Psalm 145-150]]): Celebrate God's character, works, and covenant faithfulness
- **Royal psalms** (e.g., [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 2#Psalm 2 . 1|Psalm 2]], [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 45#Psalm 45 . 1|Psalm 45]], [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 72#Psalm 72 . 1|Psalm 72]], [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 110#Psalm 110 . 1|Psalm 110]]): Focus on the Davidic king, pointing to Messiah
- **Wisdom psalms** (e.g., [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 1#Psalm 1 . 1|Psalm 1]], [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 37#Psalm 37 . 1|Psalm 37]], [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 73#Psalm 73 . 1|Psalm 73]]): Contrast the way of the righteous with the way of the wicked
- **Torah psalms** (e.g., [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 19#Psalm 19 . 1|Psalm 19]], [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 119#Psalm 119 . 1|Psalm 119]]): Delight in God's law
- **Creation psalms** (e.g., [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 8#Psalm 8 . 1|Psalm 8]], [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 19#Psalm 19 . 1|Psalm 19]], [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 104#Psalm 104 . 1|Psalm 104]]): Celebrate God's creative power
- **Historical psalms** (e.g., [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 78#Psalm 78 . 1|Psalm 78]], [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 105#Psalm 105 . 1|Psalm 105-106]], [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 136#Psalm 136 . 1|Psalm 136]]): Recount God's acts in Israel's history

**Messianic Psalms**: The New Testament identifies numerous psalms as Messianic prophecy:

**Psalm 2**: "You are my Son; today I have begotten you" ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 2#Psalm 2 . 7|Psalm 2:7]])—applied to Christ's sonship ([[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 13#Acts 13 . 33|Acts 13:33]]; [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 1#Hebrews 1 . 5|Hebrews 1:5]]; [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 5#Hebrews 5 . 5|5:5]])

**Psalm 22**: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 22#Psalm 22 . 1|Psalm 22:1]])—Jesus' cry on the cross ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 27#Matthew 27 . 46|Matthew 27:46]]). Details of crucifixion: hands and feet pierced ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 22#Psalm 22 . 16|Psalm 22:16]]), casting lots for garments ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 22#Psalm 22 . 18|Psalm 22:18]]), mocking ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 22#Psalm 22 . 7|Psalm 22:7-8]]).

**Psalm 45**: Royal wedding psalm—"Your throne, O God, is forever and ever" ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 45#Psalm 45 . 6|Psalm 45:6-7]])—applied to Christ in Hebrews 1:8-9.

**Psalm 72**: Prayer for the king—"May he have dominion from sea to sea" ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 72#Psalm 72 . 8|Psalm 72:8]])—fulfilled in Christ's universal reign.

**Psalm 110**: "The LORD says to my Lord: 'Sit at my right hand'" ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 110#Psalm 110 . 1|Psalm 110:1]])—the most quoted OT verse in the NT, affirming Christ's divine sonship and exaltation ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 22#Matthew 22 . 44|Matthew 22:44]]; [[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 2#Acts 2 . 34|Acts 2:34-35]]; [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 1#Hebrews 1 . 13|Hebrews 1:13]]). "You are a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek" ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 110#Psalm 110 . 4|Psalm 110:4]])—Christ's eternal priesthood ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 5#Hebrews 5 . 6|Hebrews 5:6]]; [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 7#Hebrews 7 . 1|7:1-28]]).

**Theological Significance**: The Psalms demonstrate the **already-not yet tension** of living under covenant. They groan under the curse (lament) yet anticipate redemption (praise). They celebrate the Davidic king yet long for the eternal King. They worship in the earthly temple yet point to the heavenly sanctuary. The Psalms train God's people to pray with honest emotion while trusting in God's covenant faithfulness.

### 3. Proverbs: Wisdom for Skillful Living and Christ as Wisdom

Proverbs presents wisdom as the **skill of living rightly** in God's created order. Primarily attributed to Solomon ([[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 1#Proverbs 1 . 1|Proverbs 1:1]]; [[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 10#Proverbs 10 . 1|10:1]]; [[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 25#Proverbs 25 . 1|25:1]]), the book compiles sayings that address **practical righteousness**—relationships, speech, work, marriage, parenting, business, justice.

**The Fear of the LORD**: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge; fools despise wisdom and instruction" ([[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 1#Proverbs 1 . 7|Proverbs 1:7]]). Wisdom is not secular pragmatism but **covenantal skill**—living according to God's design with reverence for His character.

**Two Paths**: Proverbs sets forth the **way of wisdom** vs. **the way of folly**:

- **Wisdom's way**: Leads to life, prosperity, honor, peace ([[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 3#Proverbs 3 . 13|Proverbs 3:13-18]]; [[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 4#Proverbs 4 . 10|Proverbs 4:10-13]])
- **Folly's way**: Leads to death, poverty, shame, destruction ([[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 5#Proverbs 5 . 3|Proverbs 5:3-14]]; [[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 7#Proverbs 7 . 6|Proverbs 7:6-27]])

This echoes the Adamic commission—obedience brings life in God's presence, disobedience brings death and exile.

**Wisdom Personified** ([[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 8#Proverbs 8 . 1|Proverbs 8]]):

Wisdom is personified as a woman who calls out publicly, offering life to all who heed her:

"The LORD possessed me at the beginning of his work, the first of his acts of old. Ages ago I was set up, at the first, before the beginning of the earth... When he established the heavens, I was there... Then I was beside him, like a master workman, and I was daily his delight, rejoicing before him always" ([[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 8#Proverbs 8 . 22|Proverbs 8:22-30]]).

This passage has profound **Christological implications**:

- **Preexistence**: Wisdom exists before creation, just as Christ is eternal ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 1#John 1 . 1|John 1:1]])
- **Agency in creation**: Wisdom is the master workman; Christ is the agent of creation ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 1#John 1 . 3|John 1:3]]; [[Readable Bible/51 - Colossians/Colossians 1#Colossians 1 . 16|Colossians 1:16]])
- **Delight of God**: Wisdom delights the Father; Christ is the beloved Son ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 3#Matthew 3 . 17|Matthew 3:17]])

Paul explicitly identifies Christ as **the wisdom of God**: "Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God" ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 1#1 Corinthians 1 . 24|1 Corinthians 1:24]]). "In whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" ([[Readable Bible/51 - Colossians/Colossians 2#Colossians 2 . 3|Colossians 2:3]]).

Where Proverbs offers wisdom for skillful living, Christ **embodies** perfect wisdom, enabling believers to walk in wisdom through union with Him ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 1#1 Corinthians 1 . 30|1 Corinthians 1:30]]).

**Solomon's Failure**: Despite authoring most of Proverbs, Solomon himself fails to heed wisdom—multiplying wives, turning to idolatry ([[Readable Bible/11 - 1 Kings/1 Kings 11#1 Kings 11 . 1|1 Kings 11:1-8]]), violating the very principles he taught. This demonstrates that **knowing wisdom is not enough**—only the **perfect Wise Man**, Jesus Christ, lives wisdom flawlessly and enables His people to do likewise through the Spirit.

### 4. Ecclesiastes: Vanity Under the Sun and the Need for a Greater Solomon

Ecclesiastes (traditionally attributed to Solomon, "the Preacher," [[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 1#Ecclesiastes 1 . 1|Ecclesiastes 1:1]]) explores life's meaning "under the sun"—the phrase appears 29 times, emphasizing earthly, temporal existence apart from eschatological hope.

**The Thesis**: "Vanity of vanities! All is vanity" ([[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 1#Ecclesiastes 1 . 2|Ecclesiastes 1:2]])—Hebrew *hebel*, meaning "vapor, breath, futility." Life under the curse is fleeting, frustrating, enigmatic.

**The Investigations**: The Preacher tests life's meaning through various pursuits:

- **Wisdom** ([[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 1#Ecclesiastes 1 . 12|Ecclesiastes 1:12-18]]): "In much wisdom is much vexation" (v. 18)—wisdom cannot prevent death
- **Pleasure** ([[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 2#Ecclesiastes 2 . 1|Ecclesiastes 2:1-11]]): "This also was vanity" (v. 1)—hedonism fails to satisfy
- **Toil** ([[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 2#Ecclesiastes 2 . 18|Ecclesiastes 2:18-23]]): "What has a man from all the toil... under the sun?" (v. 22)—work brings no lasting reward
- **Time** ([[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 3#Ecclesiastes 3 . 1|Ecclesiastes 3:1-15]]): "A time for everything"—yet God has "put eternity into man's heart" (v. 11), leaving restless longing
- **Injustice** ([[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 4#Ecclesiastes 4 . 1|Ecclesiastes 4:1-3]]): "The tears of the oppressed, and they had no one to comfort them"—injustice pervades the fallen world

**The Verdict**: Apart from God, life is meaningless—"All is vanity and a striving after wind" ([[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 2#Ecclesiastes 2 . 26|Ecclesiastes 2:26]]). The wise and fool both die ([[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 2#Ecclesiastes 2 . 16|Ecclesiastes 2:16]]), wealth is left to others ([[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 2#Ecclesiastes 2 . 18|Ecclesiastes 2:18]]), and death is humanity's certain end ([[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 12#Ecclesiastes 12 . 7|Ecclesiastes 12:7]]).

**The Conclusion**: "Fear God and keep his commandments, for this is the whole duty of man. For God will bring every deed into judgment" ([[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 12#Ecclesiastes 12 . 13|Ecclesiastes 12:13-14]]).

Ecclesiastes exposes the **futility of life under the curse** without resurrection hope. It demonstrates that the **Common Grace period**—life under the sun, between Fall and Consummation—cannot provide ultimate meaning. Only **eschatological hope** (resurrection, judgment, new creation) resolves Ecclesiastes' despair.

**Christological Resolution**: Jesus is the **greater Solomon** who:

- Possesses perfect wisdom yet does not end in folly
- Experiences life "under the sun" (incarnation) yet conquers death (resurrection)
- Provides meaning beyond the grave—"I am the resurrection and the life" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 11#John 11 . 25|John 11:25]])
- Judges every deed ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 25#Matthew 25 . 31|Matthew 25:31-46]]), vindicating the righteous and punishing the wicked
- Brings **new creation**, where vanity is abolished ([[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 21#Revelation 21 . 4|Revelation 21:4]])

Ecclesiastes drives us to Christ by exposing the emptiness of life without Him.

### 5. Song of Solomon: Covenant Love and Christ the Bridegroom

The Song of Solomon (also called Song of Songs) celebrates **marital love** through poetic dialogue between bride and groom. Its inclusion in Scripture affirms that **physical intimacy within covenant marriage** is God's good gift ([[Readable Bible/01 - Genesis/Genesis 2#Genesis 2 . 24|Genesis 2:24]]; [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 13#Hebrews 13 . 4|Hebrews 13:4]]).

**Literal Meaning**: The poem celebrates the beauty, delight, and exclusivity of marital love—"My beloved is mine, and I am his" ([[Readable Bible/22 - Song of Solomon/Song of Solomon 2#Song of Solomon 2 . 16|Song of Solomon 2:16]]). It reflects God's creational intent for marriage before the Fall corrupted it.

**Typological Meaning**: Throughout Scripture, **marriage is a covenant metaphor** for God's relationship with His people:

- **Hosea**: God's faithful love despite Israel's adultery ([[Readable Bible/28 - Hosea/Hosea 1#Hosea 1 . 1|Hosea 1-3]])
- **Jeremiah**: Israel as unfaithful bride ([[Readable Bible/24 - Jeremiah/Jeremiah 2#Jeremiah 2 . 2|Jeremiah 2:2]]; [[Readable Bible/24 - Jeremiah/Jeremiah 3#Jeremiah 3 . 1|3:1-20]])
- **Ezekiel**: Jerusalem as adulterous wife ([[Readable Bible/26 - Ezekiel/Ezekiel 16#Ezekiel 16 . 1|Ezekiel 16]])
- **Isaiah**: "Your Maker is your husband" ([[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 54#Isaiah 54 . 5|Isaiah 54:5]])

The New Testament extends this typology to **Christ and the Church**:

**[[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 5#Ephesians 5 . 25|Ephesians 5:25-32]]**: "Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her... 'Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and hold fast to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh.' This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to **Christ and the church**."

**[[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 19#Revelation 19 . 7|Revelation 19:7-9]]**: "The marriage of the Lamb has come, and his Bride has made herself ready."

**[[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 21#Revelation 21 . 2|Revelation 21:2]]**: "I saw the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God, **prepared as a bride adorned for her husband**."

Thus, the Song of Solomon ultimately points to **Christ the Bridegroom** who:

- Pursues His bride with passionate love ([[Readable Bible/22 - Song of Solomon/Song of Solomon 2#Song of Solomon 2 . 8|Song of Solomon 2:8-13]])—Christ seeks and saves the lost
- Calls His bride beautiful despite her sin ([[Readable Bible/22 - Song of Solomon/Song of Solomon 4#Song of Solomon 4 . 7|Song of Solomon 4:7]])—"You are altogether beautiful, my love; there is no flaw in you"—Christ sanctifies His bride ([[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 5#Ephesians 5 . 27|Ephesians 5:27]])
- Enjoys exclusive covenant union ([[Readable Bible/22 - Song of Solomon/Song of Solomon 6#Song of Solomon 6 . 3|Song of Solomon 6:3]])—Christ and the Church united eternally

The Song celebrates not only marital love but the **ultimate marriage**—the eschatological union of Christ and His redeemed people.

## Kline's Two-Register Framework

Meredith Kline's intrusion framework illuminates how Wisdom Literature mediates the **tension of the Common Grace period**:

### Life "Under the Sun": The Lower Register

Ecclesiastes' repeated phrase "under the sun" denotes the **lower register**—temporal, earthly existence bounded by death. In Kline's framework, the Common Grace period (Genesis 3 to Revelation 20) is characterized by:

- **Mixture of righteous and wicked**: Both coexist, complicating simple retribution theology (as Job demonstrates)
- **Restraint of final judgment**: God delays the eschaton, allowing time for repentance ([[Readable Bible/61 - 2 Peter/2 Peter 3#2 Peter 3 . 9|2 Peter 3:9]])
- **Vanity and frustration**: Creation groans under the curse ([[Readable Bible/45 - Romans/Romans 8#Romans 8 . 20|Romans 8:20-22]])—Ecclesiastes exposes this

The Wisdom Literature honestly confronts life's frustrations **without minimizing the curse**. Unlike false prophets who cry "Peace, peace" when there is no peace ([[Readable Bible/24 - Jeremiah/Jeremiah 6#Jeremiah 6 . 14|Jeremiah 6:14]]), Ecclesiastes acknowledges vanity, Psalms lament injustice, and Job questions theodicy.

### Eschatological Hope: The Upper Register Intrudes

Yet Wisdom Literature also contains **upper-register intrusions**—glimpses of eschatological resolution:

**Psalms**: Messianic psalms announce the coming King whose reign will be eternal ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 2#Psalm 2 . 1|Psalm 2]]; [[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 110#Psalm 110 . 1|Psalm 110]]). Resurrection hope emerges: "You will not abandon my soul to Sheol" ([[Readable Bible/19 - Psalms/Psalm 16#Psalm 16 . 10|Psalm 16:10]])—fulfilled in Christ ([[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 2#Acts 2 . 27|Acts 2:27-31]]).

**Job**: Despite earthly suffering, Job affirms resurrection: "In my flesh I shall see God" ([[Readable Bible/18 - Job/Job 19#Job 19 . 26|Job 19:26]])—an intrusion of eschatological hope.

**Ecclesiastes**: The final judgment ([[Readable Bible/21 - Ecclesiastes/Ecclesiastes 12#Ecclesiastes 12 . 14|Ecclesiastes 12:14]]) intrudes into the "under the sun" despair, providing ultimate accountability.

**Proverbs**: Wisdom promises life ([[Readable Bible/20 - Proverbs/Proverbs 3#Proverbs 3 . 18|Proverbs 3:18]])—not merely temporal prosperity but eschatological life in Christ ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 14#John 14 . 6|John 14:6]]).

The Wisdom Literature thus holds in **tension** the realities of the Common Grace period (suffering, injustice, death) with **intrusions of eschatological hope** (Messiah, resurrection, judgment, new creation).

## Christological Fulfillment: Christ as Wisdom Incarnate

The New Testament presents Jesus as the **fulfillment of all Wisdom Literature**:

### Jesus the Righteous Sufferer (Job)

Where Job suffered innocently yet remained sinful, Jesus suffers **sinlessly**:

- **Isaiah 53**: "He was oppressed, and he was afflicted... like a lamb that is led to the slaughter" ([[Readable Bible/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 53#Isaiah 53 . 7|Isaiah 53:7]])
- **[[Readable Bible/60 - 1 Peter/1 Peter 2#1 Peter 2 . 22|1 Peter 2:22-24]]**: "He committed no sin... He himself bore our sins in his body on the tree"
- **[[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 4#Hebrews 4 . 15|Hebrews 4:15]]**: "Tempted as we are, yet without sin"

Jesus is the **greater Job** who not only endures suffering but **conquers it** through resurrection, vindicating God's justice and providing redemption.

### Jesus the True Psalmist (Psalms)

Jesus perfectly fulfills the Psalms' portrait of the righteous sufferer and victorious King:

- **Psalm 22**: Jesus cries "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" on the cross ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 27#Matthew 27 . 46|Matthew 27:46]])
- **Psalm 2**: Jesus is the begotten Son installed as King ([[Readable Bible/44 - Acts/Acts 13#Acts 13 . 33|Acts 13:33]])
- **Psalm 110**: Jesus sits at God's right hand as eternal Priest-King ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 1#Hebrews 1 . 13|Hebrews 1:13]]; [[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 7#Hebrews 7 . 17|7:17]])

The **dual nature of the Psalms**—lament and praise, suffering and victory—corresponds to Christ's **humiliation and exaltation** ([[Readable Bible/50 - Philippians/Philippians 2#Philippians 2 . 6|Philippians 2:6-11]]).

### Jesus the Wisdom of God (Proverbs)

Paul explicitly identifies Christ as **God's wisdom**:

"Christ the power of God and the **wisdom of God**" ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 1#1 Corinthians 1 . 24|1 Corinthians 1:24]]).

"Christ Jesus, whom God made our wisdom and our righteousness and sanctification and redemption" ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 1#1 Corinthians 1 . 30|1 Corinthians 1:30]]).

Where Proverbs 8 personifies Wisdom as present at creation, **John 1** identifies Christ as the Logos through whom "all things were made" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 1#John 1 . 3|John 1:3]]). Christ is not merely wise—He **is Wisdom incarnate**, embodying perfect skill in living according to God's design.

### Jesus the Greater Solomon (Ecclesiastes)

Jesus surpasses Solomon:

"Something **greater than Solomon** is here" ([[Readable Bible/40 - Matthew/Matthew 12#Matthew 12 . 42|Matthew 12:42]]).

Where Solomon ended in folly, Jesus remains perfectly wise. Where Ecclesiastes declares "all is vanity," Jesus declares "I am the way, and the truth, and the **life**" ([[Readable Bible/43 - John/John 14#John 14 . 6|John 14:6]]). Where death renders earthly pursuits meaningless, Jesus **conquers death**, providing eternal meaning ([[Readable Bible/46 - 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 15#1 Corinthians 15 . 54|1 Corinthians 15:54-57]]).

### Jesus the Bridegroom (Song of Solomon)

Jesus is the **true Bridegroom** who loves His bride, the Church:

"Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her, that he might sanctify her... that he might present the church to himself in splendor, without spot or wrinkle" ([[Readable Bible/49 - Ephesians/Ephesians 5#Ephesians 5 . 25|Ephesians 5:25-27]]).

The **marriage supper of the Lamb** ([[Readable Bible/66 - Revelation/Revelation 19#Revelation 19 . 7|Revelation 19:7-9]]) consummates the redemptive-historical romance, fulfilling Song of Solomon's covenant love typology.

---

## Conclusion: Wisdom Points to the Wise One

The Wisdom Literature represents a unique intrusion of grace—teaching God's people to navigate life under the curse with skill, reverence, and hope. Yet the intrusion **fails** to bring final resolution. Job suffers without full explanation. The Psalmists cry out for deliverance. Solomon's wisdom cannot prevent his fall into idolatry. Ecclesiastes exposes life's vanity under the sun. The marital love in Song of Solomon points beyond itself to an ultimate union.

The fivefold pattern unfolds: **Chaos** (life under the curse, suffering, injustice, death) → **Creation** (wisdom granted to Job, David, Solomon) → **Commission** (fear the LORD, live skillfully, worship rightly) → **Sin** (even the wise fail and die) → **Judgment** (all is vanity, death reigns, meaning eludes apart from God).

Yet through the failure emerges **Christological hope**. The Wisdom Literature points forward to:

- The **Righteous Sufferer** who bears sin without fault
- The **True King** who reigns forever
- The **Wisdom of God** incarnate
- The **Greater Solomon** who conquers death and gives life meaning
- The **Bridegroom** who loves His bride and unites with her eternally

What the Wisdom Literature **announces**, Christ **accomplishes**. What it **seeks**, Christ **is**. What it **longs for**, Christ **provides**.

In Christ, Job's question is answered—not with explanation but with redemption. The Psalms' laments turn to praise. Proverbs' wisdom becomes embodied. Ecclesiastes' vanity is overcome by resurrection. Song of Solomon's love finds its ultimate fulfillment.

The Wisdom Literature trains us to live skillfully in a fallen world while pointing us to the One who is Wisdom itself, leading us from life "under the sun" to eternal life in the presence of God, where all vanity ceases and perfect wisdom reigns forever.
