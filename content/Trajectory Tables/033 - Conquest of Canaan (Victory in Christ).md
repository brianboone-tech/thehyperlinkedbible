## CONQUEST OF CANAAN TRAJECTORY TABLE

<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/ybm-8sb7PP4?si=0erbLmT3fBs5idDE" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allowfullscreen></iframe>

▶️ [Watch on YouTube](https://youtu.be/ybm-8sb7PP4)

Israel's conquest of Canaan under Joshua's leadership—beginning with Jericho's miraculous fall through divine intervention rather than military strategy (Joshua 6)—established the pattern of God fighting for His people to give them rest in the promised land. Yet the conquest remained incomplete due to Israel's compromise with remaining Canaanites (Judges 1:27-36), leading to ongoing cycles of oppression and partial victories that revealed the need for a greater Conqueror. The prophets anticipated this ultimate victory: Psalm 110:1 envisions the Messiah seated at God's right hand "until I make your enemies your footstool," while Isaiah 9:6-7 proclaims the Mighty God whose government will have no end. This trajectory finds its fulfillment in Christ, who through His death and resurrection "disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them" (Colossians 2:15), conquering not merely earthly foes but the cosmic enemies of sin, Satan, and death itself, giving believers participation in His victory as "more than conquerors" (Romans 8:37).

**Type Classification**: Providential Type

## Canonical Intertextuality Pairs

### OT to OT

**06 - Joshua**

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/06 - Joshua/Joshua 6.17 to Deuteronomy 7.2\|Joshua 6.17 to Deuteronomy 7.2]] - **CRITICAL:** This pair demonstrates direct verbal connection through the term חֵרֶם (cherem - devoted to destruction). Deuteronomy 7:2 commands Israel to "devote them to complete destruction" (חַרֵם תַּחֲרִימֵם) when conquering Canaan's nations. Joshua 6:17 applies this command at Jericho: "the city and all that is within it shall be devoted to the LORD for destruction." This shows canonical development from Deuteronomy's command to Joshua's execution, establishing the theological principle that conquest involves total judgment on sin. The cherem concept pictures God's holy war against wickedness, foundational to understanding conquest typology.

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/06 - Joshua/Joshua 10.10 to Isaiah 28.21\|Joshua 10.10 to Isaiah 28.21]] - This pair shows remarkable theological development through terminological connection. Joshua 10:10 describes YHWH throwing the Amorites into confusion at Gibeon, giving Israel victory. Isaiah 28:21 prophesies that YHWH "will rise up as on Mount Perazim; as in the Valley of Gibeon he will be roused; to do his deed—strange is his deed! and to work his work—alien is his work!" Isaiah reinterprets Joshua's victory site as a place where God will now fight AGAINST Israel in judgment. This shows the "strange work" of divine warfare turned against a disobedient people, demonstrating that the same God who conquered FOR Israel will conquer AGAINST them if they become like the Canaanites.

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/06 - Joshua/Joshua 11.20 to Deuteronomy 7.2\|Joshua 11.20 to Deuteronomy 7.2]] - **CRITICAL:** Joshua 11:20 states "it was the LORD's doing to harden their hearts that they should come against Israel in battle, in order that they should be devoted to destruction (חַרֵם)." This connects directly to Deuteronomy 7:2's command to "devote them to complete destruction." The pair develops divine sovereignty theology: God both commands cherem and hardens Canaanite hearts to ensure the judgment occurs. This parallels Exodus 4:21's hardening of Pharaoh for judgment purposes, showing a consistent pattern of God's sovereign control over enemies destined for destruction.

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/06 - Joshua/Joshua 11.20 to Exodus 4.21\|Joshua 11.20 to Exodus 4.21]] - Both texts share the theme of divine hardening for judgment purposes. Exodus 4:21 records God hardening Pharaoh's heart before the plagues and Exodus conquest. Joshua 11:20 describes God hardening Canaanite hearts before their destruction. This demonstrates redemptive-historical parallel: God sovereignly controls enemies to accomplish His salvation purposes (delivering Israel from Egypt; settling Israel in Canaan). The connection to conquest is strong—divine hardening ensures judgment falls and victory comes, establishing that conquest is ultimately God's work.

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/06 - Joshua/Joshua 13.2 to Judges 1.18\|Joshua 13.2 to Judges 1.18]] - Joshua 13:2 lists "all the regions of the Philistines" among land yet to be conquered. Judges 1:18 records partial conquest: "Judah took Gaza with its territory, and Ashkelon with its territory, and Ekron with its territory"—all Philistine cities. This pair tracks the historical progression from Joshua's summary of unconquered territory to Judges' account of partial fulfillment. The connection directly addresses incomplete conquest, showing that even victories mentioned in Judges were temporary (Philistines remained Israel's enemies throughout the monarchical period).

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/06 - Joshua/Joshua 13.2-3 to Judges 1.18-19\|Joshua 13.2-3 to Judges 1.18-19]] - Joshua 13:2-3 lists the Philistine territories and rulers remaining to be conquered. Judges 1:18-19 narrates Judah's conquest of Philistine cities but adds the crucial failure: "the LORD was with Judah, and he took possession of the hill country, but he could not drive out the inhabitants of the plains because they had chariots of iron." This pair demonstrates incomplete conquest despite divine presence, showing that lack of faith (fearing iron chariots despite God's power) prevented full victory. Central to conquest theology: God gives victory, but His people must trust and obey fully.

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/06 - Joshua/Joshua 17.12 to Judges 1.27\|Joshua 17.12 to Judges 1.27]] - Joshua 17:12 states "the people of Manasseh could not take possession of those cities, but the Canaanites persisted in dwelling in that land." Judges 1:27 repeats: "Manasseh did not drive out the inhabitants of Beth-shean... but the Canaanites persisted in dwelling in that land." This pair emphasizes failed conquest through repeated testimony. The Hebrew יָרַשׁ (yarash - to dispossess) appears in negated form, highlighting failure to possess the inheritance. This directly addresses incomplete conquest—a central warning within the trajectory that compromise prevents full rest.

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/06 - Joshua/Joshua 17.12-13 to Judges 1.27-28\|Joshua 17.12-13 to Judges 1.27-28]] - Both passages describe Manasseh's failure to drive out Canaanites, settling instead for putting them to forced labor when strong enough. Joshua 17:13 adds "they did not utterly drive them out," and Judges 1:28 repeats "they did not utterly drive them out." This pair develops incomplete conquest theology through the compromise pattern: partial victory (forced labor) instead of complete obedience (total removal). This became a snare to Israel (intermarriage, idolatry), demonstrating that partial obedience is disobedience.

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/06 - Joshua/Joshua 24.28-31 to Judges 2.6-9\|Joshua 24.28-31 to Judges 2.6-9]] - These parallel passages describe Joshua's dismissal of Israel to their inheritances and his subsequent death. This highlights that conquest victory was maintained during faithful leadership but would be compromised afterward (Judges 2:10ff shows apostasy). The pair demonstrates that sustained victory requires sustained faithfulness.

## Four-Step Application

### 1. What You Must Do

You must "put on the whole armor of God" (Ephesians 6:11) and fight—but fight from Christ's accomplished victory, not toward an uncertain outcome. You must resist the devil (James 4:7) and "put to death" the deeds of the flesh (Colossians 3:5). You must not compromise with remaining sin the way Israel compromised with remaining Canaanites.

### 2. Why You Can't Do It

You keep trying to negotiate with enemies instead of destroying them. Like Israel putting Canaanites to forced labor, you manage sin instead of killing it. You make truces with temptation instead of driving it out. You accommodate idols instead of tearing them down. And your compromises become thorns—leading to cycles of defeat, confession, partial victory, and repeated failure. You cannot sustain the conquest in your own strength.

### 3. How He Did It

Christ entered enemy territory—not Canaan but the domain of sin, death, and Satan. He faced not walls of stone but the gates of hell. And through His death and resurrection, He accomplished complete victory. "He disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them" (Colossians 2:15). He crushed the serpent's head (Genesis 3:15; 1 John 3:8). He swallowed up death in victory (1 Corinthians 15:54). He "destroyed the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil" (Hebrews 2:14). His victory is total, irreversible, eternal.

### 4. How Through Him You Can

Because Christ has already won, you fight differently. You are "more than conquerors through him who loved us" (Romans 8:37)—not through your effort but through His love. You put on His armor: His truth, His righteousness, His gospel, His faith, His salvation, His Word, His Spirit (Ephesians 6:14-18). You stand in His victory. And here is the promise to those who conquer: authority over nations (Revelation 2:26-27), the morning star (Revelation 2:28), white garments (Revelation 3:5), a pillar in God's temple (Revelation 3:12), a seat on His throne (Revelation 3:21). The conquest Israel never completed, Christ finished. The rest Joshua never fully secured, Christ guarantees. The enemies Israel left remaining, Christ utterly destroyed. Fight from that victory. The walls have already fallen.

## Lexicon Findings

The Conquest of Canaan trajectory reveals a precise lexical network linking OT military victories to NT spiritual warfare. At the center stands **חֵרֶם** (*cherem*, H2764)—"devoted to destruction"—the sacred ban applied at Jericho (Joshua 6:17) fulfilling Deuteronomy's command. This vocabulary of total consecration to divine judgment threads through the conquest narratives, demanding complete elimination of enemies. The possession language uses **יָרַשׁ** (*yarash*, H3423)—"to dispossess, inherit"—appearing negatively in Judges 1:27-28 when Israel failed to drive out inhabitants. Victory's goal was **נוּחַ** (*nuach*, H5117)—"rest"—achieved when "the land had rest from war" (Joshua 11:23). The **אֹיֵב** (*oyeb*, H341)—"enemy"—becomes liturgical formula in Psalm 110:1's **הֲדֹם** (*hadom*, H1916)—"footstool"—metaphor for subjugation.

The LXX rendered *oyeb* as **ἐχθρός** (*echthros*, G2190), linking Hebrew enmity language to Greek NT. Paul's "triumphing" vocabulary employs **θριαμβεύω** (*thriambeuō*, G2358)—the Roman triumph procession—in Colossians 2:15. John's Revelation repeatedly uses **νικάω** (*nikaō*, G3528)—"to conquer"—derived from *nikē* (victory), promising rewards "to the one who conquers." The ultimate enemy, **θάνατος** (*thanatos*, G2288)—"death"—swallowed in Christ's resurrection victory (1 Corinthians 15:54). NT conquest language escalates from physical **ὑπακούω** (*hypakouō*, G5219)—"obedience"—to cosmic **ὑποκάτω** (*hypokatō*, G5270)—"under/beneath"—describing all things subjected under Christ's feet. The trajectory traces *cherem's* devoted destruction through *yarash's* failed inheritance to *nikaō's* victorious conquering, culminating in eschatological elimination of *thanatos* itself.
