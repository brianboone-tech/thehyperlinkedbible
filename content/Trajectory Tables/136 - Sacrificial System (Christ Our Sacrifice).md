## SACRIFICIAL SYSTEM (CHRIST OUR SACRIFICE) TRAJECTORY TABLE

The Levitical sacrificial system, with its elaborate prescriptions for burnt offerings, sin offerings, and peace offerings, served as a comprehensive shadow of Christ's redemptive work. What required continual repetition under the old covenant—the daily morning and evening sacrifices, the weekly, monthly, and annual offerings—finds its complete and final fulfillment in the singular sacrifice of Christ. As Mather explains, these sacrifices were 'full of Light, full of Spirit, full of Gospel Marrow and Mystery,' each ceremony pointing to specific aspects of Christ's person and work: the unblemished male foreshadowing His perfection, the blood sprinkled on the altar representing His satisfaction of divine justice, the burning depicting God's wrath against sin fully borne, and the ashes carefully preserved signifying the preciousness of His death. The trajectory moves from the insufficient but instructive shadows of the law to the sufficient and final substance in Christ, from types that could only cover sin to the antitype who removes it forever.

**Type Classification**: **Direct Type** (divinely instituted sacrificial system commanded in [[Readable Bible/03 - Leviticus/Leviticus 1#Leviticus 1 . 1|Leviticus 1-7]]) and **Forward-Looking** ([[Readable Bible/58 - Hebrews/Hebrews 10#Hebrews 10 . 1|Hebrews 10:1-14]]).

## Four-Step Application

### 1. What You Must Do

You must receive Christ's sacrifice as the complete payment for your sin. You must stop trying to add to it through religious performance or self-improvement. You must come to the cross not with offerings in your hands but with empty hands extended to receive what you cannot provide for yourself.

### 2. Why You Can't Do It

You keep bringing your own sacrifices. When you fail, you try harder. When you sin, you promise to do better. Your instinct is always to pay, to compensate, to balance the moral scales through effort. Even when you understand grace intellectually, you live as though you must supplement Christ's work with your own. You stand when you should sit. You work when you should rest. Your self-atonement projects run so deep you barely notice them—the guilt that won't let go, the compulsive self-improvement, the sense that you haven't done enough.

### 3. How He Did It

Christ became the guilt offering (Isaiah 53:10). He didn't just die; His soul was "made an offering for guilt"—the specific sacrifice that required restitution plus twenty percent. He didn't just restore what we stole from God; He added infinite surplus. The Lamb of God didn't just cover sin (like animal sacrifices); He takes it away (John 1:29). "By one offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified" (Hebrews 10:14). Perfect. Forever. Complete. The priests stood because their work was never done. Christ sat down because His work was finished.

### 4. How Through Him You Can

Now you can stop standing and sit down. The work is done. You don't need to add your sacrifices to His—it would be an insult to His sufficient blood. You don't need to carry guilt that's already been borne. You don't need to keep dying for sins that have already been paid for. "Present your bodies a living sacrifice" (Romans 12:1)—not to atone, but to thank. Your sacrifices now are praise, generosity, and good works (Hebrews 13:15-16)—not payment for sin but overflow of gratitude. The Lamb was slain, and you are forever covered by His blood. Rest in the finished work. The altar fire is out because the final sacrifice has been offered.

## Lexicon Findings

The sacrificial trajectory demonstrates profound lexical continuity from Hebrew through LXX to Greek. The Hebrew olah (H5930) denotes the burnt offering ascending in smoke, etymologically linked to "going up"—perfectly rendering Christ's ascension after sacrifice. The guilt offering asham (H817) in Isaiah 53:10 explicitly typifies Christ's substitutionary work, requiring restitution plus surplus. Chattath (H2403) designates the sin offering, addressing both offense and penalty. Zebach (H2077) encompasses general sacrifice vocabulary. The fragrant offering language—reach nihoach—appears throughout Leviticus, finding NT fulfillment in Ephesians 5:2's fragrant aroma terminology.

The NT employs thusia (G2378) for sacrifice generally, while hilasterion (G2435) specifically denotes propitiation—the mercy seat sprinkled with blood. The Lamb vocabulary progresses from Hebrew kebes (H3532) to Greek amnos (G286) in John 1:29 and arnion (G721) in Revelation. Blood terminology—Hebrew dam (H1818) to Greek haima (G129)—threads through every stage, culminating in 1 Peter 1:18-19's precious blood. The daily tamid (H8548) sacrifice contrasts sharply with Hebrews' ephapax—once for all. This lexical continuity reveals divine intentionality: each Hebrew term finds precise Greek equivalents, demonstrating that the NT authors understood themselves as interpreting, not inventing, the sacrificial typology embedded in Israel's worship system from its Mosaic inception.
