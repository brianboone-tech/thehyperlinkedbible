The scapegoat (Hebrew: עֲזָאזֵל, azazel) represents a distinct aspect of Christ's atonement: not merely the payment of sin's penalty (propitiation), but the complete removal of sin from God's sight. While the slain goat addressed sin's guilt through blood sprinkled on the mercy seat, the live goat addressed sin's burden through transfer and removal. The high priest confessed Israel's sins over its head, laying hands upon it (imputation), then sent it away "into a solitary place" (Lev 16:22). This powerful visual depicted sins carried so far they could never return. The trajectory traces this removal theme from Leviticus through prophetic development to Christ's complete accomplishment—"as far as the east is from the west" (Ps 103:12).

## Four-Step Application

### 1. What You Must Do

You need your sins not just forgiven but removed—carried so far from you that they cannot return, cast so deep they cannot resurface, forgotten so completely by God that they will never be charged to your account.

### 2. Why You Can't Do It

Every attempt at self-forgiveness fails eventually. You confess, and the guilt returns. You rationalize, and the shame persists. You "release it to the universe," and it comes back at 2 AM. You cannot be your own scapegoat. You cannot carry your own sins into permanent oblivion. The distance from you to divine forgetfulness is infinite, and you have no way to traverse it.

### 3. How He Did It

Christ is the true scapegoat. The LORD laid on Him the iniquity of us all (Isaiah 53:6). He was made sin for us (2 Corinthians 5:21). He bore our sins in His body on the tree (1 Peter 2:24). But unlike a goat that merely symbolized removal, Christ actually accomplished it. He carried sins not just into the wilderness but into the infinite depths of divine forgetfulness. And He did it once—not annually, not repeatedly, but once for all.

### 4. How Through Him You Can

"There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1). Not because your sins weren't real or serious, but because they've been carried away by One strong enough to bear them into oblivion. When guilt resurfaces—and it will, because your memory isn't God's memory—you can say: "Christ has taken that sin farther than my guilt can reach. God has chosen not to remember it. My accusing conscience is not a more reliable judge than God's declared forgiveness." Rest. The scapegoat has finished its journey. The wilderness has swallowed what once weighed you down.

## Lexicon Findings

The scapegoat trajectory exhibits remarkable lexical continuity across testaments. The foundational Hebrew verb **נָשָׂא** (*nasa*, H5375) dominates the OT, meaning "to lift, bear, carry, take away"—specifically used for bearing iniquity. In Leviticus 16:22, the goat "shall bear (*nasa*) upon him all their iniquities (*avon*, H5771) into a solitary place." Isaiah 53 employs this same verb: the Servant "bore (*nasa*) the sin of many" (53:12), creating a clear typological bridge. The LXX translates *nasa* with Greek **ἀναφέρω** (*anaphero*, G399), meaning "to bear up, carry, offer"—used in cultic contexts for lifting sacrifices to the altar. This sets the stage for NT fulfillment. John 1:29 introduces **αἴρω** (*airo*, G142), meaning "to lift up and take away," explicitly linked to H5375 by lexicographers as a Hebraism for expiating sin. Hebrews 9:28 employs *anaphero* ("Christ was offered to bear sins"), directly echoing Isaiah 53's *nasa*. Peter likewise uses *anaphero* (1 Pet 2:24): "He bore our sins in His body." The lexical thread runs unbroken: Hebrew *nasa* → LXX *anaphero* → NT *airo/anaphero*—one vocabulary of substitutionary sin-bearing from Leviticus to Calvary.

**Key Lexical Threads:**
- **Hebrew**: נָשָׂא (*nasa*) - appears in Lev 16:22, Isa 53:4,12, Ps 103:12
- **LXX**: ἀναφέρω (*anaphero*) - standard translation for cultic sin-bearing
- **NT**: αἴρω (*airo*) / ἀναφέρω (*anaphero*) - NT continuation emphasizing removal and offering
