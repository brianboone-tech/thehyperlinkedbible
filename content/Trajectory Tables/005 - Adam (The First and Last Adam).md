## ADAM (THE FIRST AND LAST ADAM) TRAJECTORY TABLE


<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/8AuBFvMd7VI?si=Cuw26-RAlp8-y2KS" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allowfullscreen></iframe>

Adam stands as the federal head and representative of the human race in creation, bearing God's image and receiving dominion over the earth. His fall brought sin and death to all his posterity, demonstrating the principle of representation whereby one man's act determines the destiny of many. Christ, as the 'last Adam' (1 Corinthians 15:45), reverses this tragic trajectory, becoming the new federal head of a redeemed humanity. Where the first Adam brought condemnation through disobedience, the last Adam brings justification through perfect obedience. Where the first Adam was earthy and passed on death, the last Adam is heavenly and imparts life. The typology reveals that God's plan of redemption operates through representative headship—just as all die in Adam, so all who are in Christ shall be made alive. This is not mere moral example but covenantal solidarity: Adam's one act of disobedience made many sinners; Christ's one act of righteousness makes many righteous (Romans 5:19).

**Type Classification**: **Providential Type** (sovereignly arranged person) and **Backward-Looking** (Adam recognized as type only from NT vantage point).

## Canonical Intertextuality Pairs

### OT to OT


**01 - Genesis**

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/01 - Genesis/Genesis 1.16 to Psalm 8.3-8\|Genesis 1.16 to Psalm 8.3-8]] - Psalm 8 directly meditates on Genesis 1's account of humanity's creation and dominion mandate, with David marveling at the dignity God granted to mortals. The Psalm explicitly references the heavenly lights (v. 3, echoing Genesis 1:16) before asking "What is man?" and answering with humanity's glory and dominion (vv. 5-6, echoing Genesis 1:26-28). This is core Adam typology: reflecting on humanity's created dignity, lost dominion through fall, and prophetically pointing to Christ as the true man who fulfills this calling (per Hebrews 2:5-9).

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/01 - Genesis/Genesis 1.26 to Psalm 8.3-8\|Genesis 1.26 to Psalm 8.3-8]] - **CRITICAL:** Psalm 8 explicitly reflects on Genesis 1:26's teaching about humanity created in God's image and given dominion over creation. David's meditation "What is man that you are mindful of him?" directly engages the mystery of humanity's exalted position established in Genesis 1:26-28. The Psalm develops the themes of image-bearing, glory, honor, and dominion—all central to Adam typology. Hebrews 2:5-9 applies this christologically, showing Christ as the true man who fulfills what Adam failed to accomplish.

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/01 - Genesis/Genesis 1.28 to Psalm 8.3-8\|Genesis 1.28 to Psalm 8.3-8]] - **CRITICAL:** Psalm 8:6-8 directly quotes and expands Genesis 1:28's dominion mandate, listing specific creatures under humanity's feet (sheep, oxen, beasts, birds, fish). This is explicit Adam typology: meditating on the original dominion commission given to Adam, recognizing its present incompleteness (we don't see all things in subjection), and prophetically pointing to its fulfillment in Christ (Hebrews 2:8-9). The connection between original mandate and its eschatological fulfillment is precisely the Adam-Christ trajectory.

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/01 - Genesis/Genesis 3.14 to Isaiah 65.25\|Genesis 3.14 to Isaiah 65.25]] - Isaiah's new creation vision explicitly states "dust shall be the serpent's food" (echoing Genesis 3:14's curse on the serpent), but notably omits other curses—no more labor pains, thorns, or death. This demonstrates prophetic understanding that the serpent-crushing promise of Genesis 3:15 will be fulfilled while creation's curse is removed. The connection develops the protoevangelium theme: Adam's fall brought the curse, but the promised seed (last Adam) will defeat the serpent and restore creation beyond Eden's glory.

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/01 - Genesis/Genesis 3.19 to Ecclesiastes 12.7\|Genesis 3.19 to Ecclesiastes 12.7]] - Ecclesiastes reflects on Genesis 3:19's curse: "dust returns to the earth as it was, and the spirit returns to God who gave it." This develops the death-through-Adam theme central to Romans 5:12-21—Adam's sin brought death, making all his descendants subject to returning to dust. The Preacher's meditation establishes the universal human condition resulting from Adam's fall: mortality, dissolution, the body's return to earth. This is core Adam typology showing death's reign through the first Adam.

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/01 - Genesis/Genesis 3.19 to Ecclesiastes 3.20-21\|Genesis 3.19 to Ecclesiastes 3.20-21]] - Ecclesiastes echoes Genesis 3:19's "you are dust, and to dust you shall return," stating "all are from the dust, and to dust all return." This directly develops the mortality theme introduced by Adam's fall, establishing the universal human condition under the curse. The connection is foundational to Adam typology: the first Adam's disobedience made all his descendants subject to death and decay, a condition only the last Adam can reverse through resurrection.

**19 - Psalms**

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/19 - Psalms/Psalms 8.3-8 to Genesis 1.16\|Psalms 8.3-8 to Genesis 1.16]] - Psalm 8:3 references the heavenly lights God ordained (echoing Genesis 1:16), then asks "What is man?" before answering with humanity's glory and dominion (vv. 4-6, echoing Genesis 1:26-28). - [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/19 - Psalms/Psalms 8.3-8 to Genesis 1.26\|Psalms 8.3-8 to Genesis 1.26]] - Psalm 8's meditation on humanity's dignity directly develops Genesis 1:26's teaching about image-bearing and dominion. The Psalm expands on the mystery: God made humanity "a little lower than the heavenly beings" and crowned them with "glory and honor"—unpacking what it means to be created in God's image. This is central Adam typology: reflecting on original dignity, recognizing present incompleteness (not all things in subjection), prophetically pointing to Christ's fulfillment (Hebrews 2:6-9).

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/19 - Psalms/Psalms 8.3-8 to Genesis 1.28\|Psalms 8.3-8 to Genesis 1.28]] - Psalm 8:6-8 directly expounds Genesis 1:28's dominion mandate, listing creatures under humanity's feet and echoing the commission to have dominion. This is explicit Adam typology: the Psalm reflects on Adam's original calling, the NT recognizes its incompleteness ("we do not yet see everything in subjection," Hebrews 2:8), and identifies Christ as the one who fulfills this mandate. The trajectory from Genesis 1:28 → Psalm 8 → Hebrews 2 → Christ as last Adam is clear and direct.

**23 - Isaiah**

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 65.17 to Genesis 1.1\|Isaiah 65.17 to Genesis 1.1]] - Isaiah's "new heavens and new earth" directly parallels Genesis 1:1's "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth," establishing eschatological hope for creation's restoration. This is central to Adam typology: the first creation where Adam was commissioned (Genesis 1) → fall bringing curse on creation (Genesis 3) → new creation where Christ as last Adam restores and surpasses the original. The trajectory shows redemption as cosmic re-creation.

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 65.17-18 to Genesis 1.1\|Isaiah 65.17-18 to Genesis 1.1]] - Isaiah's new creation vision echoes Genesis 1:1's original creation language, promising joy and delight replacing sorrow and sighing. This develops the Adam-Christ trajectory: original creation's goodness (Genesis 1) → curse through Adam's fall → new creation through Christ as last Adam that surpasses Eden. The connection shows eschatological fulfillment of God's original purpose for creation, accomplished through the last Adam's redemptive work.

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 65.25 to Genesis 3.14\|Isaiah 65.25 to Genesis 3.14]] - Isaiah's new creation vision explicitly retains only the serpent's curse ("dust shall be the serpent's food" echoing Genesis 3:14) while removing other curses—no more death, pain, or labor. This demonstrates prophetic understanding of Genesis 3:15's protoevangelium: the promised seed (Christ as last Adam) will crush the serpent while restoring creation beyond Eden's glory. The selective retention of serpent's curse while removing creation's curse is theologically precise.

- [[Intertextuality Pairs/OT to OT/23 - Isaiah/Isaiah 66.22 to Genesis 1.1\|Isaiah 66.22 to Genesis 1.1]] - Isaiah promises the new heavens and new earth "shall remain before me," echoing Genesis 1:1's original creation but establishing permanence beyond the first creation's vulnerability to curse. This develops Adam typology eschatologically: the first creation through Adam was mutable and fell; the new creation through Christ as last Adam is immutable and eternal. The contrast shows escalation—not mere restoration but glorification.

## Four-Step Application

### 1. What You Must Do

You must transfer from Adam to Christ, from the first head to the last Adam. You must stop trying to save yourself through performance and rest in another's accomplished work.

### 2. Why You Can't Do It

You keep trying to improve on Adam—be more obedient, make better choices, generate your own righteousness. But you can't escape Adam's headship by performing better. You're constituted a sinner regardless of your personal moral achievement. And even your best efforts are tainted by the very corruption you're trying to overcome. You're trying to lift yourself by your own bootstraps, not realizing you're standing in Adam's grave.

### 3. How He Did It

Christ, the last Adam, succeeded where the first Adam failed. Where Adam grasped for equality with God, Christ emptied Himself. Where Adam disobeyed and brought death, Christ obeyed unto death and brought life. Where Adam lost dominion, Christ regained it through resurrection. He didn't merely try harder—He accomplished what Adam could never accomplish: perfect obedience to the Father, victory over the serpent, restoration of the image, reclamation of dominion. His one act of righteousness brings justification and life.

### 4. How Through Him You Can

Through union with the last Adam, His righteousness becomes yours. You are constituted righteous not by accumulating good deeds but by being transferred from one head to another. "As in Adam all die, so in Christ shall all be made alive." Now you're being renewed "after the image of your creator" (Colossians 3:10). The dominion Adam lost is being restored through Christ—"we see Jesus, crowned with glory and honor." You share in His victory over the serpent: "The God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet" (Romans 16:20). You await the new creation where the curse is finally and fully reversed.

## Lexicon Findings

The Adam-Christ typology reveals profound lexical continuity across the biblical canon, establishing federal headship as the controlling framework for understanding humanity's destiny. The Hebrew **אָדָם** (*adam*, H120)—meaning "man" or "mankind," derived from "ruddy" earth—appears in Genesis 1-3 and connects linguistically to **אֲדָמָה** (*adamah*, H127), "ground" or "earth." This wordplay establishes humanity's earthy origin and mortality after the fall (Genesis 3:19). Genesis 1:26-27 introduces **צֶלֶם** (*tselem*, H6754, "image") and **דְּמוּת** (*demuth*, H1823, "likeness"), establishing mankind's divinely-appointed representational dignity. The LXX translates *tselem* with Greek **εἰκών** (*eikōn*, G1504, "image"), which Paul employs in 1 Corinthians 15:49 and Colossians 1:15 to identify Christ as the perfect image-bearer. Paul's "first Adam" / "last Adam" contrast (1 Corinthians 15:45) uses **πρῶτος** (*prōtos*, G4413, "first") versus **ἔσχατος** (*eschatos*, G2078, "last"), signifying chronological and eschatological priority. The critical distinction appears in **ψυχὴν ζῶσαν** (*psychēn zōsan*, "living soul," quoting Genesis 2:7 LXX) versus **πνεῦμα ζῳοποιοῦν** (*pneuma zōopoioun*, G4151 + G2227, "life-giving spirit"), contrasting Adam's natural life with Christ's supernatural, imparting vitality. This lexical trajectory demonstrates that Christ reverses Adam's federal failure through superior representative headship.
